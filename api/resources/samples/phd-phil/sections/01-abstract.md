# Abstract

A great deal of professional advice is unimpeachable by the standards we usually apply to it and yet still fails the person who receives it. A financial adviser discloses every charge, documents every recommendation and stays loyal to the client's interests as a profiling instrument records them, but never actually sees the client in front of him. This dissertation argues that such advice breaches a real duty. I call it the duty of owed attention. Professional advisers, including financial advisers, physicians, lawyers and academic supervisors, owe their clients a disciplined, accurate and just perception of the particular client's situation, ends and vulnerabilities, and they owe it for as long as the relationship lasts.

Drawing on Murdoch, Weil and the ethics of care, I first state the attentive standard (D1), which has three components: receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness. Its opposing vice I call template advice. I then argue for four claims. First, the duty rests on two structures together: the asymmetric dependency that care ethics analyses, and the discretionary power over a client's critical resources (D2) that fiduciary theory analyses. Second, it cannot be reduced to the fiduciary duties of loyalty and care or to informed consent, because each of these presupposes a perception of the client that none of them requires. Third, it is bounded by respect for relational autonomy, and this anti-paternalism constraint (D4) separates attention from benevolent interference. Fourth, attention is partly something institutions achieve, so institutions that build up attention debt wrong advisers and clients alike.

I defend the account against four objections: that it demands too much, that it licenses paternalism, that it ignores the limits of professional roles, and that law cannot enforce a perceptual virtue. Three invented cases test it: a retirement annuity, a frequent attender in general practice, and a struggling doctoral student. The dissertation concludes that attention is the moral precondition of good advice and not an optional extra, and that what an adviser owes the client includes how the adviser looks at the client.

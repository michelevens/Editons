# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 An adviser who followed every rule

Consider a case I will come back to throughout this dissertation. It is invented, but it is meant to be recognisable.[^d1] Ruth is sixty-seven. Her husband died some months ago, and she now holds a pension pot that he had managed and she had never looked at closely. She has been told that she must decide what to do with it, and she approaches a financial adviser, Hale, at a firm her late husband used. Hale is courteous and well qualified, and he is careful about the regulations. He takes her through the firm's fact-find, a structured questionnaire covering income, outgoings, health, dependants and attitude to risk, and she scores as highly risk-averse. He explains the difference between drawdown and a lifetime annuity, discloses the charges and his commission, and records her answers. He then recommends that she use the pot to buy a lifetime annuity, on the grounds that she is risk-averse, has no other substantial pension income and has said she does not want to worry about money. He gives her the required documents and a cooling-off period. Ruth signs.

Some things in the meeting did not fit the questionnaire. Ruth said, more than once, that she did not want to have to think about it, and she said this while visibly grieving, in the first months of a widowhood whose finances she was handling alone for the first time. Hale heard the remark and recorded it, in effect, as a risk preference. Other things never came up at all. Ruth had hoped to help her son buy a home, and she did not mention it because nothing on the questionnaire asked about it and nothing in the conversation invited it. She would have valued the freedom to draw a lump sum, and she had not understood that buying the annuity could not be undone. Some months later the son's plans take shape, and Ruth finds that her capital has been converted, irreversibly, into an income stream she cannot draw on early, and that the flexibility she now wants is gone.

I will assume, for the sake of the example, that Hale broke no rule. His disclosures were complete, his recommendation fell within the range a regulator would accept as suitable for a risk-averse client with her profile, and he had no conflict of interest that the law would recognise. He was not dishonest or negligent in any sense a court would accept, and he did not exploit her. If she complained, the complaint would probably fail. Yet most people who hear the case think that something went wrong, and that what went wrong was in some sense Hale's doing, not simply bad luck. My aim in this dissertation is to say what that something is, and to show that it is the breach of a duty and not just a missed opportunity to do better.

My answer, in brief, is that Hale did not attend to Ruth. He did not let her situation register as it actually was. He took what she said and fitted it to a template the firm's instruments supplied, and the template decided the advice. Her wish not to have to think about it was something she said in the first months of grief, and it called for at least a question about what she feared, but it went into the file as a stated attitude to risk. Her hopes for her son bore directly on whether she should give up access to her capital, and no question was asked that might have brought them out. Her misunderstanding about irreversibility went undetected because the documents had been given and the consent recorded. Hale perceived Ruth in a way that suited his role, and that perception was inaccurate in ways that mattered to her. I call the resulting kind of advice **template advice**, and its opposite, the virtue and the duty this dissertation defends, **owed attention**.

The case is useful precisely because it is so ordinary. Accounts of professional wrongdoing usually focus on fraud, conflicts of interest, incompetence and abuse. Each is serious, and each is already well covered by law and by professional ethics. Hale's failure shows up on none of those maps. A great deal of what goes wrong in professional advice is like his: advice that passes every external check while resting on a picture of the client that is too thin, too convenient or simply false. The general practitioner who sees a frequent attender's tenth visit as one more instance of a familiar problem and not as a new piece of information, the supervisor who reads a doctoral student's missed deadlines as a lack of commitment when they signal a crisis, and the solicitor who hears the client's question but not the fear behind it may all be doing what their roles require on paper. If they fail their clients, they fail them at the level of perception, before any choice of action is made.

One reply to the case should be set aside at once. It might be said that what went wrong is simply the outcome: Ruth's son happened to need help, and had he not done so the annuity might have served Ruth well. On this view Hale was unlucky, or Ruth was, and hindsight is doing the moral work. I do not think this is right. Suppose her son's plans had come to nothing and Ruth had lived contentedly on her annuity. Hale would still have advised her without seeing her. He would have recommended an irreversible commitment to someone whose grief, inexperience and unspoken hopes should have prompted further questions, and he would have done so because his instruments had no place for them. The good outcome would have been fortunate for both of them, but it would not have shown that the advice was well formed, any more than a reckless driver who arrives home safely has driven well. The wrong I am concerned with lies in the relation between adviser and client at the time the advice is given, and it can be fully present when nothing bad happens. Outcomes matter, of course. They help determine what remedy, if any, is due, and they are often the only way a failure of attention comes to light. But they are evidence of the wrong, not its substance. This is one reason the duty of attention is hard to see from the standpoint of law and regulation, which tend to come into play only once harm has occurred and then ask whether a rule was broken. Neither question picks out what Hale did.

## 1.2 The thesis

The central thesis of this dissertation is this:

> Professional advisers (financial advisers, physicians, lawyers, academic supervisors and others whose role is to advise a client on matters of importance to the client) owe their clients a distinctive duty of **attention**: a disciplined, accurate and just perception of the particular client's situation, ends and vulnerabilities, sustained over the relationship.

I defend four subsidiary claims about this duty.

The first concerns its **grounds**. The duty of attention has two grounds that work together. One is the care-ethical structure of *asymmetric dependency*: the client depends on the adviser for something that matters to the client and cannot easily be obtained elsewhere, and the client cannot fully monitor how well the adviser serves her. The other is the fiduciary structure of *discretionary power* over the client's *critical resources*: the adviser's judgement shapes how the client's money, health, legal position or intellectual future is managed. Neither ground alone explains the duty as I characterise it. Asymmetric dependency without discretionary power is found in many relationships, such as those between strangers in an emergency, where we owe attention of a looser and more episodic kind. Discretionary power without dependency is found where a sophisticated principal hires an agent to execute a narrow task, and there attention of the kind I describe would be officious. The duty is fully present only where the two are combined.

The second concerns its **irreducibility**. The duty of attention is not reducible to the fiduciary duties of loyalty and care, or to the requirements of informed consent. This is the claim most likely to meet resistance, since one might think that a loyal and careful adviser who obtains genuine consent has done all that could be asked. I argue that loyalty, care and consent each presuppose a perception of the client: loyalty is loyalty to the client's interests as the adviser understands them, care is care in pursuing ends the adviser takes the client to have, and consent is consent to an option framed by what the adviser has seen to be relevant. None of these duties, as theorised in law or in professional ethics, contains a requirement that the perception be accurate. Hale was loyal to the Ruth of his questionnaire, careful on her behalf and scrupulous about her consent, and he still failed her.

The third concerns its **limit**. The duty of attention is constrained by respect for *relational autonomy*. Attention looks dangerously like paternalism: an adviser who claims to see the client's situation "more truly" than the client does may go on to override the client's choices in the name of that truer vision. I argue that attention is distinguished from paternalism by its object and by its direction. Its object is the client's own ends and the conditions of their agency, not the adviser's view of the client's good. Its direction is toward enlarging the client's capacity to decide, not replacing it.

The fourth concerns its **institutional character**. Attention is partly an institutional achievement. Whether an adviser can attend depends on time, caseload, instruments, incentives and the shape of the encounter, and these are set largely by institutions. Institutions that systematically make attention impossible create what I call **attention debt**, and in doing so they wrong both the clients who receive template advice and the advisers who are made to give it.

## 1.3 Key terms

I use a small set of terms consistently throughout. I introduce them here briefly and define them properly in the chapters indicated.

*Owed attention* is the duty as a whole: the attention an adviser owes a client in virtue of the relationship between them. It is stated formally as (D3) in Chapter 5, once its grounds have been laid out.

*The attentive standard* is the standard of perception the duty requires. It is stated as (D1) in Chapter 2 and has three components. **Receptivity** is letting the client's situation register on its own terms, rather than fitting it at once to a template. **Accuracy** is seeing the situation truly, against both self-serving and role-convenient distortion. This is the component closest to what Murdoch describes as a just and loving way of looking at another person {{murdoch1970}}. **Responsiveness** is letting what has been seen shape the advice and the manner in which it is given. The three are distinct but they come as a package: receptivity without accuracy is credulity, accuracy without receptivity is impossible, and both without responsiveness are idle.

*Critical resources* are the assets, interests or capacities belonging to the client over which the adviser exercises discretion. The phrase is taken from D. Gordon Smith's account of fiduciary duty, on which a fiduciary relationship is one in which a party acting for another exercises discretion over a resource critical to that other {{smith2002}}. I extend it, in (D2) in Chapter 3, beyond property to health, legal standing and intellectual formation.

*Discretionary power* is the power, which every adviser holds to some degree, to shape the client's choices and outcomes through judgements the client cannot fully review.

*Template advice* is the vice opposed to attention: advice whose content is fixed by a pre-given classification of the client rather than by a perception of the particular client. Template advice is not the same as standardised advice. Standards, protocols and checklists are often instruments of good attention. Advice is template advice when the classification takes the place of perception instead of informing it.

*Attention debt* is the deficit of attention that institutions produce when they set conditions under which advisers cannot meet the attentive standard. It is developed in Chapter 7.

Finally, the *anti-paternalism constraint*, stated as (D4) in Chapter 6, is the principle that limits what attention licenses. Its effect is that an adviser's more accurate perception of the client's situation never by itself gives the adviser authority over the client's decision.

## 1.4 Why a duty, and why attention

Two quick objections might stop the project before it starts, and I want to address them here, though fuller replies come later.

The first is that attention is a virtue, or a kind of perceptual skill, and not the sort of thing that can be owed. Duties, on a familiar picture, are duties to act or to refrain from acting, and one cannot be required to perceive what one does not perceive. Chapter 2 develops the reply. In outline, attention as I understand it is not a passive registering of whatever happens to strike one. It is a disciplined activity with voluntary components: asking questions, slowing down, checking one's first reading, noticing and resisting the pull of convenient interpretations, and returning to the client over time. These can be required. Moreover, we already treat some perceptual failures as culpable. A driver who fails to notice a child at a crossing has not merely had bad luck, and a doctor who fails to see an obvious symptom is negligent, not just unfortunate. The duty of attention generalises a structure we already recognise. It holds certain people answerable, in certain relationships, for how they see.[^d2]

The second objection is that, even granting that attention matters, it is already part of what we mean by competent professional practice, so there is no distinctive duty to defend. I agree that good advisers attend and that the professions have long recognised as much in their informal cultures. But the formal theories that structure professional obligation, namely fiduciary law, the bioethical principles of autonomy and beneficence, and the codes of the professions, have no place for it, or else give it only a derivative place as a means to better outcomes. The result is that when institutions are designed to make advice reliable, auditable and scalable, attention is among the first things to go, because nothing in the formal account of the adviser's obligations says that it must stay. Part of the point of this dissertation is to give attention a theoretical home, so that its loss can be recognised as a loss of something owed and not just of something nice.[^d3]

Why *attention* in particular, rather than empathy, understanding, respect or care? Each of these neighbouring notions captures part of what I mean, but each also brings commitments I want to avoid. *Empathy* suggests an affective sharing of the client's feelings, which may be neither necessary nor always desirable in an adviser. A surgeon need not feel the patient's dread in order to see it accurately and respond to it. *Understanding* is too cognitive and too static: it suggests that, once achieved, it is done, whereas attention must be kept up and renewed. *Respect* in the Kantian sense is owed to persons as such, in virtue of a capacity they share. Attention is owed to persons in their particularity, in virtue of the details that make them different. *Care* is the nearest notion, and the care-ethical tradition is one of my two main resources. But care in its full sense includes the whole practice of meeting another's needs, which is more than an adviser owes, and the word now carries institutional connotations, such as social care and care homes, that would distract. Attention names the perceptual core of care, which is the part I argue advisers owe.

## 1.5 Three literatures and the gap between them

The argument draws on three bodies of work that have had surprisingly little to say to one another. Placing the dissertation among them is the best way to show what it adds.

### 1.5.1 The ethics of care

The first is the ethics of care. From its beginnings the care tradition has insisted that moral life is lived in relationships of unequal dependency, and that the moral demands of such relationships are badly captured by theories modelled on contracts between independent equals. Gilligan's challenge to the developmental psychology of her day argued that a moral orientation centred on responsibility and relationship had been misread as an immature stage of a justice orientation, when it was a distinct voice in its own right {{gilligan1982}}. Noddings built an ethics on the caring relation itself, in which the one-caring is receptively engrossed in the reality of the cared-for and the relation is completed only when the cared-for receives the care {{noddings2013}}. Held has presented care as a moral theory in its own right, an alternative to Kantian and utilitarian ethics that takes relations rather than individuals as basic and that reaches beyond the family into social and political institutions {{held2006}}. In *Moral Boundaries*, Tronto analysed care as a practice with phases, the first of which, caring about, has attentiveness as its moral element {{tronto1993}}. Kittay has shown how dependency work makes the dependency worker vulnerable in turn, and has argued that justice requires social support for those who care {{kittay2020}}.

The care tradition has a great deal to say about attention, and Chapter 2 relies on it heavily. But it has said comparatively little about professional advice in particular. Its paradigms are parenting, nursing, teaching young children and caring for disabled dependants, all relationships in which the cared-for's dependency is deep and often lifelong and in which the carer meets needs directly. Advisers differ in several respects. Their clients are usually competent adults. The dependency is real but bounded, since it is dependency on the adviser's judgement in one domain. And the adviser does not meet the client's needs so much as advise the client on how to meet them. One aim of this dissertation is to carry the care tradition's analysis of attention into this setting, and to show that it survives the move.

### 1.5.2 Fiduciary theory

The second literature is the theory of fiduciary law, which in recent decades has become a lively field of philosophical inquiry in its own right {{goldmiller2014}}. In her foundational 1983 article, Frankel argued that fiduciary relationships form a distinct legal category whose common structure is the entrustment of power over the entrustor's property or interests, and that the entrustor's resulting exposure to abuse justifies fiduciary law's special duties and remedies {{frankel1983}}. Her later treatise, *Fiduciary Law*, extends that analysis across the relationships in which property or power is entrusted, physicians and lawyers among them {{frankel2011}}. Smith has offered a unifying theory on which fiduciary duty arises wherever one party exercises discretion over a critical resource belonging to another, and has argued that the duty's core purpose is to combat opportunism {{smith2002}}. Miller has argued that fiduciary duties are explained by the normatively significant features of a relationship in which one party holds discretionary power over the practical interests of another {{miller2011}}.

Fiduciary theory gives an exact account of the structure of the adviser's power, and Chapter 3 depends on it. But its normative focus is almost entirely on preventing the misuse of that power: self-dealing, conflicts and the diversion of the beneficiary's resources to the fiduciary's benefit. The duty of care, the second great fiduciary duty, concerns diligence and competence in exercising discretion. Neither duty, as theorised, addresses the question of whether the fiduciary has correctly perceived the beneficiary's situation and ends. That question is answered implicitly, by assuming that the beneficiary's interests are given, either by the terms of the entrustment or by a general model of what such beneficiaries want. My second claim, that attention is irreducible to loyalty and care, is in part a claim that this assumption hides a duty.

### 1.5.3 Professional ethics and role morality

The third literature is professional ethics, and in particular the debate about role morality. Bayles's systematic treatment compared several models of the professional–client relationship and favoured a fiduciary model in which the professional has superior knowledge but the client keeps significant authority {{bayles1981}}. Fried defended the lawyer's partisan loyalty by analogy with friendship, describing the lawyer as a friend for a limited purpose {{fried1976}}. Wasserstrom criticised lawyers' role-differentiated behaviour as tending toward amorality and the lawyer–client relationship as tending toward paternalism and domination {{wasserstrom1975}}. Applbaum has argued that roles and institutions generally cannot create moral permissions to do what would otherwise be wrong {{applbaum1999}}.

This debate is mostly about what roles permit, that is, whether a professional may do in role what an ordinary person may not. My question is about what roles require, and in particular whether they require a way of seeing. The friendship analogy matters here because it is the most natural way to put attention into the professional relationship: a friend attends to us, and so, it might be said, an adviser who is a friend for a limited purpose attends to us within that purpose. Chapter 4 argues that this gets things the wrong way round. The adviser's attention is owed not because the adviser is a kind of friend but because of the structure of the advisory relationship, and that structure both requires attention and limits it in ways friendship does not.

### 1.5.4 The gap

Put briefly, the care tradition has a rich account of attention but no account of the professional adviser. Fiduciary theory has a rich account of the adviser's power but no account of attention. Professional ethics has asked what roles permit more often than what they require of the professional's perception. The concepts that connect these literatures are those of trust, vulnerability and autonomy, which Chapters 5 and 6 take up. Trust is the attitude that dependency calls for and that attention honours or betrays {{baier1986;jones1996}}. Vulnerability is what turns dependency into a source of obligation {{goodin1985;mrd2014}}. Autonomy, understood relationally, is what attention must respect {{mackenziestoljar2000}}. The dissertation's contribution is to join these into a single account of what advisers owe.

## 1.6 Method

### 1.6.1 Reflective equilibrium

My method is broadly that of reflective equilibrium. I begin with considered judgements about particular cases. The most important is the judgement that Ruth was wronged, and not merely let down, by advice that broke no rule. I then look for principles that explain those judgements and test them against further cases and against our more general theoretical commitments, adjusting the principles where they conflict with firm judgements and revising judgements where a principle is well supported and the judgement turns out, on reflection, to rest on confusion. The aim is not to derive the duty of attention from first principles, but to show that recognising it makes the best overall sense of what we already believe about advisers, dependency and perception.

Two features of this method deserve comment. First, the role of cases. The cases in this dissertation, Ruth's in this chapter and the three in Chapter 7, are invented. They are thought experiments in the philosopher's sense, designed to isolate features of interest and to test whether the proposed principles give the right verdicts. They are not reports of empirical research, and I make no claims about how common template advice is or how often clients are harmed by it.[^d4] Their evidential role is the ordinary one for considered judgements in moral philosophy: if, on reflection, it is clear that something went wrong in a case, an adequate theory must say what. Second, the method allows that some considered judgements will have to give way. In Chapter 6, for instance, I argue that some interventions we might be inclined to praise as attentive are in fact paternalistic, and that our judgement about them should change.

### 1.6.2 Conceptual analysis informed by legal theory

The second methodological strand is conceptual analysis informed by legal theory. Much of the argument of Chapters 3 and 4 consists in examining how fiduciary law and the theory of professional roles define the adviser's obligations, and showing that these definitions presuppose a notion they do not supply. I do not treat legal doctrine as authoritative on moral questions. Law has its own concerns, including administrability, the need for justiciable standards and deference to markets, which may lead it to define duties more narrowly than morality does. But legal theory is a valuable resource for two reasons. It has thought harder than moral philosophy about the structure of relationships of entrusted power, and its gaps are informative, since what law cannot capture tells us something about the shape of the duty that escapes it. The unenforceability objection, answered in Chapter 7, turns on exactly this point.

### 1.6.3 What is not attempted

I do not offer a complete normative theory of professional ethics, nor do I take a stand on the deep questions dividing consequentialists, Kantians and virtue ethicists. The duty of attention is meant to be recognisable from several theoretical standpoints. A consequentialist can value it for the better advice it produces, a Kantian for the respect it shows the client as an end-setter, and a virtue theorist as a trait of the good professional. My own sympathies lie with care ethics, and the argument is framed in its terms, but I try not to make the main claims depend on accepting care ethics as a complete moral theory, which Held, for one, has defended {{held2006}} and which I do not need.[^d5]

## 1.7 Scope

By *professional adviser* I mean someone who occupies a recognised role whose central function is to advise clients on matters important to the client, in a domain where the adviser has expertise the client lacks, and who holds some degree of discretion in how that advice is formed and given. My four paradigms are financial advisers, physicians, especially in their advisory as opposed to procedural role, lawyers in their counselling as opposed to advocacy role, and academic supervisors of doctoral research. These differ in many ways, but they share the structure that matters: a client who depends on the adviser's judgement in a domain that touches a critical resource, and an adviser whose judgement cannot be fully reviewed by the client.

Several adjacent cases fall outside the main argument. Purely transactional advisers, such as a shop assistant recommending a product, lack the sustained relationship and discretionary power that ground the full duty. Friends and family members who give advice owe one another attention, but on different grounds, and I say something about the contrast in Chapter 4. Public officials who advise citizens raise questions about impartiality and scale that I leave aside. Automated advisory systems raise the question of whether anything that is not a person can owe attention at all, and I touch on it only briefly and cautiously at the end of Chapter 8. Finally, the dissertation's examples come from broadly Anglo-American professional settings, and I do not assume that the attentive standard would take the same shape in every culture of advice. That limitation is acknowledged in the conclusion.

## 1.8 Plan of the dissertation

The argument proceeds in seven further chapters.

**Chapter 2**, "Attention as a Moral Achievement", develops the concept of attention. It draws on Murdoch's account of moral vision, Weil's account of attention as a receptive waiting, the care tradition's accounts of engrossment and receptivity, and Tronto's account of attentiveness as the first phase of care. It states the attentive standard as (D1), separates out its three components and describes template advice as the vice opposed to it.

**Chapter 3**, "Fiduciary Duty and Its Limits", turns to the legal theory of fiduciary relationships. It argues that the fiduciary duties of loyalty and care presuppose a perception of the beneficiary's situation and ends without requiring that this perception be accurate, and so presuppose attention without containing it. It introduces Smith's notion of critical resources, extended as (D2), as the bridge between the fiduciary structure of discretionary power and the care-ethical structure of dependency.

**Chapter 4**, "Roles, Friendship and the Adviser", examines whether attention is a matter of the adviser's being a kind of friend. It considers role morality, Fried's lawyer-as-friend and Wasserstrom's critique, and argues for attention without friendship: a duty bounded by role but personal in its object.

**Chapter 5**, "Trust, Vulnerability and Dependency", supplies the second ground of the duty. It draws on accounts of trust, vulnerability and dependency work to argue that the asymmetry of the advisory relationship makes attention owed rather than supererogatory, and it states the full duty of owed attention as (D3).

**Chapter 6**, "Attention without Paternalism", addresses the most serious objection, that a duty to see the client more truly invites the adviser to override the client. Drawing on the literature on paternalism, relational autonomy, principlism and the capabilities approach, it states the anti-paternalism constraint as (D4). Table 6.1 sets paternalistic, template and attentive responses side by side. The chapter also answers the demandingness objection.

**Chapter 7**, "Owed Attention in Practice: Three Cases", tests the account against three invented cases: a retirement annuity, a frequent attender in general practice and a struggling doctoral student. It analyses each in terms of receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness, answers the role-limits and unenforceability objections, and develops the notion of attention debt as an institutional wrong. Table 7.1 summarises the analysis.

**Chapter 8**, "Conclusion", summarises the argument, states its contributions, acknowledges its limits and identifies further work, including a brief and cautious reflection on whether an automated system could be said to owe attention.

Ruth will return in Chapter 7, where the first of the three cases is a fuller version of hers. Before we can say exactly what Hale owed her, though, we need a clearer account of the thing he failed to give. That is the task of Chapter 2.

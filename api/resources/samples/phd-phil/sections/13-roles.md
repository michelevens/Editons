# Chapter 4: Roles, Friendship and the Adviser

## 4.1 Introduction

Chapter 3 ended with a difficulty. If professional advisers owe their clients attention, and if attention is a just and accurate perception of a particular person's situation, ends and vulnerabilities, then the duty seems to belong to a kind of relationship that professional relationships are not. Attention in the sense of (D1) is most familiar from friendship, from family life and from the relations of personal care that the ethics of care takes as its starting point. Professional relationships are, by contrast, defined by roles. The adviser deals with her client as a financial adviser, a physician, a lawyer or a supervisor, and the role fixes, at least in outline, what she is to do, for whom and within what limits. A duty to attend to the client as a particular person might seem either to dissolve the role into a quasi-friendship or to be cancelled by it.

This chapter argues that neither is the case. Owed attention is a duty that is *bounded by role* and *personal in its object*. The role determines the domain within which the adviser must attend and the purposes her attention serves; it does not determine, and could not determine, the particular person to whom attention is owed or what that person's situation turns out to be. The resulting position is distinct from two familiar alternatives in the ethics of the professions. It is distinct from the view, criticised by Richard Wasserstrom, on which the professional role licenses a detached and impersonal stance towards the client and the world. And it is distinct from Charles Fried's view that the professional is best understood as a limited-purpose friend of the client.

The argument proceeds in five stages. Section 4.2 sets out the problem of role morality and its two classic criticisms. Section 4.3 presents Fried's friendship model and Section 4.4 explains why it fails as an account of the advisory relationship. Section 4.5 develops the alternative: attention as a role-bounded but personal duty. Section 4.6 considers objections, including a preliminary statement of the role-limits objection that Chapters 6 and 7 answer in full. Section 4.7 concludes.

## 4.2 Role morality and its critics

### 4.2.1 The problem of role-differentiated morality

Professional ethics has long been occupied with a question that can be stated simply: can occupying a role change what one may permissibly do? The lawyer who cross-examines a truthful witness so as to make her appear unreliable, the official who enforces a policy she believes unjust and the negotiator who conceals his client's real limit all act in ways that would be at least questionable if done by an ordinary person, and each appeals to the role to justify the act. Role morality, in the sense at issue, is the thesis that roles can generate permissions, and perhaps requirements, that differ from those of ordinary morality.

Wasserstrom's article on lawyers as professionals is often credited with inaugurating modern philosophical legal ethics, and it frames the problem in a way that remains useful {{wasserstrom1975}}. Wasserstrom raises two criticisms of lawyers as professionals. The first is that role-differentiated behaviour leads lawyers to act amorally on behalf of their clients: the role supplies a reason to set aside considerations that would otherwise weigh, so that the lawyer does for a client what she would not do for herself and would condemn in another. The second is that the lawyer–client relationship is paternalistic and tends towards the lawyer's domination of the client. Wasserstrom locates the core of both problems in professionalism itself, rather than in the failings of particular practitioners.

The two criticisms pull in opposite directions, and it is instructive to see why. The first is a complaint that the professional is *too much* the client's instrument: she serves the client's ends without asking whether they should be served. The second is a complaint that the professional is *too little* the client's instrument: she substitutes her own judgement, backed by expertise and status, for the client's. A professional ethics that answered only the first might make the professional a gatekeeper and moral supervisor of her clients, which would aggravate the second. One that answered only the second might make the professional a pure technician of the client's will, which would aggravate the first. The difficulty is to find an account of the professional relationship that avoids both.

### 4.2.2 Applbaum and the limits of role permissions

Arthur Applbaum's *Ethics for Adversaries* takes up the first of these problems with unusual thoroughness. Applbaum examines the arguments professionals and officials use to justify acts in their roles that would be wrong if done by anyone else, opening with the case of Sanson, the executioner of Paris who served successive regimes {{applbaum1999}}. He argues that appeals to fair play, to consent and to institutional authority are weaker than they appear, and that roles and institutions generally cannot create moral permissions to do what would otherwise be wrong.

Applbaum's conclusion bears directly on the present argument, though not in the way it may first appear. If roles cannot create permissions to do what would otherwise be wrong, then an adviser cannot appeal to her role to excuse a failure of attention that would otherwise be a wrong. But that is not the use of role I need to defend. The worry raised at the end of Chapter 3 is the reverse: not that the role licenses inattention, but that the role *excludes* the kind of personal attention (D1) describes. Applbaum's argument concerns role-generated permissions; the present question concerns role-generated limits on what may be required.

There is, however, an asymmetry worth noting. Applbaum's scepticism about role permissions leaves open the possibility that roles can generate *obligations*. A person who takes up a role may thereby acquire duties she would not otherwise have, in much the way that a person who makes a promise acquires duties she would not otherwise have. That roles can create obligations is far less contentious than that they can create permissions to do wrong, since it requires nothing more than that voluntarily assuming a position of responsibility for another can bind one to act in that other's interest. I shall rely on this asymmetry below. The adviser's role does not dissolve her ordinary moral obligations, and it can add to them; the question is what it adds and what form the addition takes.[^d1]

### 4.2.3 Bayles's models of the professional–client relationship

Michael Bayles's *Professional Ethics* offers a useful map of the alternatives. Bayles analyses the professional–client relationship through competing models, namely agency, contract, friendship, paternalism and a fiduciary model, and defends the fiduciary model, on which the professional has superior knowledge but the client retains significant authority over decisions {{bayles1981}}. He sets out the obligations professionals owe to clients and to third parties, among them honesty, candour, competence, diligence, loyalty, fairness and discretion.

Bayles's taxonomy can be read as a series of attempts to answer Wasserstrom's two criticisms. The agency model, on which the professional simply carries out the client's instructions, answers the paternalism criticism at the cost of the amorality criticism; the paternalism model does the reverse. The contract model treats the parties as equals bargaining over terms, which misdescribes the asymmetry of knowledge and power that Chapter 3 found at the heart of the advisory relationship. The fiduciary model, which Bayles prefers, holds both concerns in view: it acknowledges the professional's superior knowledge while preserving the client's authority over the decision.

I accept that Bayles's fiduciary model is the best of the five as a framework. But Chapter 3 has argued that fiduciary duty, as the law formulates it, presupposes attention without containing it, and the same holds of Bayles's list of obligations. Honesty, candour, competence, diligence and loyalty can all be satisfied by an adviser whose working picture of her client is formed by template. What the list lacks is precisely an obligation governing how the professional comes to know the person to whom the other obligations are owed. The friendship model is the one among Bayles's alternatives that appears to supply this, and it is to friendship that I now turn.

## 4.3 The lawyer as friend

### 4.3.1 Fried's argument

Fried's "The Lawyer as Friend" asks whether a good lawyer can be a good person, given the duty of partisan loyalty that the lawyer owes her client {{fried1976}}. The difficulty is the one Wasserstrom identified: the lawyer must prefer her client's interests to those of others, including others whose claims may be stronger, and this preference looks like a departure from the impartiality morality ordinarily requires. Fried's answer is by analogy with friendship. A friend may legitimately prefer her friend's interests; we do not think a person morally deficient for helping a friend rather than a stranger, and we would think her deficient if she did not. The lawyer, Fried argues, is a limited-purpose friend who adopts the client's interests as her own within the legal system, and this partiality is justified because it serves the client's autonomy in dealing with the law.

The analogy has two virtues that are relevant here. First, it explains partiality without making it a mere artefact of the role. If friendship is a recognised source of legitimate partiality, and if the lawyer–client relationship shares the relevant features of friendship, then the lawyer's partiality is grounded in something morality already accepts. Second, and more importantly for this dissertation, the analogy makes the professional relationship *personal*. A friend does not serve one's interests as a type; she serves them as one's own. If the lawyer is a friend, even a limited-purpose one, then the lawyer's concern is directed at the client as the particular person she is.

### 4.3.2 Friendship as the home of attention

It is natural to suppose that if anything grounds a duty of attention, it is friendship. A friend who fails to notice that one is unhappy, who responds to one's troubles with the advice she would give anyone, or who sees in one only what is convenient for her to see, fails *as a friend*. Murdoch's account of attention as a just and loving gaze directed upon an individual reality {{murdoch1970}}, which Chapter 2 placed at the centre of the attentive standard, draws its examples from relations of this kind: her case of a mother-in-law who comes to see her daughter-in-law more justly is a case of moral progress within a family. Noddings's account of caring, in which the one-caring is engrossed in the reality of the cared-for {{noddings2013}}, likewise takes the personal relationship as paradigmatic.

If attention belongs paradigmatically to friendship, then Fried's model would seem to offer a ready account of owed attention in professional life. The adviser owes attention because she is, in the limited sense, the client's friend, and friends owe one another attention. The model would also explain why template advice is a vice: to give template advice is to treat a friend as a type, which is a failure of friendship.

I shall argue that this route is closed, and that its closure is instructive. The friendship model fails as an account of the advisory relationship, and in seeing why it fails we learn something about the shape that owed attention must take.

## 4.4 Why advisers are not friends

### 4.4.1 The disanalogies

The friendship analogy has attracted criticism from many directions, and I do not attempt to rehearse it. I focus on four disanalogies that bear directly on attention.

The first is *mutuality*. Friendship is a reciprocal relationship: each party is both friend and befriended, each attends and is attended to. The advisory relationship is constitutively asymmetric. The client depends on the adviser, in the sense (D2) makes precise, and the adviser does not depend on the client in any comparable way. The client does not owe the adviser attention to her situation, ends and vulnerabilities, and an adviser who expected it would be making a mistake about the relationship. Whatever attention is owed in the advisory relationship runs in one direction.

The second is *voluntariness and exit*. Friendship is formed and sustained by mutual choice, and either party may end it. Advisory relationships are entered by choice in a thin sense, since the client chooses an adviser and the adviser accepts a client, but the client's choice is often constrained by need, and the adviser's acceptance is often governed by institutional assignment. The general practitioner does not choose her patients as one chooses friends; the supervisor may be allocated a doctoral student by a department. The adviser who wishes to end the relationship is bound by duties not to abandon a client at a critical moment, which have no analogue in friendship.

The third is *payment*. The adviser is paid, either by the client or by an institution, for advising. Fried's analogy must treat payment as compatible with friendship, which is strained; we do not think that friendship can be bought, and we are suspicious of friendships in which one party is paid. More importantly for present purposes, payment fixes the relationship's *scope*. The adviser is paid to advise about a particular domain, such as finances, health, legal affairs or the progress of a thesis, and not about the client's life as a whole.

The fourth is *scope of concern*. A friend's concern is in principle unbounded. My friend may properly care about my career, my marriage, my health and my taste in music, and her partiality towards me extends across all of them. The adviser's concern is bounded. The financial adviser who takes it upon herself to advise her client about her marriage oversteps, and does so even if she is right. Fried acknowledges this by restricting the friendship to a limited purpose, but the restriction does more work than the analogy can bear. A friendship limited to a single purpose, entered for payment, without mutuality and without the freedom of exit that friendship enjoys, is not a friendship of any kind.[^d2]

### 4.4.2 Why the disanalogies matter for attention

These disanalogies are not merely points against Fried's model as a theory of legal ethics. They bear on attention specifically, and they bear on it in a way that explains Wasserstrom's second criticism.

Consider the fourth disanalogy, scope. If the adviser were a friend, her attention would be properly unbounded: she would attend to her client's life as a whole and let what she saw shape her concern across it. An adviser who attended in this way would be in a position to advise about matters that are not her concern, and her advice, backed by the authority and expertise of her role, would carry weight it should not carry. This is precisely the domination Wasserstrom identified {{wasserstrom1975}}. A model on which advisers attend to their clients as friends do would not cure the paternalism of the professional relationship; it would extend the professional's reach into regions of the client's life where her role gives her no standing.

Consider next the first disanalogy, mutuality. Attention in friendship is moderated by reciprocity: I attend to my friend, she attends to me, and each of us can correct the other's perception. The advisory relationship lacks this corrective. The client is not well placed to correct her adviser's picture of her, partly because she does not know what picture has been formed and partly because she depends on the adviser for the very understanding she would need to correct it. This is the deliberative resource of Chapter 3. Attention in the advisory relationship must therefore be self-correcting to a degree that friendship does not require: the adviser must hold her own picture of the client open to revision without relying on the client to revise it. This is one reason the accuracy component of (D1) is so demanding in professional settings.

The friendship model therefore gets something right and something importantly wrong. It is right that the advisory relationship is personal, in the sense that the adviser's concern is directed at the client as a particular person and not as a type. It is wrong about the source and shape of that personal concern. The concern does not flow from a quasi-friendship; it flows from the structure of dependency and discretion that Chapter 3 described. And its shape is not the unbounded concern of friendship but a concern bounded by the role.

### 4.4.3 Fried's autonomy argument

Fried's model has a further feature that deserves separate attention. He justifies the lawyer's partiality not by friendship alone but by its service to the client's autonomy in dealing with the law {{fried1976}}. This argument is independent of the friendship analogy and can be detached from it. It holds that the professional role exists to enable the client to act as an autonomous agent within a system she could not otherwise manage.

I accept the core of this argument, and it will become important in Chapter 6. But it must be noticed that an appeal to autonomy cannot, on its own, ground attention. An adviser could serve her client's autonomy, in the thin sense of carrying out the client's decisions effectively, while forming the client's decisions by template. Indeed the widow's adviser in Chapter 1 served the widow's autonomy in this sense: she made sure the widow understood that the decision was hers and implemented the decision the widow made. What she did not do was ensure that the decision the widow made was formed on an accurate understanding of her own situation. Serving autonomy, in a sense that would require attention, needs a richer conception of autonomy than the one Fried's argument relies on. Chapter 6 develops such a conception from the literature on relational autonomy {{mackenziestoljar2000}}, which holds that persons are socially embedded and that their capacities for agency are formed through social relationships. On that conception, the adviser's attention is one of the relationships through which the client's capacity for autonomous decision about her critical resources is formed.

## 4.5 Attention without friendship

### 4.5.1 A role-bounded but personal duty

I can now state the positive view. Owed attention is a duty that is role-bounded in its *domain* and personal in its *object*. I shall explain each of these features and then show how they combine.

The duty is role-bounded in its domain. The adviser's role fixes the region of the client's life to which attention is owed: the region in which the client's critical resources, as (D2) defines them, are subject to the adviser's discretion. For the financial adviser this is the client's financial situation and the ends it serves; for the general practitioner it is the patient's health and the life it supports; for the supervisor it is the student's doctoral work and the conditions under which it is done. The domain is not fixed by the narrowest technical description of the role. The financial adviser's domain includes the client's reasons for wanting her money, not merely the money, because the critical resource is the client's capacity to pursue her ends by means of her savings. But the domain is limited: it does not include the client's life as a whole.

The duty is personal in its object. Within the domain, attention is directed at the particular client: her actual situation, her actual ends and her actual vulnerabilities, rather than those of the type to which she belongs. The role determines what the adviser is attending *for*; it does not determine what she will find. Two clients who fall under the same professional category may differ in ways that make the same advice right for one and wrong for the other, and it is the work of attention to notice which differences matter.

The two features combine as follows. The role sets the purpose and limits of attention: the adviser attends to the client *so as to* advise her well about matters within the role's domain. Attention outside that purpose is not owed and may be intrusive. But within that purpose, the duty is to attend to *this* client, and template advice, the substitution of the type for the person, is a failure of the duty, not a permissible economy. Receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness are all relative to the role's domain, but within it they are owed in full.[^d3]

### 4.5.2 How domain bounds attention without making it impersonal

One might worry that the boundary of the role reintroduces the template by the back door. If the role determines which features of the client's situation are relevant, then the adviser need attend only to those features, and the features relevant to a role are just the fields of a template.

This worry conflates two things a role might determine: the *purpose* of attention and the *features* to be attended to. The role determines the purpose. The financial adviser attends to the client in order to advise her about her finances. It does not determine, in advance, which features of the client's situation bear on that purpose. That is something the adviser discovers by attending. The widow's wish to help her grandchild is not a financial fact, and it would not appear among the features a template of financial relevance would list. It is, nevertheless, relevant to the purpose for which the adviser attends, because it bears on what the widow needs her savings to do. An attentive adviser would have found it relevant; a template-bound adviser did not.

The distinction is between a role that bounds the *question* the adviser is answering and a role that fixes the *evidence* she may consider in answering it. Owed attention is bounded in the first way and not in the second. Weil's description of attention as a waiting, receptive suspension of one's own thought, which she connects with the capacity to ask someone what they are going through {{weil1951}}, captures the stance this requires. The adviser who asks a client what she is going through, within the domain of the role, does not know in advance which answer will matter. The role tells her why she is asking; it does not tell her what she will hear.

### 4.5.3 Personal without being intimate

The duty is personal, but it is not intimate. This distinction answers the most natural objection to the view: that attention of the kind (D1) describes requires a closeness that professional relationships cannot and should not have.

Intimacy, as I use the word, involves mutual disclosure, emotional entanglement and a relationship valued for its own sake. Friendship is intimate in this sense; so, often, is family life. Owed attention requires none of these. It requires that the adviser perceive the client accurately and respond to what she perceives, within the domain of the role. That can be done by an adviser who discloses nothing of herself, who remains emotionally composed and who values the relationship chiefly for what it achieves for the client. Indeed, some of the conditions of intimacy are obstacles to accurate perception in the advisory relationship. An adviser emotionally entangled with her client may see in her what she wishes to see, which is a failure of accuracy as surely as the role-convenient distortion that Chapter 2 identified.

The care-ethical literature offers resources for this distinction. Held presents the ethics of care as a moral theory that takes relations of care and interdependence, rather than independent individuals, as basic, and argues that care is both a practice and a value whose influence should extend to social and political institutions beyond the family {{held2006}}. If care's values can shape institutions beyond the family, then the attention that care involves need not be confined to intimate relationships. Tronto's account of care as an activity with distinct phases, the first of which is attentiveness {{tronto1993}}, points the same way: the phases of care are present wherever there is the work of maintaining and repairing a world, including in professional practice. Care ethics, so read, supplies a conception of personal attention that is not tied to intimacy, and this is the conception owed attention requires.[^d4]

### 4.5.4 Answering Wasserstrom

The role-bounded but personal view can now be seen to answer both of Wasserstrom's criticisms, or at least to identify the form an answer must take.

The amorality criticism holds that role-differentiated behaviour leads professionals to act amorally on behalf of their clients. Owed attention does not by itself settle what professionals may do on behalf of clients against third parties, and I do not claim that it resolves the problem of adversarial ethics that Applbaum and Wasserstrom address. But it does bear on one source of amorality: the professional's habit of seeing her client, and the situation, only through the categories of the role. A professional who attends to her client as a particular person, within the role's domain, is less able to treat the client's ends as mere inputs to a technical procedure, because attention makes the client's ends visible as hers, with their reasons and their costs. Attention does not make the professional a moral supervisor of the client's ends; it makes her a perceiver of them.

The paternalism criticism holds that the professional relationship tends towards the professional's domination of the client. The role-bounded view answers this in two ways. First, the role bounds attention, and so bounds the region of the client's life within which the adviser's perception may shape her advice. This prevents attention from becoming the license for the unbounded concern that would make domination possible. Second, attention is directed at the client's own situation and ends, not at the adviser's conception of what is good for her. An adviser who attends accurately to what the client is trying to do is thereby better placed to serve that purpose and less likely to substitute her own. Chapter 6 develops this second point into the anti-paternalism constraint (D4), and argues that attention properly understood is not merely compatible with respect for relational autonomy but required by it.

### 4.5.5 An illustration: the supervisor's domain

The academic supervisor offers a useful test of the view, because her role is less tightly defined than those of the financial adviser or the general practitioner, and the temptation to treat the relationship as a friendship is correspondingly stronger. Supervisors and doctoral students often work together for years; they may share intellectual enthusiasms, meet informally and come to know a good deal about one another's lives. Chapter 7 analyses an invented case of a struggling doctoral student in detail. Here I use the supervisory relationship only to show how the distinction between domain and object operates.

The supervisor's domain is the student's doctoral work and the conditions under which it is done. The critical resources over which she exercises discretion, in the sense of (D2), include the student's time, the direction of the project, the student's standing with the department and, as a deliberative resource, the student's understanding of how well the work is going and what would improve it. Within that domain, owed attention requires the supervisor to see the particular student: to notice, for instance, that a student whose chapters have stalled is not idle but anxious, or that a student who never disagrees in meetings is not persuaded but intimidated. These perceptions are not about matters outside the role. They bear directly on how the work can be advanced, and template supervision, which treats every stalled student as in need of a firmer deadline, misses them.

What the role does not license is the supervisor's extension of her concern across the student's life as a whole. If the student's anxiety has sources outside the doctorate, the attentive supervisor registers this, because it bears on the work, and responds within her role: by adjusting expectations, by pointing the student towards forms of support the supervisor is not placed to give and by not pressing for disclosures the student does not offer. She does not become the student's counsellor, confidante or friend, and the reason is not that she is indifferent to the student's life but that her standing to shape it is limited to the domain her role defines. The example shows that the boundary of the role is not a limit on what the supervisor may notice. It is a limit on what she may take herself to have authority over.

## 4.6 Objections and replies

### 4.6.1 The role-limits objection: advisers are not friends

The first objection is one of the four the dissertation must answer in full, and Chapters 6 and 7 do so. Here I state it and indicate the shape of the reply that the present chapter makes available.

The objection holds that by requiring advisers to attend to their clients as particular persons, the attentive standard collapses the distinction between professional and personal relationships. Advisers are not friends, and it is a mistake to ask them to treat their clients as friends would. The appropriate stance of the professional is impartial and detached: she treats like cases alike, applies her expertise without favour and keeps her personal responses out of her work. Partiality towards particular clients, and the personal engagement attention requires, are threats to the professional's fairness and objectivity.

The reply has three parts. First, owed attention is not partiality in the sense the objection fears. To attend to a client as a particular person is not to favour her over other clients; it is to see her situation as it is, and every client is owed the same. The professional who attends to each of her clients treats them alike in the relevant sense: she gives each the attention to her own situation that each is owed. What she does not do is treat them as identical, which would be a failure of accuracy rather than an expression of fairness.

Second, the detachment the objection recommends is compatible with attention and in some respects required by it. As Section 4.5.3 argued, owed attention is personal without being intimate. The adviser need not, and often should not, engage emotionally with her client in ways that would compromise the accuracy of her perception. Detachment in the sense of emotional composure supports accuracy. What attention rules out is detachment in a different sense: indifference to the client's particular situation, which manifests as template advice.

Third, the objection presupposes that the alternative to attention is impartiality. But the alternative to attention, in practice, is not impartiality; it is the template. An adviser who does not attend to her particular client does not thereby see the client impartially. She sees her through the categories of her role, her firm's products and her own habits, and these categories are not neutral. The widow's adviser did not treat her impartially; she treated her as a type, and the type was shaped by what the adviser's institution was organised to sell. Impartiality, if it is a virtue in the advisory relationship, requires attention as its condition, since one cannot treat like cases alike without first seeing what each case is.[^d5]

### 4.6.2 Objection: attention is intrusive

A second objection holds that attention, even role-bounded, is intrusive. Clients come to advisers for expertise in a particular matter, not to be seen. The patient who consults her general practitioner about a persistent cough does not want her life examined; the client who asks for advice about a pension does not want her grief perceived. A duty of attention may license advisers to probe where they are not welcome.

The reply turns on the responsiveness component of (D1). Attention includes responsiveness not only in the content of advice but in the manner of giving it. An adviser who perceives that her client does not wish a matter explored, and respects that wish, is attending; an adviser who probes regardless is failing to attend to what her client has shown her. Attention is not the same as enquiry. It can be exercised in silence, in listening, in noticing what is not said and choosing not to press it. The intrusive adviser is not too attentive but insufficiently so: she sees in her client a set of facts to be gathered rather than a person with a view about what should be disclosed.

That said, the objection identifies a real risk, and one that the role-bounded view helps to manage. Attention that strays outside the role's domain, or that pursues within it matters the client has signalled she wishes left alone, is a failure of attention. The role's boundary is not merely a limit on what the adviser is required to perceive; it is a limit on what she is entitled to pursue. Weil's account of attention as a waiting, receptive suspension of one's own thought {{weil1951}} is apt here: attention, so understood, waits on the other; it does not extract.

### 4.6.3 Objection: roles are institutional, attention is individual

A third objection draws on a theme that Chapter 7 develops. Roles are defined by institutions: by professional bodies, regulators, employers and the law. If owed attention is bounded by role, then it is bounded by institutional definitions, and those definitions may be drawn so narrowly as to leave no room for attention. A general practitioner whose role is defined by a ten-minute consultation and a target-driven practice has, on this objection, no role-bounded duty of attention beyond what ten minutes allow.

I accept the premise and reject the conclusion. Roles are institutionally defined, and institutions can define them in ways that make attention difficult or impossible. But it does not follow that the duty of attention is whatever the institutional definition permits. The domain of owed attention is fixed, I have argued, by the critical resources over which the adviser exercises discretion, in the sense of (D2), and not by the institution's description of the role. When an institution defines a role so that the adviser cannot attend to the critical resources over which she nevertheless exercises discretion, the institution has produced a gap between what the adviser owes and what she can deliver. This gap is what the dissertation calls *attention debt*. Tronto's argument that democratic societies face a caring deficit, and that the allocation of caring responsibilities should be a central concern of democratic politics {{tronto2013}}, suggests that such debts are not merely failures of individual institutions but features of how societies organise care. Chapter 7 argues that institutions that produce attention debt wrong both advisers and clients, which is clause (iv) of the central thesis.

## 4.7 Conclusion

This chapter has addressed the difficulty with which Chapter 3 ended: that a duty of attention to the particular client seems to belong to personal relationships rather than to professional roles. I set out the problem of role morality through Wasserstrom's two criticisms, that role-differentiated behaviour leads professionals to act amorally and that the professional relationship tends towards domination, and noted Applbaum's argument that roles generally cannot create permissions to do what would otherwise be wrong. I examined Fried's model of the lawyer as limited-purpose friend, which appears to offer a natural home for attention, and argued that it fails: the disanalogies of mutuality, voluntariness, payment and scope show that advisers are not friends, and a model on which they attended as friends do would aggravate rather than cure the paternalism Wasserstrom identified.

In place of the friendship model, I proposed that owed attention is role-bounded in its domain and personal in its object. The role fixes the purpose of attention and the region of the client's life within which it operates, namely the region in which the client's critical resources are subject to the adviser's discretion. Within that region, attention is owed to the particular client and not to her type. The duty is personal without being intimate, and it is compatible with, and indeed a condition of, the impartiality and composure that professional life rightly values. Bayles's fiduciary model of the professional–client relationship provides the right framework, but it needs supplementing by an obligation it does not contain: an obligation governing how the adviser comes to know the person to whom the other obligations are owed.

Two questions remain open. The first is why the asymmetric dependency of the advisory relationship generates a duty of attention that is *owed*, in the strict sense of a directed obligation whose breach wrongs the client, rather than a professional ideal whose realisation is admirable but optional. The friendship model offered one answer to this question, and the answer has been rejected. Chapter 5 offers another, drawing on accounts of trust, vulnerability and dependency work, and states (D3), the definition of owed attention. The second question is whether a duty of attention, however bounded, threatens the client's autonomy. That is the question of Chapter 6.

[^d1]: The asymmetry I rely on is that roles can add obligations more readily than they can subtract them. Applbaum's argument {{applbaum1999}} concerns the subtraction side; I do not claim he takes a view on the addition side. The asymmetry is in any case widely accepted in the ethics of the professions: Bayles's list of professional obligations {{bayles1981}}, for instance, includes duties of competence and diligence that ordinary persons do not owe to strangers.

[^d2]: Fried is, of course, aware that the lawyer is paid and that the relationship is limited, and his restriction of the friendship to a limited purpose is meant to concede both points. My claim is not that Fried overlooked the disanalogies but that, once they are conceded, little of friendship remains to do the justificatory work. What remains is partiality, and partiality can be grounded in the structure of the role, as Chapter 3's account of fiduciary loyalty suggests, without appeal to friendship at all.

[^d3]: This is the place to note a terminological choice. I speak of the "domain" of the role rather than its "scope" because the latter term is used in some discussions of fiduciary law to mean the range of matters over which the fiduciary's duties extend as a matter of law. The domain of owed attention, as I use the term, may be wider than the legal scope of the adviser's fiduciary duties, since it is fixed by the critical resources over which the adviser in fact exercises discretion, including deliberative resources, rather than by legal categories.

[^d4]: Noddings's own position on whether caring in her full sense is possible in professional relationships is complex, and I do not attempt to settle it. Her account of engrossment and motivational displacement {{noddings2013}} is developed principally for personal relationships and for teaching, and the notion of caring as completed only when the cared-for receives and responds to the care fits awkwardly with relationships in which the client may never know what the adviser saw. Owed attention borrows from Noddings the notion of receptive engrossment in the other's reality, but it does not require her full account of caring.

[^d5]: The point that impartiality presupposes accurate perception of particular cases is, I think, familiar from discussions of justice, but its application to professional advice is not often drawn out. The paternalism literature offers a parallel: Dworkin's definition of paternalism as interference with a person's liberty of action justified by reasons referring exclusively to that person's welfare {{dworkin1972}} presupposes that someone has formed a view of what that welfare is. Chapter 6 argues that the quality of that view is where paternalism and attention diverge.

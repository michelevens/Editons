# Chapter 8: Conclusion

This dissertation began with an adviser who followed every rule and still failed his client. The widow advised into an annuity, in the vignette of Chapter 1, was not deceived, defrauded or carelessly served in any sense the existing standards could register. Yet something owed to her had not been given. The aim of the dissertation has been to name that thing, to show that it is owed rather than merely admirable, and to say what follows for advisers and for the institutions in which they work. This final chapter does four things. Section 8.1 restates the argument as a whole, tracing how each chapter contributed to the four parts of the central thesis. Section 8.2 sets out the dissertation's contributions to care ethics, to the philosophy of fiduciary law and to professional ethics. Section 8.3 acknowledges the limits of the account, and Section 8.4 offers a brief and deliberately cautious coda on whether an artificial advisory system could owe attention. Section 8.5 identifies directions for further work.

## 8.1 The argument restated

The central thesis has four parts. Professional advisers owe their clients a distinctive duty of attention, a disciplined, accurate and just perception of the particular client's situation, ends and vulnerabilities, sustained over the relationship. This duty (i) is grounded jointly in the care-ethical structure of asymmetric dependency and the fiduciary structure of discretionary power over the client's critical resources; (ii) is not reducible to the fiduciary duties of loyalty and care or to informed consent; (iii) is constrained by respect for relational autonomy, which distinguishes attention from paternalism; and (iv) is partly an institutional achievement, so that institutions which make attention impossible wrong both advisers and clients.

Chapter 2 supplied the concept. Drawing on Murdoch's account of moral life as going on continuously in how we see, and on her characterisation of attention as a just and loving gaze directed upon an individual reality {{murdoch1970}}, and on Weil's account of attention as a receptive suspension of one's own thought in favour of the reality of another {{weil1951}}, it argued that attention is a moral achievement rather than a mere precondition of moral action. It connected this tradition to care ethics: to Noddings's engrossment {{noddings2013}}, to the orientation toward care and relationship that Gilligan identified as a distinct moral voice {{gilligan1982}}, to Held's presentation of care as both practice and value {{held2006}}, and to Tronto's placing of attentiveness as the first moral element of care in *Moral Boundaries* {{tronto1993}}. From these sources Chapter 2 constructed the attentive standard (D1) and its three components: receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness.

Chapter 3 turned to fiduciary law. Following Frankel's foundational analysis of fiduciary relationships as sharing a structure of entrustment {{frankel1983}}, her later comprehensive treatment of loyalty and care as responses to the entrustor's risk {{frankel2011}}, Smith's critical resource theory {{smith2002}}, Miller's account of fiduciary relationships as relations of discretionary power over another's practical interests {{miller2011}}, and the philosophical agenda set out in the collection edited by Gold and Miller {{goldmiller2014}}, it argued that loyalty and care presuppose attention without containing it. Loyalty directs the fiduciary's judgement toward the beneficiary's interests; care requires that judgement to be competent; neither guarantees that the fiduciary perceives the beneficiary's interests accurately. Chapter 3 introduced critical resources (D2) as the concept linking fiduciary structure to the care-ethical concern with dependency.

Chapter 4 addressed the worry that attention, being personal, belongs to friendship rather than to professional roles. Engaging Applbaum's scepticism about role-generated permissions {{applbaum1999}}, Bayles's models of the professional-client relationship {{bayles1981}}, Fried's account of the lawyer as limited-purpose friend {{fried1976}} and Wasserstrom's critique of role-differentiated professionalism {{wasserstrom1975}}, it argued that the duty of attention is role-bounded but personal: its scope is fixed by the critical resources the role places under the adviser's discretion, but its object is the particular client, not an instance of a type.

Chapter 5 argued that the duty is owed. Drawing on Baier's analysis of trust as accepted vulnerability {{baier1986}}, Jones's account of trust as an affective attitude {{jones1996}}, O'Neill's examination in *A Question of Trust* of how accountability regimes can distort professional practice {{oneill2002trust}}, Goodin's grounding of special responsibilities in vulnerability {{goodin1985}}, the taxonomy of vulnerability proposed by Mackenzie, Rogers and Dodds {{mrd2014}} and Kittay's account of dependency work {{kittay2020}}, it argued that asymmetric dependency over critical resources generates a duty of attention that is not supererogatory. This was stated as owed attention (D3).

Chapter 6 answered the paternalism objection. Starting from Dworkin's definition of paternalism in "Paternalism" {{dworkin1972}} and his procedural account of autonomy in *The Theory and Practice of Autonomy* {{dworkin1988}}, and drawing on the relational conception of autonomy defended by the contributors to Mackenzie and Stoljar's collection {{mackenziestoljar2000}}, O'Neill's critique of thin individual autonomy in *Autonomy and Trust in Bioethics* {{oneill2002bioethics}} and the principlist framework of Beauchamp and Childress {{beauchampchildress2019}}, it formulated the anti-paternalism constraint (D4). It also used the capabilities approach, in Sen's version {{sen1999}} and in both of Nussbaum's statements of it, the earlier in *Women and Human Development* {{nussbaum2000}} and the later in *Creating Capabilities* {{nussbaum2011}}, as a guide to what accuracy should take in: what the client is actually able to do and to be, and not only what she says she prefers. Table 6.1 set paternalistic, template and attentive responses side by side.

Chapter 7 tested the account against three invented cases and developed the concept of attention debt, drawing on Tronto's argument in *Caring Democracy* that the allocation of caring responsibility is a central question of democratic justice {{tronto2013}}. Table 7.1 summarised what template advice missed in each case, what attention required, and what institutional conditions would make attention possible.

Mapped onto the four parts of the thesis, the argument has the following shape. Part (i), the joint grounding, was established by Chapters 3 and 5 together: Chapter 3 identified discretionary power over critical resources as the fiduciary half of the ground, and Chapter 5 identified asymmetric dependency as the care-ethical half, arguing that neither alone suffices. Discretion without dependency describes a mandate between equals, who can monitor and correct each other; dependency without discretion describes a need that no one in particular has undertaken to meet. Where both are present, the duty arises. Part (ii), irreducibility, was argued in principle in Chapter 3 and shown case by case in Chapter 7, where each template adviser met every requirement of loyalty, care and disclosure. Part (iii), the anti-paternalism constraint, was formulated in Chapter 6 and applied in Chapter 7, most testingly in the case of the frequent attender. Part (iv), the institutional claim, was anticipated in Chapter 5's discussion of accountability and developed in Chapter 7 through the concept of attention debt.[^d1]

## 8.2 Contributions

The dissertation makes four contributions, which I state in ascending order of ambition.

### 8.2.1 To care ethics: a theory of attention in non-intimate relations

Care ethics has often been developed from paradigms of intimate care: parent and child, carer and dependant. Its account of attention or engrossment has correspondingly been shaped by relationships of love and close proximity. One persistent question has been how far the approach extends to relations that are structured by roles, contracts and institutions. Held's extension of care to political and global institutions {{held2006}} and Tronto's political argument {{tronto1993;tronto2013}} answer the question at the level of social structure. This dissertation answers it at an intermediate level: the professional relationship, which is neither intimate nor impersonal. Its contribution is to show that attention can be specified for such relationships in a way that keeps the perceptual and moral depth that Murdoch and Weil gave it while bounding it by role. The attentive standard is a care-ethical concept that a professional can be held to without being asked to love her clients.[^d2]

### 8.2.2 To fiduciary theory: the perceptual precondition of loyalty and care

The philosophy of fiduciary law has been preoccupied with the content and justification of loyalty: whether it is a prohibition on conflicts, a positive duty to serve, or a protection against opportunism. Much less has been said about what the fiduciary must know about the beneficiary in order to serve her. The dissertation's second contribution is to identify a perceptual precondition that the leading accounts presuppose. If Smith is right that fiduciary duty responds to discretion over a critical resource {{smith2002}}, and Miller right that it is explained by the normatively significant features of discretionary power over another's practical interests {{miller2011}}, then one such feature is that discretion over a person's interests can be exercised well only by someone who perceives those interests as they are. I have not argued that the law should create a freestanding fiduciary duty of attention, and Section 8.3 returns to the question of enforcement. The claim is that fiduciary theory cannot fully explain the strictness of the duties it describes without acknowledging what they presuppose.

### 8.2.3 To professional ethics: template advice as a named vice

The third contribution is diagnostic. Professional ethics has a well-developed vocabulary for the vices of conflict (self-dealing, disloyalty), of incompetence (negligence, lack of diligence) and of domination (paternalism). It has had less to say about the vice displayed in all three cases of Chapter 7, in which the adviser is loyal, competent and respectful of formal autonomy, and yet does not see the client. Naming *template advice* as a distinct vice, opposite to attention, gives professionals and those who train them a way to describe a failure that otherwise appears only as a vague sense that something was missing. The tripartite structure of (D1) gives the diagnosis precision: a failure of receptivity is different from a failure of accuracy, and both differ from a failure of responsiveness, as the comparative discussion in Section 7.6 showed.

### 8.2.4 To institutional ethics: attention debt

The most ambitious contribution is the concept of attention debt. The usual moral analysis of professional failure targets the individual practitioner. The usual political analysis targets distributions of goods. Attention debt names something between these: a deficit produced when institutions place practitioners in positions that generate a duty of attention while withholding the conditions for meeting it. Its significance is twofold. It explains why a duty that individual advisers owe can also be a standard by which institutions are judged. And it explains why advisers, and not only clients, are wronged by institutions that make attention impossible, which in turn suggests that the demand for attention can be a demand made on behalf of professionals as well as against them.

## 8.3 Limits

### 8.3.1 Cross-cultural scope

The first limit concerns scope. The cases and the literature on which the dissertation draws come almost entirely from Anglo-American professional cultures and from a broadly liberal tradition of thinking about autonomy and professional roles. Both the content of what accuracy should take in and the forms responsiveness may take are likely to vary across cultures. In some settings, the appropriate unit of advice may be a family rather than an individual; in others, the professional relationship may carry expectations of personal involvement that the role-bounded account of Chapter 4 would regard as excessive. The relational conception of autonomy defended in Chapter 6 is better placed than an individualist conception to accommodate such variation, since it already treats persons as socially embedded {{mackenziestoljar2000}}. Nussbaum's defence in *Women and Human Development* of universal capabilities against cultural-relativist objections {{nussbaum2000}} suggests that some core of the attentive standard may travel. But I have not shown that it does, and the claim that the duty of attention is owed by advisers everywhere would require comparative work that this dissertation has not done.

### 8.3.2 Non-professional advising

The second limit concerns the boundary of the professional. The account is built on two structural features: asymmetric dependency and discretionary power over critical resources. These features are found most clearly in professional relationships, but they are not confined to them. A trusted relative who manages an elderly person's affairs, a friend who advises on a medical decision, or a community leader who guides a family through an immigration process may stand in relations with the same structure. The account implies that something like owed attention arises there too, but the role-bounding argument of Chapter 4 relied on the existence of a defined role with a defined scope. Where the role is informal, the boundaries of attention are less clear, and the risk of paternalism correspondingly greater. I have not worked out how (D4) applies in such relations, and I suspect that Kittay's account of dependency work {{kittay2020}}, rather than fiduciary theory, would be the better starting point.

### 8.3.3 Method

The third limit is methodological. Reflective equilibrium can show that a principle coheres with considered judgements about cases; it cannot show that those judgements are correct. The cases in Chapter 7 were constructed to isolate the variable of attention, and readers who do not share the judgement that the template advisers did something wrong will not be persuaded by them. I have tried to strengthen the argument by grounding the duty independently, in the structures of dependency and fiduciary power examined in Chapters 3 and 5, so that the cases confirm rather than carry the conclusion. But the dependence on intuitive judgement remains, and a reader who regards professional duties as exhausted by explicit rules will find the argument question-begging at its first step.[^d3]

### 8.3.4 Enforcement

The fourth limit concerns the unenforceability objection, which Chapter 7 answered only in part. I conceded that law cannot directly require a perceptual virtue and argued that the duty survives as one enforced indirectly through institutional design and professional formation. That reply leaves open how far indirect enforcement can go before it turns attention into another template. O'Neill's warning about audit cultures {{oneill2002trust}} applies with special force here, and the dissertation offers no general account of where the line falls. It is possible that the most that can be done institutionally is negative, removing obstacles to attention, and that its positive cultivation belongs to professional education and to the character of practitioners rather than to regulation.

### 8.3.5 The fallibility of attention

A fifth limit is internal to the concept. Accuracy was defined as seeing the situation truly, but no adviser sees infallibly, and an adviser who attends carefully may still misread a client. The second Dr Amadi of Chapter 7 might have been wrong about the significance of Mr Fenwick's pattern of attendance; the second Dr Okafor might have mistaken a student's withdrawal for shame when it had another cause. Does the duty of attention require success, or only the effort to see well?

My answer, which I have assumed rather than fully defended, is that the attentive standard is a standard of disciplined perception, not of perfect knowledge. An adviser meets it when her perception is receptive, aimed at accuracy and corrected by what the client says and does, and when her response is shaped by that perception. Error that results from such perception is not a breach of the duty, although it may still call for repair. This makes the standard resemble the duty of care in negligence more than a strict guarantee, and it is part of what makes the standard livable. But it leaves a question I have not settled: how to distinguish a reasonable misreading from a failure of accuracy disguised as one. Murdoch's insistence that the self is ingenious in defending its comfortable descriptions {{murdoch1970}} suggests that the distinction will often be hard to draw from the inside, and that the adviser's own sense of having attended is weak evidence that she has. The safeguard, here as elsewhere in the account, is responsiveness to the client: an adviser who checks her reading against what the client says is less likely to mistake her template for the client's reality.[^d4]

## 8.4 Coda: can a system owe attention?

Advisory functions are increasingly performed, or assisted, by automated systems. A brief and cautious word on what the account implies for them is in order, though I make no claims about any particular product, and the question deserves a treatment of its own.

Two questions should be separated. The first is whether an artificial system could *meet* the attentive standard, in the sense of producing advice that is receptive, accurate and responsive to a particular client. The second is whether such a system could *owe* attention, in the sense of being the bearer of a duty.

On the first question, the account suggests a mixed answer. Some aspects of receptivity and accuracy concern what information is taken in and how it is used, and there is no obvious reason in principle why a system could not be designed to register more of a client's situation than a fixed questionnaire does. But the account has also treated attention as a moral achievement, in Murdoch's sense, involving the overcoming of self-serving and role-convenient distortion {{murdoch1970}}. Whether that description has any application to a system that has no ego to overcome is unclear. It may be that a system could avoid some distortions to which human advisers are prone while being subject to others, such as distortions built into the data or objectives on which it was designed, that are harder to detect because no one in particular is responsible for them.

On the second question, the account counsels scepticism. The duty of attention was grounded in relations of asymmetric dependency and discretionary power, and in the trust that clients place in advisers. On Baier's analysis, trust involves vulnerability to another's possible ill will {{baier1986}}; on Jones's, it involves the expectation that the trusted party will be moved by the thought that one is counting on them {{jones1996}}. Neither analysis applies straightforwardly to a system that has no will to be good or ill and cannot be moved by anything. The more natural conclusion is that where a system advises, the duty of attention falls on the persons and institutions who design, deploy and supervise it. If so, the concept of attention debt applies with special force: an institution that replaces attentive human advising with a system that cannot attend, without ensuring that someone does, incurs a debt that no one is positioned to discharge. I put this forward as a conjecture for further work rather than a conclusion of this dissertation.[^d5]

## 8.5 Further work

Four lines of further work follow most directly from the argument.

The first is a fuller treatment of the relation between owed attention and professional education. If attention is partly a matter of character and perceptual habit, as Murdoch's account suggests, then it is formed and not only exercised. How it is formed in professional training, and whether the forms of reflective practice proposed in Section 7.5.3 can foster it, are questions on which philosophical analysis would need to work alongside the study of professional education.

The second is an extension of the account to legal advising, which the dissertation discussed in Chapter 4 but did not analyse through a case. The adversarial structure of legal practice, and the debate between Fried and Wasserstrom about whether the lawyer's partisan role is morally defensible {{fried1976;wasserstrom1975}}, raise distinctive questions about what it is for an adviser to attend to a client whose interests are defined partly by conflict with others.

The third concerns the political theory of attention debt. Chapter 7 conjectured that attention may be unequally distributed, with those of greater means more often able to secure it. If that is so, attention debt is not only a professional matter but a question of distributive justice, and Tronto's proposal that democratic politics should be organised around the allocation of caring responsibility {{tronto2013}} would need to be extended to the professions. That extension, and an account of how professional institutions could be made answerable for the attention they make possible, would complete the argument this dissertation has begun.

A fourth line concerns the client's side of the relation. Noddings holds that caring is completed only when the cared-for receives and responds to the care {{noddings2013}}, and Tronto makes care-receiving a phase of care in its own right {{tronto1993}}. The dissertation has concentrated on what advisers owe, and has said little about whether clients bear any corresponding responsibilities: to disclose what bears on the advice, to say when they have not understood, to resist the comfort of being told what to do. It would be wrong to make the adviser's duty conditional on the client's performance of such responsibilities, since the dependency that grounds the duty is often precisely what makes them hard to discharge; Ruth, in the first case of Chapter 7, did not mention her son because nothing in the process invited her to. But an account of the advisory relationship as a whole, rather than of the adviser's duty within it, would need to say how attention is received as well as given.

## 8.6 Closing

The adviser in the opening vignette did nothing that any existing standard would condemn. This dissertation has argued that he nonetheless failed in a duty he owed: the duty to see his client truly, to let what he saw shape what he advised, and to do so without taking from her the authority to decide. That duty has a name, owed attention; a structure, receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness; a ground, in asymmetric dependency and discretionary power over critical resources; a limit, in respect for relational autonomy; and an institutional condition, without which advisers cannot reliably meet it. None of this asks advisers to be saints or friends. It asks that they see the person in front of them, and that the institutions which employ them make that possible.

# Chapter 7: Owed Attention in Practice: Three Cases

The previous five chapters built the account of owed attention in stages. Chapter 2 set out the attentive standard (D1) and its three components, receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness. Chapter 3 argued that fiduciary loyalty and care presuppose attention without containing it, and introduced critical resources (D2) as the bridge between fiduciary structure and dependency. Chapter 4 showed that the duty is role-bounded but personal, and so does not collapse into friendship. Chapter 5 argued that asymmetric dependency over critical resources makes attention owed rather than supererogatory (D3). Chapter 6 formulated the anti-paternalism constraint (D4) and answered the charge that a duty to attend is a licence to manage clients' lives. This chapter puts the whole account to work. It does so through three invented cases: a financial adviser and a recently widowed client choosing a retirement product; a general practitioner and a patient who attends the surgery often with symptoms that never resolve into a diagnosis; and an academic supervisor and a doctoral student whose work has stalled.

The chapter has three aims. The first is to show that the attentive standard is determinate enough to distinguish good from deficient advising in cases where the familiar standards (loyalty, competence, informed consent) are all met. The second is to test the account against the four objections set out in Chapter 1 and answered in principle in Chapters 4 to 6: demandingness, paternalism, role-limits and unenforceability. The third is to develop the institutional claim that forms the fourth part of the thesis: that attention is partly an institutional achievement, and that institutions which make it impossible produce what I call *attention debt*, a deficit that wrongs advisers as well as clients. Section 7.1 explains the method. Sections 7.2 to 7.4 take the cases in turn. Section 7.5 develops the institutional argument, drawing on Tronto's later work on caring democracy. Section 7.6 brings the cases together in Table 7.1 and draws the comparative lessons, and Section 7.7 hands on to the conclusion.

## 7.1 The use of invented cases

All three cases in this chapter are invented. They are thought experiments, not reports: no client, patient, student or professional described here exists, and no claim is made about how often such situations arise or how actual practitioners behave. Their purpose is philosophical. Within the method of reflective equilibrium adopted in Chapter 1, cases serve as considered judgements against which principles are tested, and the principles in turn discipline and sometimes revise our judgements about cases. A case is useful to the extent that it isolates a variable. Each of the three cases below is constructed so that the adviser satisfies every requirement of the standard accounts examined earlier: there is no conflict of interest, no incompetence in the narrow technical sense, no failure of disclosure. If we nonetheless judge that something has gone wrong, and if the attentive standard identifies what, the case supports the claim made in Chapter 3 that loyalty and care do not contain attention.

Two cautions are in order. First, invented cases can be rigged. A case in which the adviser is cold, the client sympathetic and the outcome disastrous will elicit the judgement that the adviser erred, but it will not tell us which feature of the adviser's conduct carried the error. To guard against this, each case is presented first in a version where the adviser offers *template advice*, the vice opposite to attention identified in Chapter 2, and then in a version where the same adviser, under the same rules and with the same technical knowledge, meets (D1). The contrast is controlled: only the perceptual and responsive quality of the adviser's engagement changes. Secondly, cases from three professions risk suggesting that the account applies uniformly across all advising. It does not. The content of what accuracy must take in, and the forms responsiveness may take, vary with the critical resources at stake and with the institutional setting. The cases are chosen to show that variation as much as the common structure.[^d1]

The cases are also used to test the objections. Each objection is most forceful in one setting: demandingness and unenforceability where advice is commercial and regulated, paternalism where the adviser has authority over the client's body, partiality and role-limits where the relationship is long and personal. I take each objection where it is strongest.

## 7.2 The retirement annuity

### 7.2.1 The case

Ruth is sixty-seven. Her husband, who managed the household's finances throughout their marriage, died eight months ago. She has inherited his defined-contribution pension pot and the family home, which is mortgage-free. She has a modest state pension of her own and some savings. She approaches a financial adviser, Hale, at a firm her late husband used, because she has been told she must decide what to do with the pension. In their first meeting she says that she wants "something safe" and that she "doesn't want to have to think about it". Hale runs the firm's fact-find, a structured questionnaire covering income, outgoings, health, dependants and attitude to risk. Ruth scores as highly risk-averse. The questionnaire records that she is in good health, has one adult son living abroad, and has no debts.

In the first version, Hale recommends a lifetime annuity. The recommendation follows the firm's guidance for clients of her profile; it is defensible on paper, since an annuity provides a guaranteed income for life and removes investment risk. Hale explains the product clearly, discloses the charges and his commission, gives Ruth the required documents and a cooling-off period, and records her signed consent. Every rule has been followed. Some months later it emerges that Ruth's anxiety about money was in large part grief and the unfamiliarity of managing it; that she had hoped to help her son buy a home and had not mentioned it because the questionnaire did not ask; that she would have valued flexibility to draw a lump sum; and that she had not understood that the annuity purchase was irreversible. None of this was concealed. It simply did not register.

In the second version, Hale has the same knowledge and is bound by the same rules. He notices, early in the first meeting, that Ruth's phrase "I don't want to have to think about it" is doing work the questionnaire cannot capture. He asks how long she has been managing money on her own and what decisions she has had to make since her husband's death. He proposes that they not decide at the first meeting. He asks what she would want the money to do, not only what she fears. When she mentions her son in passing, he asks whether she has thought about helping him. He explains, slowly and twice, that the annuity cannot be undone, and checks that the point has landed by asking her to tell him in her own words what she would be giving up. Ruth chooses a mixed arrangement: a smaller annuity securing an income floor, with the remainder held more flexibly. She might, with equal legitimacy, have chosen the full annuity. What matters is that the choice is hers in a sense that the first version's choice was not.

### 7.2.2 Receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness

The first Hale fails each component of (D1). His failure of receptivity is the most visible. Chapter 2 characterised receptivity, following Noddings's account of engrossment, as letting the other's reality register rather than fitting it to a template {{noddings2013}}. The fact-find is not itself the problem; structured questions are a reasonable starting point and are often a safeguard against neglect. The problem is that the first Hale treats the questionnaire's output as the client. Ruth's risk-aversion score is accurate as far as it goes, but what the score records is a disposition expressed in a particular state of grief and unfamiliarity. The template has a slot for "attitude to risk" and none for "recently bereaved, never previously responsible for household finances, unsure what she wants because she has never been asked". What does not fit the template does not register.

His failure of accuracy is subtler. Accuracy, in the sense defended in Chapter 2, is seeing the situation truly, against self-serving or role-convenient distortion. Murdoch's account of moral attention as a just and loving gaze directed upon an individual reality is the model {{murdoch1970}}. The first Hale does not lie to himself in any gross way. But his perception is role-convenient: Ruth presents as a known type, the type has a known product, and the product discharges the firm's obligations efficiently. Murdoch's point is that moral failure often lies less in the moment of choice than in the continuous, habitual quality of our seeing, which can be coarsened by self-interest and by the comfort of ready-made descriptions. Hale's description of Ruth as "a highly risk-averse retiree seeking a secure income" is not false. It is too thin to be accurate, and its thinness is convenient to him. The point bears on Chapter 6's use of the capabilities approach. If accuracy attends to what a person is actually able to do and to be, rather than to a preference ranking elicited at one moment {{sen1999;nussbaum2011}}, then the relevant facts about Ruth include her present capacity to deliberate about money at all, a capacity grief has temporarily narrowed and which, with time and support, she can regain.

His failure of responsiveness follows. Responsiveness, as the third component of (D1), requires that what is seen shapes both the content of the advice and the manner of giving it. Even an adviser who had noticed Ruth's grief might have proceeded identically, treating it as background. The second Hale responds in manner as well as content: he slows the process, defers the decision, and checks understanding in a way that goes beyond handing over documents. Here the relation to informed consent, examined in Chapter 3, is instructive. Both versions of the case satisfy formal disclosure. Only the second secures what disclosure is for. As Chapter 6 argued, drawing on O'Neill's critique of thin conceptions of autonomy in *Autonomy and Trust in Bioethics*, consent procedures can be satisfied while the agent's actual capacity to understand and choose is bypassed {{oneill2002bioethics}}. The first Hale obtains Ruth's signature. The second obtains her decision.

It is worth stressing what the second Hale does not do. He does not decide that Ruth ought to help her son, or that flexibility is better for her than security. He does not withhold the annuity she first asked for. He widens the field of what she considers and ensures she understands the stakes; the choice remains hers. This is the anti-paternalism constraint (D4) at work. In Dworkin's procedural terms in *The Theory and Practice of Autonomy*, the second Hale supports rather than overrides her capacity to reflect on and endorse her first-order preferences {{dworkin1988}}. "I want something safe" is a first-order preference; what the second Hale makes room for is Ruth's reflection on whether safety, in the form she first imagined, is what she wants given everything else she cares about.[^d2]

### 7.2.3 Critical resources and the fiduciary baseline

Why is the first Hale's failure a wrong to Ruth rather than merely a missed opportunity to do better? The answer given in Chapter 5 is that owed attention (D3) arises where an adviser holds discretionary power over a client's critical resources in a relation of asymmetric dependency. All three conditions are clearly met here. Ruth's pension savings are a critical resource in the sense of (D2), drawing on Smith's account of the fiduciary as one who exercises discretion over a resource belonging to the beneficiary {{smith2002}}: they are the means by which she will sustain her life for perhaps two decades, and the decision about them is largely irreversible. Hale's discretion is real even though Ruth signs the forms; his framing of the options, his selection of what to explain and what to leave in the document, and his judgement of when a decision is ready, all shape her choice. And the dependency is asymmetric in the double sense developed in Chapter 5: Ruth lacks the expertise to evaluate the advice, and her bereavement has heightened her situational vulnerability. In the taxonomy of Mackenzie, Rogers and Dodds, her vulnerability is situational rather than inherent, arising from circumstances that a responsive relationship can mitigate or a careless one can deepen {{mrd2014}}.

Frankel's account of fiduciary law explains why the law treats such entrustment strictly: the entrustor's exposure to the fiduciary's power generates the duties of loyalty and care {{frankel2011}}. The first Hale is not disloyal. He has disclosed his commission, and nothing in the case suggests he recommended the annuity for his own gain. Nor is he careless in the technical sense; the annuity is a suitable product for some clients like Ruth. What the case shows is the gap identified in Chapter 3. Loyalty asks whether the adviser's judgement is directed to the client's interests; care asks whether it is competent. Neither asks whether the adviser has perceived the client accurately enough for his judgement to be directed at *her* interests rather than at those of a type she resembles. Miller's account locates fiduciary duties in the normatively significant features of a relationship of discretionary power over another's practical interests {{miller2011}}. My claim is that one such feature, the fact that discretion over a person's interests can only be exercised well by someone who sees those interests, generates a duty that fiduciary doctrine as currently framed leaves implicit.

### 7.2.4 The demandingness and unenforceability objections

The retirement annuity is the natural setting for two of the four objections. The demandingness objection holds that the attentive standard asks too much: advisers have many clients, limited time and firms with targets, and to require each client to be perceived in her particularity is to require a saintliness no profession can staff. The unenforceability objection holds that whatever the moral merits of attention, it cannot be a *duty* in the sense this dissertation claims, since a perceptual virtue cannot be required by law or regulation; one can mandate the fact-find, but not the quality of the gaze.

Take demandingness first. The objection trades on an ambiguity between the attention Murdoch and Weil describe as an ideal of the moral life and the attention owed by an adviser. Weil presents attention as rare and precious, a suspension of one's own thought in favour of the reality of the sufferer {{weil1951}}. If owed attention required that, the objection would succeed. But (D1) is a standard calibrated to the role, as Chapter 4 argued. What the second Hale does is not saintly. He asks three or four questions the template did not, defers a decision by a week, and checks understanding. The marginal cost is perhaps an hour. The relevant comparison is not between template advice and infinite attentiveness but between template advice and advice that is adequate to the stakes. Where the critical resource is a person's lifetime income and the decision is irreversible, an hour of attention is not demanding. The standard scales with the stakes and the vulnerability, which is just what the grounding in Chapter 5 predicts: the more the client depends on the adviser's discretion over something critical, the more attention is owed, and conversely.

That reply leaves a residue. Suppose the firm allots Hale thirty minutes per client and penalises him for deferred decisions. Then the attentive standard does demand more than he can give, but the demand falls on the firm, not on him alone. This is the institutional point developed in Section 7.5. Here it is enough to note that the demandingness objection, pressed hard, turns out to be an objection to institutional arrangements rather than to the standard.

The unenforceability objection is more serious, and I concede part of it. Law cannot directly require that an adviser see accurately, any more than it can require that a judge be wise. But the objection assumes that a duty must be directly enforceable to be a duty, and that assumption is false of much of professional ethics. Bayles's treatment of the obligations professionals owe to clients includes candour, diligence and discretion, none of which can be fully specified in rules and all of which are nonetheless recognised as obligations {{bayles1981}}. Moreover, attention has observable correlates that can be required: that irreversible decisions are not taken at a first meeting; that understanding is checked rather than assumed; that the adviser records not only the client's answers but what the client said she wanted. These are not attention, but they are conditions that make attention more likely and template advice less comfortable. The relation between the duty and its institutional scaffolding is like that between honesty and audit: no audit makes an accountant honest, but some audit regimes make dishonesty harder and others, as O'Neill argues in *A Question of Trust*, distort practice so badly that they undermine the trustworthiness they aim to secure {{oneill2002trust}}. The lesson for attention is that enforcement must be indirect and must avoid turning attention itself into another template.[^d3]

## 7.3 The frequent attender

### 7.3.1 The case

Dr Amadi is a general practitioner in a busy urban practice. One of her patients, Mr Fenwick, is fifty-four and attends the surgery roughly once a month with a changing set of complaints: fatigue, back pain, headaches, poor sleep, palpitations. Each has been investigated to the appropriate standard. Nothing has been found. Mr Fenwick is polite, a little apologetic, and always leaves with a reassurance and, sometimes, a referral or a prescription. The practice's records flag him as a "frequent attender". Colleagues have begun to describe his visits, informally, as a burden.

In the first version, Dr Amadi treats each consultation on its own terms. She takes a focused history of the presenting complaint, examines him, orders tests when indicated and reassures him when results are normal. She is courteous and clinically competent. She has never asked him about his life outside the complaint, because the ten-minute appointment leaves no room, and because the complaints, each taken singly, do not seem to call for it. Over two years Mr Fenwick's attendance does not fall. Eventually a locum, with more time on a quiet afternoon, asks him how things are at home. It emerges that he lost his job some months before the visits began, that he lives alone and that the surgery is one of the few places where he speaks to anyone. He has not mentioned any of this because he was not asked and because he did not think it was what doctors were for.

In the second version, Dr Amadi notices the pattern itself as clinically significant. At the third or fourth visit she says that she has seen him several times recently and wonders whether something more general is going on. She books a double appointment. She asks open questions about work, sleep and company. She does not leap to a psychological explanation, and she continues to take his physical symptoms seriously. But she lets the shape of his attendance register as information, and she responds by offering continuity (seeing him herself rather than whoever is free), by discussing with him what might help, and by leaving open whether he wants to talk about the loss of his job at all.

### 7.3.2 Receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness

The first Dr Amadi's failure is instructive because it is a failure of receptivity produced by a correct procedure. Each complaint is a legitimate presentation and each is handled well. But the template here is not a questionnaire; it is the structure of the consultation itself, organised around the presenting complaint. What the template cannot represent is a pattern across consultations, and still less the possibility that the complaints are the form in which something else is being brought. Noddings's engrossment, as Chapter 2 interpreted it, involves allowing the other's reality to set the terms of one's perception, rather than receiving only what one's role has prepared one to receive {{noddings2013}}. The first Dr Amadi receives what the consultation is designed to receive.

The failure of accuracy has a characteristic distortion: the label "frequent attender", and the informal description of Mr Fenwick as a burden. Labels of this kind are role-convenient in precisely the sense (D1) warns against. They make a person legible as a problem for the practice rather than as a patient whose pattern of attendance is itself something to understand. Murdoch's example of the mother-in-law who comes to see her daughter-in-law more justly is apt here {{murdoch1970}}. The progress she describes involves no new information and no outward act; it consists in replacing a description that served the observer's comfort with one that does justice to the person observed. The second Dr Amadi's shift from "a frequent attender" to "a man who keeps coming back and whom I have not yet understood" is a shift of the same kind. It is, in the sense of Chapter 2, a moral achievement before it is a clinical one.

Responsiveness in this case takes a form that Beauchamp and Childress's discussion of professional-patient relationships would recognise under fidelity and the character of health professionals {{beauchampchildress2019}}. But principlism by itself does not settle what the response should be. Beneficence might license an assertive referral to mental health services; respect for autonomy might counsel waiting until he raises the matter himself. The attentive standard does not replace this balancing, but it changes its inputs. What the second Dr Amadi sees, accurately, is a man who is isolated, who has suffered a loss he does not regard as medical, and who has nonetheless chosen the surgery as a place to come. A response that met beneficence by redirecting him elsewhere might sever the one relation he has; a response that met autonomy by saying nothing would leave him where he was. Responsiveness steers between these by making the relation itself part of the response: continuity of care, more time, and an open door.

### 7.3.3 The paternalism objection

The frequent attender is where the paternalism objection bites hardest. A physician who attends to a patient's life beyond his presenting complaint, it may be said, has exceeded her remit and begun to manage him. Mr Fenwick came about his back. He did not ask to be counselled about unemployment or loneliness, and perhaps he would resent the suggestion that his symptoms are "really" about something else. Attention, on this view, is paternalism wearing a softer face.

Chapter 6 answered this objection in principle. Paternalism, in Dworkin's original analysis in "Paternalism", is interference with a person's liberty of action justified by reasons referring exclusively to that person's welfare {{dworkin1972}}. Attention, as such, does not interfere with liberty at all; it is a way of perceiving. Paternalism enters only if what is perceived is then used to override the client's choices or to substitute the adviser's judgement of his good for his own. The anti-paternalism constraint (D4) forbids exactly that. The case lets us see how the line falls in practice.

Consider three possible versions of Dr Amadi's response. In the first, she concludes that Mr Fenwick's symptoms are psychosomatic, declines further investigation of his back pain and refers him to counselling without asking him. That is paternalistic, and it is also inaccurate: she has replaced one template ("a sequence of physical complaints") with another ("a lonely man somatising"), and in doing so has stopped attending to the physical symptoms he actually reports. In the second, she continues to investigate the symptoms but says nothing of what she has noticed. That is not paternalistic, but it fails responsiveness. In the third, the version given above, she names what she has noticed, asks rather than tells, continues to take his symptoms seriously, and leaves to him what he wishes to do with her observation. That third response is neither template nor paternalism. It is what Table 6.1 in the previous chapter called attentive advice, and the case makes concrete the distinction that table drew in the abstract.

The relational conception of autonomy defended in Chapter 6 explains why the third response respects rather than threatens his autonomy. On the view set out by Mackenzie and Stoljar's contributors, persons' capacities for agency are formed and sustained through social relationships, and can be impaired by isolation and by the loss of the social bases of self-respect {{mackenziestoljar2000}}. Mr Fenwick's loss of work and of company is not merely a fact about his welfare that a paternalist might seize on; it bears on his capacity to identify and pursue his own ends. An adviser who attends to that capacity, and who offers a relation within which it might be exercised, supports his autonomy. Mackenzie, Rogers and Dodds draw a further distinction that sharpens the point: paternalistic responses can themselves generate *pathogenic* vulnerability, compounding the dependency they claim to address {{mrd2014}}. The first of the three responses above risks exactly that. The attentive response is designed to avoid it.[^d4]

### 7.3.4 The patient's own resources

There is a further feature of the case that the template obscures and attention reveals: the patient's own resources. Sen's account of development as the expansion of the real freedoms people enjoy treats persons not merely as recipients of goods but as agents whose capabilities to lead lives they have reason to value are both ends and means {{sen1999}}. Nussbaum's list of central capabilities includes practical reason and affiliation {{nussbaum2011}}. In Chapter 6 these frameworks were introduced as guides to what accuracy should take in. Here they guide what the physician should look for. Mr Fenwick is not only a man with a loss; he is also a man who has kept coming back, who is courteous and apologetic, who evidently wants something from the relation. An accurate perception sees that as a resource, an expression of affiliation and of practical reason working under constraint, rather than only as a symptom of need. Responsiveness can then build on it. This is one way in which attention differs from the welfare-oriented gaze of paternalism: it perceives agency as well as need.

## 7.4 The struggling doctoral student

### 7.4.1 The case

Dr Okafor supervises a doctoral student, Priya, now in the third year of a four-year funded programme in a laboratory science. In her first two years Priya was productive, and her first-year review was strong. For the past six months her output has fallen sharply. She misses some supervision meetings and arrives at others with little to show. When asked, she says she is "fine" and "just behind". Dr Okafor has four other doctoral students, a large grant to deliver and teaching commitments. The department's procedures require a formal progress review at the end of each year and set out steps for students who fall behind: a written warning, a revised timetable, and, if milestones are missed, possible referral to a progress committee.

In the first version, Dr Okafor follows these procedures carefully and fairly. She records the missed meetings, sets clear revised milestones, and reminds Priya of the support services available. She is not unkind. But she relates to Priya's difficulty entirely through the categories the procedures supply: progress, milestones, risk of non-completion. When Priya misses a further milestone, Dr Okafor initiates the formal review, as the rules require. It later emerges that Priya's central experiment had failed in a way she believed was her fault and was ashamed to report; that she had come to doubt whether she belonged in the programme; and that she had interpreted Dr Okafor's procedural correctness as confirmation that her supervisor had given up on her.

In the second version, Dr Okafor notices that the change in Priya's work is abrupt and that her "fine" is said in a way that does not invite further questions. She sets aside a meeting with no agenda and no talk of milestones. She asks what has been happening with the experiment, and when Priya hesitates, says that experiments fail all the time and that she wants to see the data. They look at the results together. Dr Okafor recognises the failure as a known difficulty with the technique, not a sign of Priya's incompetence. She tells Priya so, and tells her about a failure of her own as a student. She adjusts the project, and they agree a revised plan which Priya drafts. The formal procedures remain in place; Dr Okafor does not suspend them. But they no longer constitute her whole perception of her student.

### 7.4.2 Receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness

The supervisory case differs from the first two in two respects. The relationship is longer and more personal, which makes the role-limits objection relevant; and the critical resource at stake is less obviously a resource at all. Priya's critical resources, in the sense of (D2), include her research time, her funding, her standing in the field and, most importantly, the formation of her capacities as a researcher. These are what the supervisor has discretion over: what projects to propose, when to intervene, how to frame setbacks, what to write in references. That discretion is wide and largely unreviewable. The dependency is asymmetric in a way familiar from Baier's analysis of trust between unequals {{baier1986}}: Priya entrusts to her supervisor something she cares about intensely, her development as a scientist, and is vulnerable to the supervisor's possible, if unexpected, failure of goodwill. Baier's test of the moral decency of a trust relationship asks whether it could survive each party's knowledge of what the other relies on to sustain it. In the first version, the relation fails that test: Priya relies on concealment of her failure, and Dr Okafor relies on procedural correctness to discharge her responsibility without needing to know what lies behind the lost months. Neither reliance could survive disclosure.

Receptivity fails in the first version because the procedural template, like the fact-find and the consultation, supplies the categories through which the student is seen. "Falling behind" is a description of output, not of a person. Accuracy fails because the description is convenient to a supervisor with many demands: a student who is falling behind is a problem with a procedure attached, whereas a student who is ashamed and doubting herself is a person whose difficulty asks something of the supervisor. Responsiveness fails because the supervisor's manner, correct and impersonal, is read by Priya as a judgement, and so the advice compounds the condition it is meant to address.

Jones's account of trust as an affective attitude helps explain this last point {{jones1996}}. Trust on her view involves optimism about another's goodwill and the expectation that they will be moved by the thought that we are counting on them. Priya's trust has not been betrayed in any ordinary sense; Dr Okafor has done nothing wrong by the rules. But the affective content of trust has drained away, because nothing in the supervisor's response signals that she is moved by Priya's dependence on her. The second Dr Okafor restores that signal not by being warmer in some generic way but by attending accurately: seeing the experiment's failure for what it is, and seeing Priya's shame as the obstacle it has become.

### 7.4.3 The role-limits objection

The supervisory case is where the role-limits objection is strongest. Supervisors are not friends, still less therapists. Wasserstrom's critique of professional relationships warned that they tend toward paternalism and domination {{wasserstrom1975}}, and one might think that a supervisor who attends to a student's shame and self-doubt has entered territory where domination is easy and boundaries matter most. Conversely, Fried's model of the lawyer as a limited-purpose friend, who adopts the client's interests as her own within a bounded domain {{fried1976}}, might seem to license exactly the kind of personal engagement that the second Dr Okafor displays, and so to inherit the problems Chapter 4 found with that model.

Chapter 4 argued that the duty of attention is role-bounded but personal: it is owed in virtue of the role and its scope is set by the role's critical resources, but it is directed to the particular person rather than to an instance of a type. The supervisory case shows how this works. The second Dr Okafor does not become Priya's confidante about everything. She does not ask about her family, her relationships or her health except as Priya chooses to raise them. What she attends to is everything bearing on the critical resources over which she has discretion: the experiment, Priya's capacity to continue, her understanding of what failure means in research. Shame and self-doubt fall within that scope because they are obstacles to the formation of research capacity, not because the supervisor has taken on a general care of the student's well-being. The role sets the boundary of what is attended to; attention fills the space within it.

This reply also answers the worry, drawn from Applbaum's examination of role morality, that roles can be invoked to excuse what would otherwise be wrong {{applbaum1999}}. Applbaum argues that institutional roles generally cannot create permissions to do what would otherwise be wrong. The first Dr Okafor's conduct is not wrong by the standards of ordinary morality in any flagrant way. But it relies on the role in a related manner: the procedures permit, and even encourage, a mode of perception that would be recognised as a failure of attention in any non-professional relationship of comparable dependency. If a parent or a mentor outside an institution responded to a young person's sudden collapse in work only by setting milestones, we would think them inattentive. The role does not cancel the demand; it shapes and bounds it. The partiality worry is thus misplaced. Attention to Priya is not preferential treatment of her over the other students; it is the attention owed to each, which may take different forms with each. A supervisor who attended thus to one student and gave the others template advice would be failing the others, not favouring Priya.[^d5]

### 7.4.4 Dependency work and the supervisor's own position

One further feature of the case anticipates the institutional argument. Dr Okafor is herself constrained. Kittay's account of dependency work holds that those who care for dependants become vulnerable themselves, and that justice requires social support for them {{kittay2020}}. A supervisor is not a dependency worker in Kittay's central sense, since the student is not a dependant in the way a severely disabled person or an infant is. But the structure is analogous. The supervisor carries responsibility for the student's formation, is held accountable for completion rates, and is given limited time and recognition for the attentive part of the work. Supervision done well is invisible to the metrics by which supervisors are judged. A department that measures supervisors by completions and publications, and allots no time to supervision beyond formal meetings, makes the second Dr Okafor's conduct a private sacrifice rather than a professional expectation. That is the condition I call attention debt, to which I now turn.

## 7.5 Attention debt: the institutional condition

### 7.5.1 What attention debt is

Each of the three cases contains a second story behind the first. Hale works for a firm that allots fixed time per client and rewards completed sales. Dr Amadi works in ten-minute appointments, with no continuity of care built into the rota. Dr Okafor supervises five students while delivering a grant, in a department whose metrics register completions but not the work that produces them. In each case the template is not merely the adviser's vice; it is the institution's design. The fact-find, the presenting-complaint consultation and the progress procedure were each built for good reasons: to ensure that nothing standard is missed, that time is used fairly, that students are not left to drift. But they also make template advice the path of least resistance, and in combination with time pressure they can make attention close to impossible.

I call the resulting deficit *attention debt*. An institution incurs attention debt when it assigns advisers a duty of attention (by placing them in relations of discretionary power over clients' critical resources) while withholding the conditions under which that duty can be met. The term "debt" is chosen deliberately. The attention is owed, as Chapter 5 argued; when it is not given, the shortfall does not vanish but accrues, both as harm to clients who are poorly seen and as a burden on advisers who must either absorb the cost of attending privately or accept that they are failing those they serve. The debt is institutional because it is produced by institutional arrangements and can only be discharged by changing them.

Tronto's argument in *Caring Democracy* provides the frame. Tronto contends that democratic societies face a caring deficit, and that the allocation of caring responsibilities, rather than economic growth, should be the central concern of democratic politics {{tronto2013}}. She questions whether markets can be caring, and proposes "caring with" as an ideal of shared responsibility. Attention debt is a specific form of the caring deficit, located not in the family or the welfare state but in the professions. In *Moral Boundaries* Tronto had already made attentiveness the first moral element of care, corresponding to the phase of caring about {{tronto1993}}. If attentiveness is the first phase, then an institution that makes it impossible corrupts everything that follows: responsibility is assigned to the wrong problem, competence is exercised on a misdescribed situation, and the response of the care-receiver, which in Tronto's scheme completes the cycle, never arrives, because the client has not been seen.

### 7.5.2 Who is wronged

The fourth part of the thesis claims that institutions which make attention impossible wrong both advisers and clients. The claim that clients are wronged is straightforward once (D3) is in place: they are owed attention and do not receive it. The claim that advisers are wronged needs defence.

The argument runs as follows. An institution that assigns a person a role-bounded duty, and that benefits from her occupying the role, owes her the conditions necessary to discharge the duty. This is a general principle of fair role design, and it does not depend on care ethics; some version of it belongs to any account of what employers owe employees whose work carries professional obligations. The distinctive contribution of care ethics is to explain why failure here is a wrong and not only an inefficiency. Kittay's argument that dependency workers become vulnerable through their work, and that justice requires social responsibility for supporting them, transfers to advisers, with the adjustment noted in Section 7.4.4 {{kittay2020}}. The adviser who attends at her own cost, staying late or seeing patients through her lunch hour, bears a burden the institution has shifted onto her. The adviser who does not attend bears a different burden: the knowledge that she is failing people she is supposed to serve. Either way, the institution has placed her in a position where meeting her obligations is costly or impossible. Held's insistence that the values of care should shape institutions beyond the family, together with her account of the limits of market thinking {{held2006}}, supports the further point that this is not a private matter between adviser and employer but a question of how professional institutions should be governed.

Goodin's grounding of special responsibilities in vulnerability adds a further element {{goodin1985}}. On Goodin's view, responsibilities follow vulnerability, and they reach beyond those who have voluntarily committed themselves. Institutions that structure advising are among those to whose choices clients are vulnerable. A client's exposure to template advice is, in large part, exposure to the design of the firm, the practice or the department. The institution's responsibility to make attention possible is therefore not derivative of the adviser's duty; it is a responsibility grounded in the same vulnerability, falling on a different agent.

### 7.5.3 Discharging the debt

What would discharge attention debt? It would be a mistake to answer by proposing a new layer of regulation requiring advisers to demonstrate attention. That would convert attention into another template, a box to tick, with the predictable result that the box is ticked and the attention is not given. O'Neill's critique, in *A Question of Trust*, of audit and accountability regimes that distort professional practice and can undermine the trust they aim to restore is directly relevant {{oneill2002trust}}. What she proposes instead is intelligent accountability, which lets people assess trustworthiness rather than substituting measurable proxies for it.

Applied to attention, intelligent accountability suggests three kinds of institutional change, each visible in the cases. The first is *time proportioned to stakes*: irreversible decisions about critical resources should not be structured so that they must be taken at a first meeting, and appointment systems should allow time to be extended where a pattern calls for it. The second is *continuity*: attention is sustained over a relationship, as (D1) requires, and continuity of adviser is a precondition of noticing patterns, as the frequent attender case shows. The third is *recognition*: the attentive part of advising should be visible in how advisers are evaluated, not through metrics of attention, which would invite gaming, but through forms of peer review and reflective practice in which the quality of an adviser's perception of her clients can be discussed. None of these guarantees attention. Each removes an obstacle to it.[^d6]

There is a political dimension here that Tronto's notion of caring with brings out {{tronto2013}}. If attention debt is a form of the caring deficit, then its distribution is a question of justice. It is plausible that clients with greater means can more often buy attention, through private banking, private medicine or a well-resourced university, while others are more likely to receive template advice; this is an empirical conjecture that I do not attempt to establish here [source needed]. An account of owed attention that ignored the possibility would risk becoming a counsel of perfection for individual advisers while leaving the structural distribution of attention untouched. The institutional claim of this dissertation is that owed attention is not only an interpersonal duty but also a standard for judging the institutions within which advising takes place.

## 7.6 The three cases compared

Table 7.1 brings together the analysis of the three cases.

**Table 7.1** The three cases analysed.

| Case | What template advice misses | What attention requires | Institutional condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| The retirement annuity (financial adviser) | That the client's stated risk-aversion is shaped by recent bereavement and inexperience; her unstated wish to help her son; that she has not grasped the decision's irreversibility | Receptivity to what the fact-find cannot record; an accurate description of her present capacity to deliberate; deferring an irreversible decision and checking understanding, while leaving the choice with her | Time proportioned to stakes; no penalty for deferred decisions; recording of the client's own aims as well as her risk score |
| The frequent attender (general practitioner) | That the pattern of attendance is itself significant; the loss of work and isolation behind the complaints; his resources as an agent, not only his need | Letting the pattern register; replacing the "frequent attender" label with a just description; continuing to take symptoms seriously while naming what is noticed and leaving the response to him | Continuity of care; flexible appointment length; resistance to labels that make patients legible only as burdens |
| The struggling doctoral student (academic supervisor) | That the fall in output follows a failed experiment she blames on herself; her shame and self-doubt; how procedural correctness reads to her | Seeing the failure as a known difficulty rather than incompetence; attending to obstacles to her formation as a researcher within the role's bounds; restoring the affective content of trust | Supervision time recognised in workload; supervisory loads set to allow attention; progress procedures that supplement rather than replace supervisory judgement |

*Note.* All three cases are invented thought experiments. In each, the adviser in the template version satisfies the applicable rules of loyalty, competence and disclosure.

Four comparative lessons emerge. First, in every case template advice is compatible with full compliance with the standard accounts of what advisers owe. The cases therefore support the thesis's second claim, that owed attention is not reducible to the fiduciary duties of loyalty and care or to informed consent. Chapter 3 argued this abstractly; here the reduction fails case by case.

Secondly, the three components of (D1) are distinct in practice as well as in analysis. In each case one could imagine an adviser succeeding at one component and failing another. A Hale who registered Ruth's grief (receptivity) but described it to himself as "an emotional client who needs to be steered" (a failure of accuracy) would arrive at paternalism rather than attention. A Dr Okafor who saw accurately that Priya's experiment had failed for technical reasons but responded only by revising the timetable would fail responsiveness. The tripartite structure is not decorative; each component catches a different failure.

Thirdly, the anti-paternalism constraint (D4) does real work in each case, and the line it draws is the one Chapter 6 predicted. In each attentive version, the adviser widens what the client can consider and supports her or his capacity to decide, while leaving the decision with the client. In none does the adviser's perception of the client's good override the client's own choice. The frequent attender case shows that the risk of paternalism is real; the constraint shows how to avoid it without retreating into template advice.

Fourthly, the objections fare differently across the cases, and this is itself instructive. Demandingness is weakest where stakes are high and the marginal cost of attention is low, as in the annuity case, and strongest where institutions have made attention costly, which converts it into the institutional objection answered in Section 7.5. Paternalism is a real risk, but one that attention, properly understood, corrects rather than produces, since the paternalistic response in the frequent attender case turned out to be a failure of accuracy. Role-limits are respected when the role's critical resources bound what is attended to. Unenforceability is partly conceded, but the duty survives as one enforced indirectly through institutional design and professional formation, as many professional obligations are.

## 7.7 Conclusion

This chapter has tested the account of owed attention against three invented cases drawn from finance, medicine and doctoral supervision. In each, an adviser who followed every rule nonetheless failed the client, and the failure was identified precisely by the attentive standard: a failure of receptivity, in which the template fixed what could be seen; a failure of accuracy, in which a role-convenient description took the place of a just one; and a failure of responsiveness, in which the manner of the advice compounded the client's difficulty. In each, an attentive alternative was available without breaching the anti-paternalism constraint, without collapsing the professional role into friendship, and at a cost that was demanding only where institutions had made it so. The chapter has also developed the concept of attention debt, the institutional deficit produced when advisers are placed in relations of discretionary power over critical resources but denied the conditions under which attention is possible, and argued with Tronto that this deficit is a matter of justice as well as of professional ethics.

The cases do not prove the account. Reflective equilibrium does not deliver proofs, and invented cases can always be redescribed. What they show is that the attentive standard picks out a real and recurring kind of failure that the standard accounts leave unnamed, that it does so across professions with different critical resources, and that it survives the four objections in the settings where they are strongest. Chapter 8 draws the argument together, sets out the dissertation's contributions and its limits, and considers briefly whether the account extends to advisers who are not persons at all.

# Chapter 3: Fiduciary Duty and Its Limits

## 3.1 Introduction

Chapter 2 gave an account of attention as a moral achievement and stated the attentive standard (D1), with its three components of receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness. That chapter drew its resources from moral philosophy and from the ethics of care. It said little about the setting in which the advisers of this dissertation actually work. Financial advisers, physicians, lawyers and academic supervisors do not meet their clients as strangers who happen to notice one another, nor as intimates bound by affection. They meet them inside relationships that the law has long treated as special, and the name the common law gives to most of these relationships is *fiduciary*. If professional advisers owe their clients attention, the first and most obvious place to look for the ground and shape of that duty is fiduciary law and the theory that has grown up around it.

This chapter does two things. Its first task is constructive. I set out three influential accounts of what makes a relationship fiduciary: Tamar Frankel's account in terms of entrustment, D. Gordon Smith's account in terms of discretion over a critical resource, and Paul Miller's account in terms of discretionary power over another's practical interests. From Smith's account, suitably extended, I draw the second of the four definitions on which the dissertation relies, (D2), the notion of a *critical resource*. I argue that (D2) is the bridge between the legal and the care-ethical halves of the argument: it identifies, in juridical terms, the same asymmetric dependency that care ethics identifies in relational terms, and it therefore allows the argument of Chapter 5 to show why such dependency generates a duty that is owed rather than merely admirable.

The chapter's second task is critical. I argue that the two classical fiduciary duties, loyalty and care, *presuppose* attention but do not *contain* it. Each duty takes as given a determination of what the beneficiary's interests are in the case at hand, and neither duty, as the law formulates and enforces it, regulates how that determination is made. An adviser can therefore satisfy both duties completely while failing the attentive standard; the widow in the vignette of Chapter 1, advised into an annuity by an adviser who followed every rule, is a case of exactly this kind. The conclusion is not that fiduciary law is defective. Fiduciary law has good reasons, which I shall acknowledge, for confining itself as it does. The conclusion is that the moral relationship which fiduciary law partly regulates is richer than the duties it enforces, and that one of the things it contains, attention, needs a separate account.

The chapter proceeds as follows. Section 3.2 sets out the three theories of the fiduciary relation. Section 3.3 introduces (D2) and defends an extended reading of Smith's notion of a critical resource. Section 3.4 examines what loyalty and care require. Section 3.5 states and defends the presupposition argument. Section 3.6 considers three objections. Section 3.7 draws the threads together and hands on to Chapter 4.

## 3.2 Three theories of the fiduciary relation

Fiduciary law is notoriously difficult to theorise. Courts have applied fiduciary principles to trustees, company directors, partners, agents, solicitors, physicians in some jurisdictions and some financial intermediaries, and they have done so without settling on a single test of when a relationship is fiduciary. The philosophical study of fiduciary law is accordingly a young field; the collection edited by Andrew Gold and Paul Miller presents itself as an attempt to set an agenda for that study, gathering essays on the nature of fiduciary relationships, the content and justification of loyalty, the relation of fiduciary duty to morality, economic analyses, and the extension of fiduciary ideas to public and international law {{goldmiller2014}}. I do not attempt to survey that field. I take three accounts that have been influential and that, between them, isolate the structural features the argument of this dissertation needs.

### 3.2.1 Entrustment

The first account is Frankel's. In her 1983 article, which did much to establish fiduciary law as a field of its own, Frankel argued that fiduciary relationships form a distinct legal category whose importance grows in a society organised around specialised services, and that they share a common structure: the fiduciary is entrusted with power over the entrustor's property or interests, and that power is to be exercised for the entrustor's benefit {{frankel1983}}. The entrustor's resulting vulnerability to the abuse of the power is what justifies the special duties and remedies the law imposes. In the later book-length treatment, *Fiduciary Law*, Frankel extends the analysis across the relationships in which property or power is entrusted to another, including corporate managers, money managers, lawyers and physicians, and argues that entrustment and the risk it creates for the entrustor explain both the duties of loyalty and care and the strictness with which the law polices fiduciaries {{frankel2011}}.

Three features of the entrustment account matter here. First, it is structural rather than consensual: what makes the relationship fiduciary is not that the parties agreed to call it so but that one has come to hold power over the other's interests. Second, it locates the moral significance of the relationship in a *risk*, the entrustor's exposure to misuse of what has been entrusted. Third, and importantly for what follows, Frankel's own examples already include advisers of the kind this dissertation is concerned with. The physician and the lawyer are not exotic additions to a law designed for trustees; they belong to the central cases of a society of specialised services, in which most people must hand over some part of their lives to experts whose work they cannot themselves perform or fully assess.

The entrustment account is, however, permissive about *what* is entrusted. Property is the paradigm, but Frankel speaks of power over interests more generally. This is both a strength and a difficulty. It is a strength because advisers rarely take possession of their clients' property; the financial adviser who recommends an annuity does not hold the widow's savings, and the general practitioner does not hold the patient's body in any proprietary sense. It is a difficulty because "interests" is a word wide enough to swallow the distinction between fiduciary relationships and ordinary relationships of influence. Many people have power over my interests, including my neighbours and the drivers who share my road, without being my fiduciaries. The entrustment account needs some further specification of the kind of power and the kind of interest at stake.

### 3.2.2 Discretion over a critical resource

Smith supplies one such specification. On his critical resource theory, a fiduciary relationship exists when one party acts on behalf of another while exercising discretion over a critical resource belonging to the beneficiary {{smith2002}}. Smith draws on the property-rights theory of the firm, and he argues that the core purpose of fiduciary duty is to combat opportunism in relationships of this kind. The theory explains, on his view, why fiduciary duty is a close cousin of the contractual duty of good faith, and why restitution, the stripping of gains wrongly made, is the usual remedy.

Smith's account narrows Frankel's in two ways. The power must be *discretionary*: a party who merely carries out precise instructions, with no latitude in how to do so, does not occupy the relevant position, because there is no space in which opportunism can operate unobserved. And the thing over which discretion is exercised must be *critical* to the beneficiary. Smith's interest in criticality is driven by the theory of the firm, in which certain resources are critical because the value of a joint enterprise depends on them and because control over them confers leverage. But the idea has a natural generalisation. A resource is critical to a person when her pursuit of her significant ends depends upon it in a way that leaves her exposed if it is misused. That generalisation is what (D2) will make precise.

The opportunism framing deserves comment, because it will matter to the critical half of the chapter. To say that fiduciary duty exists to combat opportunism is to say that its target is a specific failure: the fiduciary's use of discretion to serve her own ends at the beneficiary's expense. That is a failure of *motive* or *allegiance*. It is not a failure of perception. An adviser who misreads her client's situation through laziness, habit or the routines of her office is not, in Smith's sense, behaving opportunistically; she may gain nothing at all from the misreading. I shall argue in Section 3.5 that this is precisely the gap through which template advice passes.

### 3.2.3 Discretionary power over practical interests

The third account is Miller's. Miller argues that fiduciary law has lacked a coherent foundation and offers one drawn from the common law, taking as his starting point the Supreme Court of Canada's decision in *Galambos v. Perez* {{miller2011}}. On his view the fiduciary relationship is a distinctive legal relationship in which one party holds discretionary power over the practical interests of another, and fiduciary duties can be explained entirely by the normatively significant features of that relationship. Where Smith reasons from the economics of opportunism, Miller reasons from the structure of the relation itself: given that one party holds this kind of power over another, certain duties follow, and they follow from the relation rather than from contingent facts about incentives.

Miller's account shares with Smith's the emphasis on discretion, and with Frankel's the emphasis on power, but it adds something neither makes central: the idea that the fiduciary relation is a *relation*, with a normative shape of its own, rather than an arrangement for controlling risk. This relational emphasis is congenial to the argument of this dissertation, because it opens the possibility that the relation might ground duties beyond those the law in fact enforces. If fiduciary duties are explained by the normatively significant features of a relationship of discretionary power, then one may ask whether those same features, attended to with a moral rather than a juridical eye, explain further duties. I shall argue that they do, and that attention is among them.[^d1]

### 3.2.4 What the three accounts share

Despite their differences of method, the three accounts converge on a common structure. In each, the fiduciary relationship involves (a) a party who acts for another, (b) a power or discretion that the acting party holds over something of the other's, (c) a resulting exposure of the other to misuse of that power, and (d) duties whose point is to protect the other against such misuse. They differ chiefly over what grounds (d): risk management in Frankel, the suppression of opportunism in Smith, the normative structure of the relation in Miller. For present purposes the convergence matters more than the difference. All three accounts identify an asymmetry between the parties that is not merely an asymmetry of knowledge, though it usually includes one, but an asymmetry of *standing*: one party is in a position to shape the other's practical situation in ways the other cannot easily control, monitor or undo.

This is the asymmetry care ethics calls dependency. The vocabulary differs, and so does the moral emphasis, but the structure is recognisably the same. Tronto's account of care as activity to maintain, continue and repair our world, beginning in a phase of attentiveness to unmet needs {{tronto1993}}, and Kittay's insistence that human life involves inevitable dependency on others who are thereby placed in positions of responsibility {{kittay2020}}, both describe relations in which one party's flourishing is in the hands of another. Fiduciary theory describes the same relations from the side of the law's interest in preventing abuse. The task of the next section is to find a concept that holds both descriptions together.

## 3.3 Critical resources: (D2)

I propose to take over Smith's notion of a critical resource and to extend it in one respect. The definition is as follows.

> **(D2) Critical resources.** A resource R is a *critical resource* of a client C relative to an adviser A just in case (i) C's capacity to pursue some of her significant ends depends on R; (ii) A exercises discretion, on C's behalf, over R or over C's access to, understanding of or use of R; and (iii) C cannot readily monitor A's exercise of that discretion or recover from its misuse by her own efforts.

Clauses (ii) and (iii) are recognisably Smith's: discretion exercised on another's behalf, in a setting where the beneficiary's exposure makes opportunism both possible and costly. Clause (i) generalises the economic notion of criticality into a practical one. A resource is critical not because a firm's value depends on it but because a person's ability to live the life she is trying to live depends on it. Savings in retirement, bodily health, legal standing in a dispute and progress towards a doctorate are all, in this sense, critical resources of the people who seek advice about them.

### 3.3.1 The extension: deliberative resources

The extension I want to defend lies in clause (ii), in the words "or over C's access to, understanding of or use of R". In the classical fiduciary cases the fiduciary has discretion over the resource itself: the trustee manages the trust fund, the director deploys the company's assets. Advisers often do not. A financial adviser who recommends but does not execute a transaction, a physician who recommends but does not compel a treatment and a supervisor who advises but does not write the thesis all leave the final decision, formally, with the client. One might conclude that advisers of this kind are not fiduciaries at all, or are fiduciaries only in an attenuated sense.

That conclusion would be a mistake, and seeing why is the key to the rest of the dissertation. What the adviser has discretion over is the client's *understanding* of her own situation. The widow does not know what an annuity is, how it compares with the alternatives, what she gives up by purchasing one or whether her circumstances make it wise. She comes to know these things, insofar as she comes to know them at all, through what her adviser selects for her attention, how the adviser frames it and what the adviser leaves out. The adviser's discretion over these matters is real and wide. There is no fact of the matter fixed in advance about which features of a pension decision a client must be told, in what order, with what emphasis, and against what background picture of her life. Each of those choices is the adviser's to make, and each shapes what the client is able to decide.

I shall call the client's understanding of her situation, insofar as it is formed and mediated by the adviser, a *deliberative resource*. A deliberative resource is a critical resource in the sense of (D2) whenever the client's capacity to pursue her significant ends depends on deciding well about R, the adviser has discretion over how R is presented to her, and she cannot readily check the adviser's presentation against an independent source. On this reading, the adviser's discretion over the deliberative resource is the advisory counterpart of the trustee's discretion over the fund. It is less visible, because nothing changes hands, but it is no less consequential.[^d2]

### 3.3.2 Why the extension is not ad hoc

Two considerations suggest that the extension is principled rather than gerrymandered to fit the conclusion. The first is that it follows the logic of the theories from which it departs. Frankel's account speaks of power over interests, not merely over property {{frankel1983}}, and Miller's speaks of discretionary power over practical interests {{miller2011}}. A person's practical interest in deciding well about her own pension is as much a practical interest as her interest in the pension itself; indeed, in an advisory relationship, it is the interest through which the pension is protected or harmed. If the fiduciary concept tracks power over practical interests, it should track power over deliberation about them.

The second consideration is that the extension explains features of the advisory professions that a narrower reading leaves puzzling. Medical ethics, for instance, has long treated the disclosure of information to patients as a matter of moral weight in its own right. The standard text of principlism treats veracity, privacy, confidentiality and fidelity as central features of the professional–patient relationship and places respect for autonomy among its four prima facie principles {{beauchampchildress2019}}. On a narrow reading of fiduciary power, this emphasis on information looks like a separate moral concern grafted onto a relationship whose fiduciary core lies elsewhere. On the extended reading it is the fiduciary core: what the physician holds in trust, among other things, is the patient's understanding of her own condition.

### 3.3.3 Critical resources as the bridge to dependency

The importance of (D2) for the dissertation lies in its double aspect. Read from the side of fiduciary law, it states the conditions under which one party's discretion makes another vulnerable to opportunism. Read from the side of care ethics, it states the conditions under which one party has become dependent on another for something that matters to her life. The two readings pick out the same relationships, and they do so for related reasons: the client is exposed because she depends, and she depends because something critical to her has passed, in part, into another's discretion.

This double aspect is what allows (D2) to serve as a bridge. Chapter 5 will argue that asymmetric dependency of this kind generates a duty of attention that is owed to the dependent party rather than merely admirable when supplied, and it will draw on accounts of trust and vulnerability to make that case. Its argument will need a precise specification of which relationships are in view; without one, the claim that dependency generates owed attention would extend to every relationship in which one person's welfare is affected by another's choices, and would become implausibly demanding. (D2) supplies the specification. The relationships in which attention is owed, in the sense this dissertation defends, are those in which an adviser holds discretion over a client's critical resources, including her deliberative resources, under conditions that prevent her from monitoring or correcting that discretion herself.[^d3]

## 3.4 What loyalty and care require

The classical content of fiduciary obligation is usually summarised as two duties, loyalty and care. I do not claim to give a complete doctrinal account of either; doctrine varies across jurisdictions and across kinds of fiduciary, and the philosophical literature disagrees about how the duties are best understood {{goldmiller2014}}. What I need is a characterisation precise enough to show what the duties regulate and what they leave unregulated.

### 3.4.1 Loyalty

The duty of loyalty is the distinctive fiduciary duty. On the entrustment account it exists because entrusted power can be turned to the fiduciary's own advantage {{frankel2011}}; on the critical resource account it exists to combat opportunism {{smith2002}}. In either form, its content is principally *exclusionary*. It forbids the fiduciary to place herself in a position where her own interests conflict with the beneficiary's, to profit from her position without the beneficiary's informed consent, and to act for a third party whose interests are adverse to the beneficiary's. The duty is often described as strict, in the sense that a fiduciary who breaches it may be liable even where the beneficiary suffered no loss and the fiduciary acted in good faith, and restitution of the gains made is the characteristic remedy {{smith2002}}.

The exclusionary character of loyalty is important. Loyalty, so understood, tells the fiduciary what considerations must *not* enter her deliberation: her own gain, a third party's advantage, the convenience of her firm at the beneficiary's cost. It does not tell her what considerations *must* enter. It requires that she act for the beneficiary's interests, but it takes the identification of those interests as given and regulates only the fiduciary's allegiance to them. A fiduciary who has correctly identified the beneficiary's interests and then acts to advance them is loyal; so is a fiduciary who has *incorrectly* identified those interests, provided the error does not arise from, or serve, a conflicting allegiance.

### 3.4.2 Care

The duty of care, by contrast, is not distinctive to fiduciaries. It is the duty, shared with many non-fiduciary relationships, to exercise the skill and diligence a reasonable practitioner in the fiduciary's position would exercise. Frankel treats it, alongside loyalty, as explained by entrustment {{frankel2011}}; but its standard is borrowed from the general law of negligence, and its content is set by the norms of competent practice in the relevant field. A financial adviser meets the duty of care when she does what competent financial advisers do; a physician meets it when her conduct accords with what a responsible body of medical opinion would endorse.

It is a feature of this standard, not a defect, that it is *generic*. The reasonable practitioner is a construct: she represents what the profession collectively expects of its members in situations of a given type. The standard is applied to particular cases, of course, and courts attend to particular facts in deciding whether it was met. But the question it poses is whether the adviser's conduct fell within the range of conduct acceptable for a case of *this kind*, not whether the adviser saw *this* client truly. The two questions often have the same answer. They do not always.

### 3.4.3 Disclosure and suitability

In the advisory professions, loyalty and care are typically supplemented by duties of disclosure and, in financial advice, by duties of suitability or of knowing one's client. These rules require the adviser to gather specified information about the client, such as her age, income, assets, dependants, investment horizon and stated tolerance for risk, and to make recommendations consistent with that information. They are the nearest thing fiduciary practice possesses to a rule requiring attention, and I shall return to them in Section 3.6.3. For the moment it is enough to note their form. They specify *which facts* about the client must be collected and impose a requirement of *consistency* between those facts and the advice given. They are, in a precise sense, templates: a fixed set of fields that every client's situation must be made to fill.[^d4]

## 3.5 The presupposition argument

I can now state the central critical claim of the chapter. Fiduciary loyalty and care presuppose attention, in the sense that each operates on a determination of the client's interests that attention is needed to secure; but neither duty contains attention, in the sense that an adviser can fully satisfy both while failing the attentive standard (D1).

### 3.5.1 The argument stated

The argument can be set out in six steps.

1. The fiduciary duties of loyalty and care each require the adviser to act for the client's interests, in the case at hand, as those interests bear on the critical resources over which the adviser has discretion.
2. To act for a client's interests in the case at hand, an adviser must have some determination of what those interests are, a working picture of the client's situation, ends and vulnerabilities as they bear on the decision.
3. Whether that working picture is adequate depends on how it was formed: on whether the client's situation was allowed to register on its own terms, whether it was seen truly against distortion, and whether what was seen was allowed to shape the advice. These are the three components of the attentive standard (D1).
4. Loyalty regulates the adviser's allegiance to the client's interests as determined, but not the formation of the determination itself, except where a defective determination is produced by or serves a conflicting allegiance.
5. Care regulates the adviser's conduct by reference to what a reasonable practitioner would do in a case of the relevant type, and so regulates the formation of the working picture only to the extent that the picture departs from what the profession's generic expectations would yield.
6. Therefore an adviser can satisfy loyalty and care while forming her working picture of the client in a way that fails receptivity, accuracy or responsiveness: that is, while giving template advice.

The conclusion is the claim that loyalty and care presuppose but do not contain attention. They presuppose it because premises 1–3 show that the point of both duties is defeated if the working picture is wrong: loyalty to a misidentified interest protects nothing, and diligence in serving a misdescribed situation is wasted effort. They do not contain it because premises 4 and 5 show that the duties, as formulated, leave the formation of the working picture unregulated in a range of cases.

### 3.5.2 Premise 3 and the role of (D1)

Premise 3 does the most philosophical work, and it is here that the argument draws on Chapter 2. A working picture of a client is not given to the adviser; it is made. The materials from which it is made, such as what the client says, what she does not say, the documents she brings and the history of the relationship, underdetermine the picture, and the adviser fills the gap with her own categories and expectations. Murdoch's central claim, as Chapter 2 developed it, is that moral life goes on continuously in how we see, and that the characteristic mark of the active moral agent is a just and loving gaze directed upon an individual reality {{murdoch1970}}. The adviser's working picture is a product of exactly this kind of seeing. It can be made attentively or inattentively, and the difference shows up not in what the adviser decides once the picture is formed but in the picture she decides on.

The three components of (D1) correspond to three distinct ways the working picture can go wrong. A failure of *receptivity* occurs when the adviser fits the client to a pre-existing type before the client's particular situation has had a chance to register; the widow is classified as "recently bereaved, risk-averse, needs income" before anyone has asked what she is trying to do with the rest of her life. A failure of *accuracy* occurs when the picture is distorted by what is convenient for the adviser or her role: the adviser who sees in a client the case her firm's products are designed for, or the physician who sees in a frequent attender a nuisance rather than a patient. A failure of *responsiveness* occurs when the adviser sees truly but does not let what she sees shape the advice or the manner of giving it, proceeding with the recommendation that was always going to be made.

### 3.5.3 Premise 4: loyalty without attention

Premise 4 is a claim about the scope of loyalty. Its plausibility depends on the exclusionary character of loyalty established in Section 3.4.1. Consider a financial adviser who receives no commission on annuities and whose firm has no interest in which product the widow buys. She asks the questions the suitability rules require, records the answers, and recommends the product that a standard risk profile recommends for a person with those answers. She has no conflicting allegiance. She profits nothing from the recommendation beyond her ordinary fee. She is, in every sense the duty of loyalty recognises, loyal.

Suppose, however, that the widow's real concern, which a receptive adviser would have heard, is not income but the wish to help a grandchild through university in the next five years, something an annuity, which converts capital into a stream of payments, makes difficult. The adviser did not hear this because the intake form had no field for it and because the widow, not knowing it was relevant, mentioned it only in passing. The adviser's loyalty is to the widow's interests as determined by the form. Those were not the widow's interests. Loyalty has been satisfied and its point has been missed.

It might be said that the adviser in this example is not really loyal, because a loyal adviser would have cared enough to find out. That reply trades on an ordinary-language sense of loyalty, as devotion, which is broader than the fiduciary sense. In the fiduciary sense, the adviser's failure is not a failure of allegiance. If the law treated it as a breach of loyalty, the strict liability and restitutionary remedies characteristic of that duty {{smith2002}} would apply to every inattentive misjudgement, which no account of fiduciary law I know of supports. I take up a more sophisticated version of the reply in Section 3.6.1.

### 3.5.4 Premise 5: care without attention

Premise 5 is a claim about the standard of care. The reasonable practitioner standard asks whether the adviser's conduct fell within the range acceptable to her profession for cases of the relevant type. That standard incorporates attention exactly to the extent that the profession's norms do. If competent financial advisers routinely ask clients about their family obligations, the adviser who fails to ask breaches the duty of care. But the standard can incorporate attention only as a *procedure*: as a set of things a reasonable practitioner would ask, check or consider. It cannot incorporate attention as a *quality of perception*, because a quality of perception is not something a generic standard can specify in advance.

The point is not merely practical. Attention, as Chapter 2 argued, is responsive to the particular; its object is an individual reality, not an instance of a type. A generic standard, by contrast, is defined over types: it says what a reasonable practitioner would do in a case *of this kind*. A procedure derived from such a standard can require the adviser to ask about the client's family; it cannot require the adviser to hear the answer. The widow's adviser may well have asked whether she had dependants, recorded "no", since the grandchild is not a dependant in the form's sense, and moved on. She met the standard. What she did not do was let the widow's situation register on its own terms.

This argument does not assume that the reasonable practitioner standard is lax or that professions set their norms too low. Even a demanding professional standard is generic in form. The more demanding it becomes, the more procedures it can require; but each procedure is another field in the template, and an adviser can complete every field without attending to the person whose situation they describe. This is why I describe the vice opposed to attention as *template advice*. Template advice is not careless advice. It can be extremely careful. It is advice formed by fitting the client to a type and responding to the type.[^d5]

### 3.5.5 The widow revisited

It is worth stating plainly what the presupposition argument says about the vignette with which Chapter 1 began. The widow's adviser followed every rule. On the analysis just given, that description is accurate and not ironic: the adviser was loyal in the fiduciary sense, met the standard of care, satisfied the suitability rules and obtained the widow's consent. Nothing in fiduciary doctrine, as I have characterised it, was breached. And yet, as Chapter 1 argued and as most readers will feel, the widow was wronged.

The presupposition argument explains both halves of this judgement. The adviser did not breach fiduciary duty, because fiduciary duty regulates allegiance and generic competence, and the adviser's failure was neither disloyal nor incompetent by her profession's lights. The widow was nevertheless wronged, because she depended on the adviser for something, an accurate understanding of her own situation and options, that the adviser had discretion over and that the adviser formed inattentively. In the terms of (D2), her deliberative resource was mishandled. The wrong consists in this mishandling, and it is a wrong that fiduciary law presupposes the absence of but does not itself prohibit.

### 3.5.6 Why fiduciary law confines itself

It would be easy to read the presupposition argument as an indictment of fiduciary law, and that is not my intention. There are good reasons for the law to regulate allegiance strictly and competence generically while leaving the quality of perception largely to the professional. Allegiance is relatively visible after the fact: conflicts of interest leave traces in commissions, transactions and relationships with third parties. Competence can be assessed against professional norms by expert evidence. The quality of an adviser's perception of a particular client is, by contrast, very hard to establish retrospectively and, as Chapter 6 will argue in answering the unenforceability objection, is in some respects not the kind of thing law can directly require.

The law's restraint here is therefore a form of wisdom about its own instruments. The consequence of that restraint, however, is that the moral content of the advisory relationship outruns its legal content. An account of what advisers owe their clients that stops at fiduciary duty will systematically miss the wrong done by template advice. The dissertation's claim that owed attention is not reducible to fiduciary duty, which is clause (ii) of its central thesis, is the claim that this gap is real and morally significant.

## 3.6 Objections and replies

### 3.6.1 Objection: loyalty, properly understood, already includes attention

The most serious objection holds that I have caricatured loyalty by treating it as merely exclusionary. On a richer understanding, loyalty includes a positive orientation towards the beneficiary's good: not only refraining from serving other interests but actively serving the beneficiary's. And actively serving someone's good, the objection continues, requires finding out what it is. Miller's account, on which fiduciary duties are explained entirely by the normatively significant features of a relationship of discretionary power over another's practical interests {{miller2011}}, might seem to support this reading. If the relation is one of power over practical interests, then the duty that flows from it is a duty to exercise that power *for* those interests, and that includes the effort to understand them.

I accept part of this. Loyalty in the fullest moral sense, devotion to another's good, does include attention, and one could describe owed attention as an aspect of loyalty in that sense. My claim concerns fiduciary loyalty as a legal duty with a determinate content and characteristic remedies. Two points support treating the two separately.

First, the richer reading would make the strictness of fiduciary loyalty unintelligible. The duty can be strict, applying even without loss and even in good faith, because its content is a prohibition that the fiduciary can always comply with by declining to place herself in a conflict. A positive duty to understand the beneficiary's good fully could not be strict in that way; it would require the law to determine, for every fiduciary decision, whether the fiduciary had understood well enough, and to impose restitutionary liability whenever she had not. No account of fiduciary law I am aware of draws that consequence, and Miller's relational account, so far as I can see, explains the existing duties rather than adding new ones.

Second, even if the positive reading were adopted, it would not by itself yield the attentive standard. A positive duty to serve the beneficiary's interests could be discharged by diligent enquiry along standard lines, which is to say by careful template advice. What distinguishes attention from diligence is not the amount of effort but the quality of perception: receptivity to what does not fit the template, accuracy against role-convenient distortion and responsiveness in the manner as well as the content of advice. A richer loyalty would close part of the gap between fiduciary duty and attention, but not all of it.[^d6]

### 3.6.2 Objection: informed consent fills the gap

A second objection holds that whatever gap exists between fiduciary duty and attention is filled by the requirement of informed consent. If the widow was given the information needed to decide, and decided, then whatever was missing from the adviser's perception was supplied by her own. On this view, the client is the guardian of her own particular interests, and the adviser need only provide general information and a recommendation suitable to the type of case; the client then applies it to her own situation.

This objection rests on a picture of the client as an independent chooser who brings a complete understanding of her own ends to the advisory encounter and needs from the adviser only technical information. That picture is precisely what the extended reading of (D2) denies. The client's deliberative resource, her understanding of what her situation is and what turns on the decision, is not fully formed before the encounter and then informed by it; it is formed *in* the encounter, partly by the adviser's selections and framings. Consent obtained on the basis of a deliberative resource the adviser shaped inattentively does not cure the inattention. It inherits it.

O'Neill's critique of the conceptions of individual autonomy dominant in bioethics is relevant here. She argues that those conceptions are philosophically and ethically inadequate, that they tend to undermine rather than support trust between patients, professionals and institutions, and that informed consent, as commonly practised, needs rethinking {{oneill2002bioethics}}. I do not need her positive proposal of principled autonomy to make the present point. It is enough that consent, understood as a client's authorisation of a course of action on the basis of information supplied, cannot do the work of attention, because the quality of the information supplied depends on the quality of the adviser's perception of what this client needs to know. Chapter 6 develops the relation between attention and autonomy at length, and argues that attention, far from threatening the client's autonomy, is among its conditions in advisory relationships. Here I note only that informed consent is the third item, alongside loyalty and care, to which owed attention is irreducible.

### 3.6.3 Objection: know-your-client rules already require attention

A third objection is more concrete. In financial advice, at least, regulators already require advisers to know their clients: to gather detailed information about their circumstances and objectives and to ensure that recommendations are suitable. Is this not a legal requirement of attention, which shows that law can and does regulate the formation of the adviser's working picture?

The reply follows from Section 3.4.3. Know-your-client rules are the most developed attempt in advisory regulation to secure the conditions of attention, and they are valuable. But their form is necessarily that of a template. They specify fields: age, income, dependants, horizon, risk tolerance and so on. They require that the fields be filled and that the advice be consistent with them. An adviser who fills the fields mechanically, reading the questions aloud and recording the first answers given, complies. Indeed, the existence of such rules may in some settings make attention *harder*, because it invites both adviser and client to treat the completed form as the adviser's knowledge of the client. O'Neill's argument that audit and accountability regimes adopted to restore trust can distort professional practice and undermine it {{oneill2002trust}} is relevant here, and Chapter 5 takes it up. Chapter 7 will argue that institutional devices of this kind can contribute to what I call *attention debt*: an institutionally produced deficit of attention that individual advisers cannot easily make good.

The objection therefore confirms rather than refutes the presupposition argument. Know-your-client rules exist because regulators recognise that loyalty and care do not on their own secure an accurate picture of the client. But the rules secure the picture only to the extent that a template can, and the residue, the difference between filling in a client's form and seeing her, is what the attentive standard names.

## 3.7 Conclusion

This chapter has examined the fiduciary structure of the advisory relationship and has reached two conclusions. The first is constructive. Drawing on Frankel's account of entrustment, Smith's critical resource theory and Miller's account of discretionary power, I introduced (D2): a critical resource is something on which a client's capacity to pursue her significant ends depends, over which an adviser exercises discretion on her behalf, and whose handling she cannot readily monitor or correct. I extended Smith's notion to include deliberative resources, a client's understanding of her own situation as formed and mediated by the adviser, and argued that this extension is continuous with the fiduciary theories from which it departs and explains features of advisory practice that a narrower reading leaves puzzling. (D2) identifies in juridical terms the same asymmetric dependency that care ethics describes in relational terms, and so serves as the bridge between the two halves of the dissertation's grounding claim.

The second conclusion is critical. Fiduciary loyalty regulates the adviser's allegiance to her client's interests as determined; the fiduciary duty of care regulates her conduct against the generic expectations of her profession; neither regulates the quality of perception through which the client's interests come to be determined in the first place. Both duties presuppose attention, because their point is defeated if the adviser's working picture of the client is wrong, but neither contains it, because an adviser can satisfy both while giving template advice. Informed consent and know-your-client rules narrow the gap without closing it. Fiduciary law has good reasons for confining itself in this way, but the confinement means that the moral content of the advisory relationship outruns its legal content.

These conclusions raise a question that the fiduciary framework cannot answer on its own. If advisers owe their clients more than loyalty and care, and specifically owe them an attention directed at the particular person, then the advisory relationship begins to look personal in a way that sits uneasily with its professional and role-bound character. An adviser is not her client's friend, yet attention of the kind (D1) describes is most at home in friendship and in other relations of personal care. Chapter 4 takes up this difficulty. It examines the morality of professional roles and the most famous attempt to model the professional relationship on friendship, and argues that owed attention is a duty that is bounded by role yet personal in its object.

# Chapter 2: Attention as a Moral Achievement

## 2.1 Introduction

Chapter 1 claimed that Hale failed to attend to Ruth, and that this failure, not any breach of rule, was what wronged her. The claim depends on a concept of attention that is not yet clear. In ordinary speech "attention" can mean anything from the bare fact of noticing ("the noise caught my attention") to a sustained concentration of effort ("she gave the problem her full attention"). Neither sense is what I need. Hale noticed what Ruth said, and he concentrated hard on the meeting. What he failed to give her is something more specific and more demanding, something that can be done well or badly and that can be owed.

This chapter builds that concept from four sources. Section 2.2 takes up Iris Murdoch's account of moral vision, which gives attention a place at the centre of moral life and links it to accuracy, or justice, of perception. Section 2.3 turns to Simone Weil, from whom Murdoch borrowed the term, and draws out the receptive, self-suspending side of attention. Section 2.4 considers the care tradition's accounts of engrossment and receptivity in Noddings, Gilligan and Held, which locate attention within a relationship between a carer and a cared-for. Section 2.5 examines Tronto's account of attentiveness as the first phase of care, which makes attention a practical and political matter and not only a personal one. Section 2.6 then states the attentive standard as (D1) and examines its three components. Section 2.7 asks whether attention so understood is a virtue, a skill or a duty, and argues that the answer need not be exclusive. Section 2.8 describes the vice opposed to attention, template advice. Section 2.9 answers three objections to the account, and section 2.10 hands on to Chapter 3.

A word on the strategy. None of the four sources was written about professional advisers, and I am not offering an interpretation of any of them for its own sake. I take from each what it contributes to an account of attention that can do work in the advisory setting, and I note where I depart from them. The resulting concept is meant to be recognisably continuous with theirs while answering to a different question.

## 2.2 Murdoch: moral vision and the just gaze

### 2.2.1 Against the moment of choice

In *The Sovereignty of Good*, Murdoch argues against a picture of the moral agent that she finds common to existentialism and to the analytic moral philosophy of her day {{murdoch1970}}. On that picture, moral life consists of discrete moments of choice. The agent surveys a situation whose features are publicly available and morally neutral, and then exercises a will that is free precisely because it is not determined by anything in the agent's inner life. Morality is located in the choosing, and whatever happens in the agent's mind between choices is either morally irrelevant or merely a private preparation for the public act.

Murdoch objects that this leaves out most of what matters. Moral life, she holds, goes on continuously, in how we see and in the quality of our consciousness, and not only at the moments when we act. By the time the moment of choice arrives, much of the moral work has already been done, because the situation presents itself to us under descriptions that our habits of attention have shaped. The person who has trained herself to see others charitably, accurately and without self-preoccupation will find that the right choice is often obvious when it comes. The person who has not will find that the situation presents itself under descriptions that make a poor choice look reasonable. On this view, one might say, we can choose only within the world we are able to see, and so what we are able to see is the first moral question.[^d1]

This matters for the advisory case in an obvious way. If morality lived only in choices, then Hale, whose choices were all defensible, would have done nothing wrong. Murdoch's view allows us to say that his failure came earlier, in the way he took in Ruth's situation, and that his defensible choices were made within a world he had seen badly. It also explains why the failure is hard to detect from outside. A choice is public and can be checked against rules. A way of seeing is largely private, and its defects show up only indirectly, in what it leads the agent to overlook.

### 2.2.2 The example of M and D

Murdoch's best-known illustration is the case of a mother-in-law, M, and her daughter-in-law, D {{murdoch1970}}. As I shall summarise it, M begins with an unfavourable view of D, one coloured by her own prejudices and attachments, while behaving correctly toward her. Over time M works on her own perception, looking at D again and questioning the motives behind her first judgement, and she comes to see D more justly. The example is constructed so that D's conduct need not change and M's outward behaviour need not change either. Yet something morally significant has happened, and it has happened in M's way of seeing. Murdoch uses the case to show that moral progress can consist in an inner reorientation of this kind.

Three features of the example are important for what follows. First, M's change is an *achievement*. It does not simply happen to her. She works at it, against resistance, by asking questions of her own perceptions and by deliberately looking again. Attention in Murdoch's sense is not passive receptivity but a disciplined activity. Second, the change is toward *accuracy*. M's new vision is not merely kinder but more just, and on Murdoch's view justice in perception is a matter of seeing the individual as she really is, freed from the distortions the self imposes. The just gaze is just because it sees what is there. Third, the change is directed at an *individual*. M is not adopting a better general theory of daughters-in-law. She is coming to see D, this particular person, more clearly. Attention is directed at particulars, and its success is measured by how well it grasps them.

Each of these features carries over into the attentive standard. The adviser's attention must be disciplined, aimed at accuracy and directed at the particular client. But the advisory case also differs from M's in one important respect. M's attention to D is morally significant even though it changes nothing in her behaviour. The adviser's attention matters because it must change the advice. Murdoch is concerned with the inner life as a moral domain in its own right. I am concerned with attention as a precondition of a practical relationship in which the adviser's perception issues in recommendations the client acts on. That is why the third component of the attentive standard, responsiveness, has no strict counterpart in Murdoch's example.[^d2]

### 2.2.3 The ego and distortion

Why is accurate perception of another person difficult? Murdoch's answer is that the self gets in the way. Our perception of others is continually distorted by self-interest, by anxiety, by the wish to be comfortable and by the pull of consoling fantasy. She treats the ego as the chief obstacle to moral vision, and she presents the Good, like art, as something that draws us out of self-preoccupation toward a reality that is not of our making {{murdoch1970}}. Attention, on this picture, is the effort to see past the self to what is really there.

I want to keep this diagnosis while broadening it. In the advisory setting the distorting forces include not only the personal ego, the adviser's vanity, impatience or wish to be thought competent, but also what I will call *role convenience*. Every professional role comes with a set of categories, procedures and expectations that make some readings of a client's situation easy and others hard. The financial adviser's risk profiler makes it easy to read Ruth as "highly risk-averse" and hard to read her as grieving and unsettled. The general practitioner's familiarity with a frequent attender makes it easy to read a new complaint as more of the same. These readings are convenient not because they serve the adviser's personal interests, though they sometimes do, but because they fit the role's ready-made categories and so require no further work. A perception distorted by role convenience can be just as false as one distorted by vanity, and it is harder to notice because it presents itself as professionalism.

This extension is not something Murdoch says, and I do not claim that she would accept it. But it is in the spirit of her view. If the obstacle to attention is whatever substitutes a comfortable fiction for an uncomfortable reality, then the comfortable fictions supplied by roles and institutions belong on the list. They are, in a sense, the institutional form of the ego.

## 2.3 Weil: attention as waiting

### 2.3.1 The school-studies essay

Murdoch borrowed the notion of attention from Simone Weil, and Weil's account brings out an aspect that Murdoch's emphasis on effortful reorientation can obscure. In the essay on the right use of school studies collected in *Waiting for God*, Weil argues that the real point of academic study, whatever its subject, is to develop the faculty of attention {{weil1951}}. A student who works at a geometry problem and fails to solve it has not wasted the effort if the effort has trained attention, because attention is the substance of prayer and of the love of one's neighbour.

What is striking is how Weil characterises attention. It is a kind of waiting: a receptive suspension of one's own thought, in which the mind holds itself open to receive its object {{weil1951}}. I read this as a contrast with rushing to grasp the object under a concept already to hand. On this reading, many of the errors we make, in study and elsewhere, come from haste, from seizing on an answer too early because we cannot bear the open state of not yet knowing. Attention is the discipline of tolerating that state long enough for the reality to show itself.

### 2.3.2 Attention to the sufferer

Weil connects this intellectual discipline directly to the moral life. The capacity to give attention to a person who is suffering is, she holds, rare and precious {{weil1951}}. To attend in this way is to be able to ask the sufferer what they are going through, and to mean the question, which is to recognise the sufferer not as a specimen of a category (one of the unfortunate, a case of a familiar kind) but as a particular human being whose situation has to be learned from them. This requires precisely the suspension of one's own ready-made thoughts that the school-studies essay describes. The person who already knows what the sufferer is going through, because they have classified it, cannot ask.

The relevance to the advisory case is direct. Ruth was not suffering in any dramatic sense, but she was grieving, alone with unfamiliar responsibilities, and unsure about her future. The question Hale needed to ask, in effect, was what she was going through. He asked instead the questions on the fact-find, which are questions about which category she belonged to. Weil's account shows why these are not the same. A fact-find can be completed without the adviser ever suspending his own framework, because its purpose is to place the client within one. The attentive question can be asked only by an adviser who is, for the moment, willing not to know.

### 2.3.3 What I take from Weil, and what I leave

Weil's account is embedded in a religious metaphysics that I do not rely on. For her, attention to the neighbour is continuous with attention to God, and its value derives in part from that continuity. Nothing in my argument depends on this, and I take from Weil only the phenomenology of attention as receptive waiting and her claim that such attention is what the suffering person most needs and least often receives.[^d3]

I also depart from Weil in one further respect. Weil's attention is, in its purest form, almost wholly passive: the mind empties itself and waits. The adviser cannot simply wait. Advice must eventually be given, usually within limited time, and the adviser's expertise, which is precisely the framework of categories that Weil asks us to suspend, is what the client is paying for. The adviser's attention must therefore combine Weil's receptive suspension with Murdoch's active correction and with the expert's knowledge of what to look for. This is why the attentive standard separates receptivity from accuracy. Receptivity is the Weilian moment, the letting-register. Accuracy is the Murdochian work of seeing truly, which may involve the active use of expertise to interpret what has registered. The two must be held together, and the hardest part of attentive advising is knowing when to suspend one's categories and when to apply them.

## 2.4 Engrossment and the caring relation

### 2.4.1 A different voice

Murdoch and Weil give us attention as an individual moral achievement. The ethics of care sets it within a relationship. The tradition's starting point is usually traced to Gilligan's *In a Different Voice*, which argued that the dominant psychology of moral development had measured women's moral reasoning against a standard built around justice and rights, and so had misread an orientation toward care, responsibility and relationships as a deficient stage, when it was a distinct moral orientation {{gilligan1982}}.

For present purposes the importance of Gilligan's work lies less in its claims about gender, which have been much debated and which I do not need, than in its picture of what moral reasoning in the care orientation attends to. On the reading I adopt, a moral orientation organised around justice and rights invites abstraction from the particulars of a situation so that general principles can be applied impartially, whereas an orientation organised around care, responsibility and relationships keeps those particulars in view: who is involved, what they need, how they are connected and what would sustain or damage the relationships at stake. Moral competence in this orientation is in large part a competence of perception, an ability to see what a particular situation involves for the particular people in it. That is already close to what I mean by attention, and it suggests that a moral theory that abstracts from particulars will tend to lose sight of attention as a demand.

### 2.4.2 Noddings on engrossment

Noddings makes the perceptual element of care explicit {{noddings2013}}. On her account, ethics is grounded in the caring relation between a one-caring and a cared-for, not in universal principles. Caring has two marks on the side of the one-caring. The first is *engrossment*, a receptive attention to the reality of the other, in which the one-caring takes in the other's situation as the other experiences it. The second is *motivational displacement*, in which the one-caring's motive energy flows toward the other's projects. And the relation is completed only on the side of the cared-for, who must receive the care and respond to it.

Three aspects of Noddings's account bear on the attentive standard. First, engrossment is receptive in something like Weil's sense: it is a matter of letting the other's reality in, not of projecting one's own. This supports the place of receptivity as the first component. Second, the link between engrossment and motivational displacement shows that, in the caring relation, perception and motivation are not separable stages. What the one-caring sees of the other's reality already moves her toward the other's projects. This is the root of what I call responsiveness. Attention that leaves the adviser's advice untouched has not really been attention in the care-ethical sense. Third, Noddings's insistence that the relation is completed by the cared-for's reception gives the client a role in determining whether attention has succeeded. An adviser who believes she has attended, but whose client experiences the advice as generic and unseeing, has at least some reason to doubt that she has done so.[^d4]

I depart from Noddings in two ways. First, her paradigm relations, between parent and child or teacher and pupil, involve a degree of engrossment and motivational displacement that would be inappropriate in most advisory relationships. A financial adviser whose motive energy flowed wholly toward a client's projects would have lost the critical distance that makes his advice worth having. The attentive standard therefore requires receptivity and responsiveness but not the full motivational displacement of Noddings's one-caring. Second, Noddings is suspicious of principles and of obligations that extend beyond actual caring relations. My project, by contrast, is to state a principle, a duty that holds of advisers as such. I take it that Noddings's account of the structure of caring attention can be detached from her scepticism about principles, but I acknowledge that she might not accept the detachment.

### 2.4.3 Held: care as practice and value

Held's account helps with this detachment {{held2006}}. Held presents the ethics of care as a moral theory in its own right, taking relations of care and interdependence rather than independent individuals as basic, and she argues that care is both a practice and a value. As a practice, care consists of activities that can be done well or badly, and it has standards internal to it. As a value, care identifies what is good in caring relations and what should be fostered, not only in families but in social and political institutions. Held extends the approach to the limits of rights-based and market thinking.

Two consequences follow for the present argument. The first is that, if care is a practice with internal standards, then attention, as a component of good care, is also subject to standards. It can be assessed, criticised and required, and not only admired when it happens. The second is that, if care's values properly shape institutions beyond the family, then the attention appropriate to care is not confined to intimate relations. It can be asked of professionals, and of the institutions in which professionals work. Held's account thus gives the care-ethical basis for the fourth claim of the dissertation, that attention is partly an institutional achievement, which Chapter 7 develops.

## 2.5 Tronto: attentiveness as the first phase of care

### 2.5.1 Four phases and four moral elements

Tronto's *Moral Boundaries* gives the most systematic care-ethical account of attention, and it is the one closest to the use I want to make of the concept {{tronto1993}}. Tronto defines care broadly, as the activity by which we maintain, continue and repair our world, and she argues against treating care as a specifically feminine morality, a framing she holds to be historically inaccurate and politically unwise. She distinguishes four phases of care: *caring about*, in which a need is recognised; *taking care of*, in which someone assumes responsibility for meeting it; *care-giving*, in which the need is actually met; and *care-receiving*, in which the cared-for responds. To each phase corresponds a moral element: attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness.

Tronto's scheme brings out two points that the earlier accounts leave implicit. The first is that attentiveness comes *first*. It is the condition on which the other phases depend, because a need that has not been noticed cannot be taken responsibility for, met or responded to. A failure of attentiveness therefore spoils everything that follows, however competently the later phases are carried out. This is exactly the structure of Hale's failure. His competence was not in doubt, and he took responsibility for advising Ruth. But because he did not recognise what she needed, his competence was applied to the wrong problem.

The second point is that Tronto treats attentiveness as a *moral* element, something one can fail at culpably, and not merely as a cognitive precondition. Not noticing the needs of others is, on her account, a moral failing, and her wider argument, that the boundaries separating morality from politics keep care marginal and protect the privileged, suggests that such failures are not evenly distributed.[^d5] This political dimension is important for my argument in Chapter 7, where I suggest that institutions can produce systematic inattention, much as social positions can.

### 2.5.2 From attentiveness to the attentive standard

Tronto's scheme does not map exactly onto mine, and the differences should be made explicit. First, Tronto's attentiveness is the first of four phases, while my attentive standard runs through the whole advisory relationship. An adviser must notice the client's needs at the outset, but must also keep perceiving them as the relationship develops, as circumstances change and as the client's responses to advice reveal more. Attention in the advisory setting is not a phase to be completed but a standard to be kept up.

Second, Tronto uses "responsiveness" for the moral element of care-receiving, which is the cared-for's response and the carer's sensitivity to it. I use the term for the third component of the attentive standard, the shaping of advice by what has been perceived. The two uses are related, since an adviser who lets what she has seen shape her advice will also be sensitive to the client's response to it, but they are not identical. I keep the term because it names the feature I need, and I mark the difference here to avoid confusion.[^d6]

Third, Tronto's attentiveness is mainly about the recognition of *needs*. The attentive standard concerns the client's *situation, ends and vulnerabilities*, of which needs are only a part. Advisers' clients are, for the most part, competent adults pursuing their own projects, and what they most need from the adviser is often not that a need be met but that their ends be correctly understood. Ruth did not need Hale to meet her needs. She needed him to see what she was trying to do with her life, so that his advice could serve it. The shift from needs to ends is also, as Chapter 6 argues, part of what keeps attention from becoming paternalism.

## 2.6 The attentive standard

### 2.6.1 The definition

The discussion so far yields the following definition.

> **(D1) The attentive standard.** An adviser A meets the attentive standard toward a client C over an advisory relationship R if and only if, throughout R, (a) A allows C's particular situation, ends and vulnerabilities to register in A's deliberation, rather than assimilating them at once to a pre-formed classification (*receptivity*); (b) A forms and keeps a true picture of C's situation, ends and vulnerabilities, corrected against distortion by A's own interests and by the convenience of A's role (*accuracy*); and (c) what A thereby perceives shapes both the content of A's advice and the manner in which A gives it (*responsiveness*).

Several features of the formulation should be noted before the components are discussed. (D1) is a standard, not yet a duty. It says what it is to attend, not who owes attention to whom or why. The argument that advisers owe it, which issues in (D3) in Chapter 5, depends on the grounds developed in Chapters 3 to 5. (D1) is also relational, holding between a particular adviser and a particular client, and it holds over a relationship, not at an instant. Finally, its object has three parts, situation, ends and vulnerabilities, each of which can be the site of a distinctive failure.

By *situation* I mean the client's circumstances insofar as they bear on the matter of the advice: Ruth's finances, her widowhood, her son's plans. By *ends* I mean what the client is trying to achieve, both in the narrow domain of the advice and in the wider life it serves: her hope of helping her son buy a home, her wish to keep some flexibility. By *vulnerabilities* I mean the ways in which the client is exposed to harm, including harm from the advice itself and from the advisory relationship: her grief, her inexperience with money, her reliance on Hale's judgement. An adviser can see the situation correctly while misreading the ends, as when a physician correctly diagnoses a condition but assumes the patient wants the most aggressive treatment. Or an adviser can grasp both while missing a vulnerability, as when a supervisor understands a student's project and goals but does not see that the student is in no state to take criticism in the form in which it is being given.

### 2.6.2 Receptivity

Receptivity is the Weilian moment of the standard, and the care tradition's engrossment is its nearest relative. It is the adviser's openness to the client's situation as the client presents it, including the parts that do not fit the adviser's categories. Its characteristic failure is premature classification: the client's words are heard as answers to questions the adviser has already framed, and whatever does not answer those questions is discarded as noise.

Receptivity is not credulity. A receptive adviser does not take everything the client says at face value, and the client's own account of her situation may be partial, confused or self-deceived. What receptivity requires is that the client's account be allowed to *register*, to count as evidence the adviser must deal with, before the adviser decides what it means. Ruth's repeated remark that she did not want to have to think about it should have registered with Hale as a fact about her state that might bear on the advice, whether or not he concluded in the end that it did. Instead, it registered only as an attitude to risk, because his instruments had no other place for it. And a receptive adviser would have left room for what she did not say, such as her hopes for her son, which no question on the fact-find invited.

Receptivity also has a temporal dimension. It cannot be performed once, at the intake interview, and then set aside. Clients reveal themselves over time, often indirectly, through their reactions to advice, through what they come back to and through what they avoid. An adviser who was receptive at the first meeting but who thereafter hears every remark through the classification formed then has ceased to be receptive. This is one reason why the standard is defined over the relationship as a whole.

### 2.6.3 Accuracy

Accuracy is the Murdochian heart of the standard. It requires that the adviser's picture of the client be true, and it identifies two sources of distortion against which the adviser must guard.

The first is distortion by the adviser's own interests. This includes the obvious cases of financial self-interest, the adviser who sees the client as someone who needs the product that pays the best commission, but also subtler ones: the adviser's wish to be done quickly, to avoid a difficult conversation, to be confirmed in a favoured view, or to be liked. Each of these can shape perception without the adviser's being aware of it. The second is distortion by role convenience, discussed in section 2.2.3. The categories and procedures of the role make some readings of the client easy and others hard, and an adviser who relies on them uncritically will see the client as the role finds it convenient to see her.

Accuracy is a success term. The adviser meets it only if the picture she forms is in fact true, or near enough for the purposes of the advice. But it would be unreasonable to require infallibility, and the standard is therefore best read as requiring that the adviser's picture be as accurate as a diligent and self-critical adviser in her position could make it. Diligence includes asking the questions whose answers would correct a misreading. Self-criticism includes noticing when a reading is suspiciously convenient. An adviser who forms a false picture despite exercising both has not failed the standard in the sense that matters for culpability, though the client may still have been let down.[^d7]

### 2.6.4 Responsiveness

Responsiveness requires that what the adviser perceives make a difference to what the adviser does. It has two aspects. The first concerns the *content* of the advice: the recommendation must reflect the client's situation, ends and vulnerabilities as the adviser has perceived them. The second concerns the *manner* of the advice: the way it is given, its timing, its framing, the language used, how much is said at once and how far the client's own deliberation is invited, must reflect the same perception.

The second aspect is easy to overlook, but it matters. A physician who correctly perceives that a patient is frightened, and who adjusts the treatment recommendation accordingly but delivers it in the same brisk technical register she uses with every patient, has been responsive in content but not in manner. A supervisor who sees that a student's confidence has collapsed, and who changes the substance of her feedback but delivers it with the same unrelieved list of defects, has done the same. In each case the client may fail to take in or act on advice that is, in content, exactly right. Manner is therefore not decoration. It is part of what determines whether advice serves the client.

Responsiveness does not require that the adviser give the client what the client wants. An adviser who perceives accurately that a client's stated goal is unrealistic should say so, and saying so may be the most responsive thing she can do. What responsiveness rules out is advice whose content and manner would have been exactly the same whatever the adviser had perceived. Hale's recommendation fails this test: given her profile, it would have been the same whatever Ruth had told him about her grief or her hopes for her son.

### 2.6.5 The unity of the standard

The three components are distinct, but they are not independent. Receptivity without accuracy leaves the adviser with a mass of unprocessed impressions, or with whatever reading of them the client happens to offer. Accuracy without receptivity is impossible, since one cannot see truly what one has not let in. Receptivity and accuracy without responsiveness are idle, a private moral achievement of the Murdochian kind but not yet the adviser's attention to the client. The attentive standard is met only when all three are present, and a failure in any one is a failure of the whole.

This unity has a practical consequence. It means that attention cannot be secured by securing any one component in isolation. An institution that requires advisers to record everything clients say may improve the conditions for receptivity, but it does nothing for accuracy if the records are read through the same templates, and nothing for responsiveness if the recommendation engine ignores them. An institution that trains advisers in "communication skills" may improve the manner of advice without touching its content, and so produce advisers who are responsive in tone to clients they have not accurately perceived. The second of these is in some ways worse than plain inattention, because it gives the client the experience of being attended to without the substance.[^d8] Chapter 7 returns to these institutional failures under the heading of attention debt.

## 2.7 Virtue, skill or duty?

Attention as (D1) describes it has the marks of a virtue. It is a stable disposition of perception and response, it is acquired by practice, it is exercised in particular situations whose demands cannot be fully stated in advance, and it makes its possessor good at something that matters. It also has the marks of a skill: it can be taught, at least in part, it improves with experience, and there are better and worse techniques for exercising it. Can it also be the content of a duty?

The worry was raised briefly in Chapter 1. Duties, it is said, must be within the agent's control, and perception is not. I cannot decide to see a client accurately any more than I can decide to find a joke funny. If attention is a matter of how things strike me, then it is not something I can be required to give.

The reply has three parts. First, the premise that perception is outside the agent's control is false in the relevant sense. Murdoch's M does not decide to see D differently and then find that she does. She *works* at seeing D differently, by questioning her first reactions, looking again, and resisting the pull of a familiar resentment. Each of these is a voluntary activity, and their cumulative effect is a change in perception. The attentive standard can be met only by an adviser who performs such activities: asking further questions, pausing before classifying, checking a reading against what the client has said, noticing when a reading is suspiciously convenient and returning to the client's situation over time. A duty to meet the attentive standard is, in the first instance, a duty to perform these activities, which are within the adviser's control.

Second, the duty is not only a duty to perform the activities but also a duty to achieve their point, which is accurate perception. Here control is partial. But many duties have this structure. A duty of care in negligence requires one to take reasonable steps, and one can fail it by failing to take them. It is also breached when the steps one takes fall short of what a reasonable person would have done, even if one tried one's best. The relevant standard is objective: it asks what a diligent and self-critical adviser in the circumstances would have perceived, not what this adviser happened to perceive. An adviser who, through lack of training or experience, cannot meet that standard is not thereby excused. She has failed to acquire a competence her role requires, as a surgeon who lacks a basic surgical skill has.

Third, and most important, the duty need not exclude the virtue. Some philosophers think duties and virtues belong to different theoretical frameworks, and that a theory must choose between them. I do not think the choice is forced here. The attentive adviser has a virtue. The adviser who owes attention is under a duty. The duty is a duty to act as the virtuous adviser would, which for the non-virtuous adviser means doing deliberately what the virtuous adviser does by second nature. This is no stranger than the duty to be honest, which the honest person discharges without effort and the dishonest person must work at. The virtue is the ideal, and the duty is what the ideal requires of anyone in the role.[^d9]

## 2.8 Template advice

### 2.8.1 The vice opposed to attention

If attention is a virtue, it has an opposing vice. I call it template advice. Advice is template advice when its content is fixed by a pre-given classification of the client, in such a way that the classification takes the place of perception instead of informing it.

The definition is meant to be precise about what is wrong. Template advice is not wrong because it uses classifications. Every adviser classifies, and expertise largely consists in knowing which classifications matter. A physician who did not classify symptoms would be unable to diagnose. A lawyer who did not classify the client's problem by area of law would be unable to advise. Template advice is wrong because, and when, the classification is allowed to *settle* what the adviser perceives, so that whatever falls outside it does not register, is not checked and does not shape the advice. The difference is between using a template as a lens and using it as a blindfold.

Template advice has three typical forms, corresponding to failures of the three components. In *receptive* template advice, the client's situation is never allowed to register beyond the categories the template provides. This was Hale's failure with Ruth's hopes for her son, which the fact-find gave no occasion to voice. In *accurate-seeming* template advice, the client's situation registers but is interpreted through the template, so that a feature that should prompt a question is read as confirming the classification. This was his failure with Ruth's wish not to have to think about it. In *unresponsive* template advice, the adviser perceives that the client does not fit the template but gives the template's advice anyway, perhaps because institutional pressure or liability concerns make departure from it costly. Hale may have committed this third failure as well: it is possible that he sensed Ruth's uncertainty and recommended the annuity regardless, because it was the safe recommendation for a client with her profile.

### 2.8.2 Why template advice is tempting

Template advice is not usually the product of laziness or ill will. It is tempting for good reasons, and the attentive standard must be defended against them.

First, templates are *reliable*. A risk profiler applied consistently will rarely produce advice that is wildly unsuitable, and it protects clients from advisers whose idiosyncratic judgement is poor. Second, templates are *fair*. They treat like cases alike, and they make it harder for an adviser's prejudices about particular clients to affect the advice. Third, templates are *auditable*. An institution can check that an adviser followed the profiler, and it cannot easily check whether the adviser attended to the client. Fourth, templates are *efficient*. They allow an adviser to serve many clients in limited time.

Each of these is a real good, and I do not argue that templates should be abandoned. The argument is that each good is bought at a cost that the template itself conceals. Reliability is reliability in the average case, and it is bought at the expense of the client whose situation is not average, who is exactly the client most in need of attention. Fairness that treats like cases alike depends on correctly identifying which cases are alike, and a template that ignores relevant differences treats unlike cases alike, which is itself a form of unfairness. Auditability rewards whatever can be audited, and so systematically disfavours attention, which mostly cannot. Efficiency, as Chapter 6 discusses under the heading of demandingness, is a legitimate constraint on how much attention can be owed, but it is not a reason to give none.[^d10]

### 2.8.3 Template advice and the experience of the client

Template advice has a characteristic effect on the client, which deserves a brief mention because it bears on the arguments of Chapters 5 and 6. A client who receives template advice may not know that she has, but she often senses it. She feels that she was not listened to, that the adviser had made up his mind, that her questions were answered but not heard. This experience is not merely unpleasant. It undermines the trust on which the advisory relationship depends, and it may lead the client either to disengage from the advice or to defer to it without understanding. Both responses diminish the client's agency. The first leaves her without the benefit of expertise. The second leaves her subject to it without the capacity to evaluate it. Attention, by contrast, tends to support the client's own deliberation, because the client who has been accurately seen is in a better position to recognise whether the advice fits her life. This is one reason, developed in Chapter 6, why attention is not the enemy of autonomy but one of its conditions.

## 2.9 Three objections

### 2.9.1 The objection from vagueness

The first objection is that the attentive standard is too vague to guide action or to ground a duty. Words like "accurate", "receptive" and "responsive" have no precise application, and two advisers might disagree about whether a given piece of advice met the standard.

I accept that the standard is not precise, but deny that this is a defect. Many important moral and legal standards share this feature: reasonableness in negligence, good faith in contract, the best interests of the child in family law. Such standards are open-textured because the situations they govern are too varied to be captured by precise rules, and precise rules applied to such situations would tend to produce the very template behaviour that the attentive standard opposes. The standard does not tell the adviser what to do. It tells the adviser how to look, and leaves the doing to judgement. It is no objection to a standard of perception that it requires judgement to apply. The more serious version of the objection, that a vague standard cannot be enforced, is the unenforceability objection, answered in Chapter 7.

### 2.9.2 The objection from origins

The second objection is that the concept of attention I have drawn from Murdoch and Weil is too bound up with their metaphysical and religious commitments to be transplanted into professional ethics. Weil's attention is a form of prayer, and Murdoch's is directed by a Platonic conception of the Good. Stripped of these, the objection goes, attention is either empty or merely a fancy name for good listening.

My reply is that the transplant takes what is phenomenologically and ethically distinctive about their accounts without their metaphysics. From Weil it takes the receptive suspension of one's own categories. From Murdoch it takes the idea that perception can be more or less just, that it is distorted by the self and that moral effort can correct it. Neither of these ideas depends on Weil's theology or Murdoch's Platonism, though each is given a particular depth by them. And the result is not merely good listening. Good listening is part of receptivity, but the attentive standard also requires accuracy, which can demand that the adviser *not* simply accept what the client says, and responsiveness, which requires that the adviser act on what she has seen. The care-ethical sources, which have no comparable metaphysical commitments, independently support the same structure.

### 2.9.3 The objection from the client's authority

The third objection comes from the other direction. It holds that the accuracy component gives the adviser an authority over the client's self-understanding that she should not have. If the adviser is required to form a "true picture" of the client's ends, and that picture may differ from the client's own account, then the adviser is being licensed to tell clients what they really want.

This is the paternalism objection, and Chapter 6 is devoted to answering it. Here I note only the shape of the reply. The accuracy component requires the adviser to see the client's ends truly, but the client's own understanding of her ends is among the most important facts about them, and an adviser who disregarded it would not be seeing them accurately. Where the adviser's perception and the client's self-understanding diverge, accuracy requires the adviser to take the divergence seriously as a fact, which usually means raising it with the client, not acting on it unilaterally. The anti-paternalism constraint (D4) makes this explicit: what the adviser perceives may shape her advice only in ways that enlarge the client's deliberation about her own ends and leave the client's judgement with the client. Accuracy, properly understood, is a check on the adviser as much as on the client.

## 2.10 Conclusion

This chapter has developed the concept of attention that the rest of the dissertation relies on. From Murdoch it took the ideas that moral life goes on in how we see, that perception can be more or less just, and that the self and its conveniences distort it. From Weil it took the receptive suspension of one's own categories that allows another person's situation to register. From the care tradition, in Gilligan, Noddings and Held, it took the location of attention within relationships of dependency, the link between perception and motivation, and the claim that care's standards extend beyond intimate life. From Tronto it took the place of attentiveness as the first and conditioning phase of care, and its status as a moral element whose absence is a moral failing. The result is the attentive standard (D1), with its three components of receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness, and the vice opposed to it, template advice.

What the chapter has not yet shown is that advisers *owe* attention. (D1) says what attention is. It does not say why a financial adviser, as opposed to a friend or a stranger, should be held to it. The argument for that conclusion has two grounds, as Chapter 1 announced: the fiduciary structure of discretionary power over the client's critical resources, and the care-ethical structure of asymmetric dependency. Chapter 3 takes up the first. It asks whether the fiduciary duties of loyalty and care already contain the attentive standard, and argues that they presuppose it without containing it.

# Chapter 5: Trust, Vulnerability and Dependency

## 5.1 Introduction: from structure to obligation

The previous three chapters established two things about the professional adviser. Chapter 2 gave content to the idea of attention, defining the attentive standard (D1) through its three components of receptivity, accuracy and responsiveness. Chapters 3 and 4 then located the adviser within two structures that are often thought to exhaust her obligations. Chapter 3 argued that the fiduciary relationship, understood as discretionary power over another's critical resources (D2), presupposes attention without containing it: the duties of loyalty and care tell the adviser whose interests to serve and with what diligence, but they are silent about whether she has seen those interests at all. Chapter 4 argued that the adviser's role is bounded but personal: she is not her client's friend, and the partiality of friendship is not available to her, yet the role she occupies is one whose point is the particular person in front of her rather than a type.

What those chapters did not do is show that attention is *owed*. A sceptic can grant everything so far and still hold that attention is a professional excellence rather than a professional obligation. On this view the attentive adviser is admirable, like the surgeon with unusually good bedside manner or the barrister whose closing speeches are works of art, but the adviser who gives competent, loyal, template advice has done all that she was bound to do. Attention, on this picture, is supererogatory: good to give, praiseworthy when given, but not something whose absence wrongs the client.

This chapter answers the sceptic. Its claim is that the asymmetry between adviser and client, properly analysed, generates a directed duty of attention: a duty owed *to* the client, whose breach wrongs her and not merely falls short of an ideal. I build the argument from three bodies of work that have rarely been brought together with fiduciary theory. The first is the philosophy of trust, in particular Annette Baier's account of trust as accepted vulnerability {{baier1986}}, Karen Jones's account of trust as an affective attitude {{jones1996}}, and Onora O'Neill's diagnosis in *A Question of Trust* of what institutions do to trustworthiness {{oneill2002trust}}. The second is the philosophy of vulnerability, from Robert Goodin's argument that special responsibilities are grounded in others' vulnerability to our choices {{goodin1985}} to the taxonomy of inherent, situational and pathogenic vulnerability proposed by Catriona Mackenzie, Wendy Rogers and Susan Dodds {{mrd2014}}. The third is Eva Feder Kittay's account of dependency and the workers who meet it {{kittay2020}}.

The chapter proceeds as follows. Section 5.2 examines trust and argues that what a client entrusts to an adviser includes, ineliminably, her situation as something to be perceived. Section 5.3 turns to vulnerability and identifies a specifically epistemic vulnerability that advisory relationships create: the vulnerability of being misperceived by someone whose perception one cannot monitor. Section 5.4 uses Kittay's work to show why the dependency in question is not a failure of the client's autonomy but a normal condition of a life lived among specialists, and to prepare the institutional argument of Chapter 7. Section 5.5 assembles the argument and introduces the third of the dissertation's definitions, (D3) owed attention. Section 5.6 answers four objections, and Section 5.7 hands on to Chapter 6, where the duty so grounded must be shown not to license paternalism.

A word on method. As in the preceding chapters, I proceed by reflective equilibrium between considered judgements about cases and general principles, drawing on legal theory where it sharpens the concepts. The central considered judgement here is one that the vignette of Chapter 1 was designed to elicit: that the widow who was advised into an annuity by an adviser who followed every rule was *wronged*, and not merely served less well than she might have been. If that judgement is sound, a theory that makes attention supererogatory is mistaken, and the task is to say what makes it a duty. If the judgement is unsound, the arguments of this chapter should expose why.[^d1]

## 5.2 Trust and what is entrusted

### 5.2.1 Baier: trust as accepted vulnerability

Baier's 'Trust and Antitrust' begins from a complaint about moral philosophy itself. The tradition, she argues, has been preoccupied with relations between equals who make voluntary agreements, and has consequently neglected trust, which pervades moral life and is especially important between unequals {{baier1986}}. Her positive analysis treats trust as a three-place relation: one party trusts another with something she cares about, and in doing so accepts a vulnerability to the other's possible, though not expected, ill will toward that good. To trust is to let another have a kind of discretionary access to what one values, and to rely on her not to abuse it.

Three features of the analysis matter for advisers. The first is that trust is relative to a good entrusted. We do not simply trust people; we trust them with things, and the scope of the trust is fixed by what has been handed over. This aligns closely with the fiduciary structure set out in Chapter 3. What Gordon Smith calls a critical resource {{smith2002}}, and what Tamar Frankel's foundational article describes as the property or interests over which the entrustor confers power {{frankel1983}}, are precisely the goods with respect to which a client accepts vulnerability. The legal and the moral analyses converge on the same object.

The second feature is that Baier's analysis is built for asymmetry. Contractual models assume parties who can monitor one another, specify terms in advance and enforce them; trust is what we fall back on when these conditions fail, and they fail characteristically between parties of unequal knowledge, power or capacity. A client who seeks advice does so because she lacks something the adviser has. She therefore cannot, in the nature of the case, fully specify what she wants done or check that it has been done well. Her reliance is not a contract with gaps that could in principle be filled; it is reliance on the other's discretion, and discretion is exactly what contracts leave unspecified.

The third feature is Baier's test for the moral decency of a trust relationship: whether the relationship could survive each party's knowledge of what the other relies on to sustain it {{baier1986}}. A relationship in which one party's continued trust depends on not knowing that the other is exploiting it, or on not knowing what the other is counting on to keep it going, fails the test. I shall call this the *expressibility test*, and it will do a good deal of work in this chapter and the next.[^d2]

Apply the test to the vignette of Chapter 1, filled out in one way consistent with it. The widow relied on her adviser to tell her what to do with the proceeds of her husband's pension. The adviser, for his part, relied on her not knowing that he had fitted her to the profile his firm's compliance software associated with 'recently bereaved, risk-averse, over sixty', and that he had not asked about her plans to help her grandson through university, her uncertain health, or her late husband's wish that some capital be kept liquid for the family. Suppose each party came to know what the other relied on. She would learn that he had not looked; he would learn that she had assumed he had. It is hard to believe the relationship would survive the disclosure in its existing form. The trust was sustained by an unexpressed asymmetry: her belief that she was being seen, his reliance on her not discovering that she was not. By Baier's test, the relationship was morally indecent even though no rule was broken.

This is the first clue to why attention is owed. What the widow entrusted was not only her money. She entrusted her *situation*, as something to be understood, to a person she took to be in the business of understanding such situations. That is why discovering template advice feels like a betrayal of trust rather than the discovery of a below-average service. One does not betray someone by being merely mediocre on their behalf; one betrays them by treating as unimportant what they had handed over as important.

### 5.2.2 Jones: being counted on

Baier's analysis tells us what is entrusted. Karen Jones's account of trust as an affective attitude tells us what the trusting party expects of the trusted. Trust, Jones argues, is not primarily a belief about another's reliability but an attitude of optimism about her goodwill and competence in the domain of interaction, combined with the expectation that she will be directly and favourably moved by the thought that we are counting on her {{jones1996}}. The account explains, among other things, why trust can resist contrary evidence, and why its betrayal provokes resentment rather than mere disappointment.

Two elements of this account are significant here. The first is that the optimism is about *competence* as well as goodwill. A client who trusts an adviser is optimistic that the adviser can do the thing in question. But the competence that advising requires is not only technical; Chapter 2 argued that the epistemic task of advice includes forming an accurate picture of the particular client's situation. A financial adviser who can calculate the yield of every product on the market but cannot tell which of them fits this client lacks the competence that the client is optimistic about. Competence in advising is, in part, competence in attention.

The second element is the more distinctive one. On Jones's view, the trusting party expects the trusted party to be moved by the fact that she is counting on him. This is a demand for a certain kind of uptake. It is not enough that the trusted party behaves in the way the trusting party hoped; he must do so, at least in part, *because* he registers her reliance. Now, to be moved by the thought that a particular person is counting on you requires that you register her as that particular person. Someone who processes a client as an instance of a type is not moved by the fact that *she* is counting on him; at most he is moved by a policy about what persons of that type are owed. The uptake that Jones's account makes constitutive of the trusted party's side of trust is therefore already a form of receptivity in the sense of (D1). It is, at minimum, the registering of this person's reliance as hers.

Jones's account also gives us a diagnostic for directedness. She holds that betrayal of trust characteristically provokes resentment rather than mere disappointment {{jones1996}}. Resentment, unlike disappointment, is a reactive attitude that presupposes that one was wronged, that something was owed to one and withheld. If clients who discover template advice respond with resentment, and if that response is apt rather than confused, then the thing withheld was owed to them. I return to this diagnostic in Section 5.5, where it serves as evidence for the directedness of the duty.[^d3]

### 5.2.3 O'Neill: trustworthiness and the audit society

Baier and Jones analyse trust between individuals. Professional advice, however, takes place within institutions, and the trust a client places in an adviser is partly placed in the institution that credentialed, employs and regulates her. O'Neill's 2002 Reith Lectures, published as *A Question of Trust*, are directly concerned with this institutional layer {{oneill2002trust}}. O'Neill questions the widespread claim that there is a crisis of trust, noting that people continue to rely on the very institutions they say they distrust. Her more pointed argument is that the culture of audit and accountability adopted to restore trust often distorts professional practice and can undermine the trustworthiness it was meant to secure. What is needed, she argues, is intelligent accountability, and forms of communication that let people assess trustworthiness rather than merely report on compliance.

This argument bears directly on the problem of template advice. The adviser in the vignette of Chapter 1 followed every rule. His firm's compliance regime recorded that a fact-find had been completed, that risk tolerance had been assessed by questionnaire, that the recommended product fell within the risk band so assessed, and that the required disclosures had been made. Every box was ticked. What the regime could not record, and therefore did not reward, was whether the adviser had *seen* the widow. O'Neill's analysis suggests that this is not an accidental gap but a structural tendency: accountability regimes measure what is measurable, practitioners rationally attend to what is measured, and what is measured in advisory practice is overwhelmingly procedural. The result is a form of practice that is accountable in the auditor's sense while being inattentive in the sense that matters to the client.

Two consequences follow for the argument of this chapter. First, the client's trust cannot be grounded in the adviser's compliance alone, since compliance is compatible with exactly the failure the client most fears. What the client needs is for the adviser to be *trustworthy*, and trustworthiness in an adviser includes being the kind of person whose perception of one's situation can be relied upon. Second, and looking ahead to Chapter 7, the very institutions that police advisers may be among the causes of inattention. If so, the duty of attention cannot be borne by advisers alone, and its systematic violation is partly an institutional wrong. I develop this point under the heading of *attention debt* in Chapter 7; here I note only that O'Neill's diagnosis gives it its first foothold.[^d4]

### 5.2.4 What trust adds to the fiduciary analysis

It is worth pausing to say exactly what the philosophy of trust contributes that the fiduciary analysis of Chapter 3 did not. Fiduciary theory, in the versions examined there, explains why a person who holds discretionary power over another's critical resources must not use it for herself and must use it with diligence. Frankel locates the justification in entrustment and the resulting risk to the entrustor {{frankel2011}}; Smith in the need to combat opportunism where discretion over critical resources is held {{smith2002}}; Paul Miller in the normatively significant features of a relationship of discretionary power over another's practical interests {{miller2011}}. All three accounts are, in a broad sense, accounts of a hazard and its control. They tell us what the fiduciary must not do with the power she holds.

Trust, as Baier and Jones analyse it, adds a positive and relational dimension that these hazard-control accounts leave implicit. The trusting party does not merely hope that the trusted party will refrain from abuse. She hands something over in the expectation of being met: that the other will take up what she has entrusted as hers, and be moved by her reliance. This is why, as Chapter 3 argued, a fiduciary can satisfy loyalty and care while failing the client. Loyalty answers the question of whose interests; care answers the question of how diligently. Neither answers the question of whether the fiduciary has formed a true picture of what those interests are in this case. Trust answers it, or rather presupposes an answer to it: the client trusts the adviser to see.

I therefore draw the following interim conclusion. In advisory relationships, what the client entrusts includes her situation as an object of perception. The adviser's discretion over critical resources is exercised *through* her perception of that situation; there is no way to exercise it except on the basis of some picture of the client, accurate or not. To trust an adviser with discretion is therefore to trust her with the picture. This is the first premise of the argument assembled in Section 5.5.

## 5.3 Vulnerability

### 5.3.1 Goodin's vulnerability model

Trust tells us what the client hands over. It does not by itself tell us why the adviser is bound to receive it well. One might think the answer is consent: the adviser agreed to advise, and attention is part of what she agreed to. But the consent answer is weak in exactly the place we need strength. Most advisory engagements do not specify attention as a term, and an adviser who protests that she agreed only to recommend a suitable product and has done so is not obviously misdescribing the agreement. If attention is owed, it must be owed in virtue of something other than what the parties explicitly agreed.

Robert Goodin's *Protecting the Vulnerable* offers such a ground {{goodin1985}}. Goodin argues that our special responsibilities, to family, friends, clients and others, are not grounded in voluntary commitment, as the standard view has it, but in the vulnerability of others to our actions and choices. Voluntary undertakings matter, on his view, because and insofar as they create or shape such vulnerability: when I promise, you come to rely on me, and your reliance makes you vulnerable to my performance. The deeper principle is that we are bound to protect those whose interests are especially vulnerable to what we do. Because that principle reaches beyond those to whom we have committed ourselves, Goodin uses it to justify wider social obligations as well.

The vulnerability model has an obvious appeal for the present inquiry. Clients are among the examples Goodin gives of persons to whom special responsibilities are owed, and the model explains why such responsibilities persist even where the explicit terms of engagement are thin. If a client's interests are vulnerable to her adviser's choices, and the adviser's choices are made on the basis of her perception of the client, then the client's interests are vulnerable to the adviser's perception. Goodin's principle then implies that the adviser has a special responsibility to protect the client against the hazards of that perception, which is to say, to perceive well.

I accept the core of the vulnerability model but not its claim to be the whole story. Goodin's principle, taken alone, generates responsibilities whose strength is proportional to the degree of vulnerability and to the agent's capacity to affect it. It does not, by itself, distinguish between a responsibility that is owed to a particular person and one that is merely a responsibility *regarding* her. A passer-by who could rescue a drowning child has a responsibility grounded in the child's vulnerability, but it is contested whether that responsibility is directed to the child in the way that a lifeguard's is. The difference, intuitively, is that the lifeguard has taken up a position whose point is the protection of swimmers, and swimmers enter the water partly on the strength of that position. The adviser is like the lifeguard. To capture what makes her responsibility directed, we must combine Goodin's vulnerability with Baier's entrustment: the vulnerability is one that the client accepted *because* the adviser held herself out as one to whom it could safely be accepted. Section 5.5 makes this combination explicit.[^d5]

### 5.3.2 Three sources of vulnerability

Goodin speaks of vulnerability as a single relation between an agent's choices and another's interests. Mackenzie, Rogers and Dodds argue that this is too coarse. Their editors' introduction to *Vulnerability: New Essays in Ethics and Feminist Philosophy* distinguishes inherent vulnerability, which arises from our embodiment and our dependence on others; situational vulnerability, which arises from particular personal, social, economic or environmental circumstances; and pathogenic vulnerability, a subset of situational vulnerability that arises from morally dysfunctional relationships or institutions, including responses to vulnerability that are themselves paternalistic {{mrd2014}}. The essays in the volume ask what vulnerability is, what is owed to the vulnerable and by whom, and how responses to vulnerability can respect autonomy.

The taxonomy maps onto advisory relationships with some precision. Every client is inherently vulnerable in the minimal sense that she is a finite, embodied being whose life goes better or worse depending on decisions she cannot make alone. Most clients, at the moment they seek advice, are also situationally vulnerable: the widow is recently bereaved and facing a decision she has never had to make; the patient who visits a general practitioner is frightened and unwell; the doctoral student whose work has stalled is isolated and anxious about her future. Situational vulnerability is not a defect in the client. It is often the very occasion of advice, which is typically sought when ordinary competences are outstripped by circumstance.

Pathogenic vulnerability is the category that most illuminates template advice. Mackenzie, Rogers and Dodds define it to include vulnerability produced by the institutions and relationships meant to respond to vulnerability {{mrd2014}}. An advisory relationship that processes clients through templates does more than fail to reduce their situational vulnerability. It adds a new one: the client becomes vulnerable to a decision made on the basis of a picture of her that is not of her. The widow's annuity, purchased on the basis of a risk profile that omitted everything particular about her, created a vulnerability (illiquidity at a time when she needed flexibility) that she would not have faced without the advice. The relationship designed to protect her became a source of harm. Template advice is, in this precise sense, pathogenic.

The point of this taxonomy for the argument is that it lets us identify the specific vulnerability against which attention protects. It is not inherent vulnerability, which no adviser can remove. Nor is it simply the situational vulnerability that brought the client to the adviser, which competent advice of any kind may reduce. It is the vulnerability created by the advisory relationship itself: the vulnerability of having one's situation represented, for decision-making purposes, by another person.

### 5.3.3 Epistemic vulnerability: being misperceived

Let me give this vulnerability a name. Call it *epistemic vulnerability* in the advisory sense: the vulnerability of a person whose interests will be affected by decisions made on the basis of another's perception of her, where she cannot readily monitor or correct that perception.

Three features distinguish epistemic vulnerability from the more familiar vulnerability to abuse of power that fiduciary law addresses. The first is that it does not depend on ill will. The fiduciary hazard, as Smith describes it, is opportunism {{smith2002}}; the trust hazard, as Baier describes it, is the other's possible ill will {{baier1986}}. Epistemic vulnerability, by contrast, can be realised by an adviser of perfect goodwill. The adviser in the vignette did not wish the widow harm and did not profit at her expense beyond his ordinary commission. He simply did not see her. The hazard is not that the adviser will turn the power against the client, but that the adviser will exercise it, in perfect loyalty, on behalf of someone who is not quite there.

The second feature is that epistemic vulnerability is hard to detect, even after the fact. A client can often tell whether her adviser has taken a secret profit or been negligent in executing an instruction, because these failures leave traces in outcomes and records. She can rarely tell whether her adviser saw her accurately, because the adviser's perception is not itself recorded and its effects are mixed with the effects of luck and of the client's own choices. The widow might never have discovered that her plans for her grandson had not registered; she might simply have found, years later, that there was less available than she had hoped, and blamed the markets.

The third feature is that the client's ordinary means of self-protection are largely unavailable. Against opportunism, a client can seek disclosure, demand reasons, or take a second opinion. These protections presuppose that she knows what to ask about. Against misperception she is in a worse position, because what has been missed is, by hypothesis, something the adviser failed to register, and the client typically does not know that it bears on the advice. She lacks the expertise to know which features of her situation are relevant, which is why she sought advice in the first place. The asymmetry of knowledge that makes advice valuable is the same asymmetry that makes misperception dangerous.

These three features together explain why attention cannot be left to the client to secure. In most relationships of mutual reliance, each party can protect herself against the other's inattention by drawing attention to what matters. In advisory relationships, the client is not in a position to know what matters, and so cannot reliably draw attention to it. The burden falls on the adviser, not because she is morally superior, but because she alone is positioned to discharge it.[^d6]

## 5.4 Dependency and dependency work

### 5.4.1 Kittay against the fiction of independence

Talk of clients as vulnerable and dependent invites a worry that will occupy Chapter 6 at length: that it casts clients as less than fully autonomous, and so prepares the ground for paternalism. Before turning to that worry, it is important to see why dependency is not, in itself, a deficit in the dependent party. Kittay's *Love's Labor* is the most sustained philosophical argument for this view.

Kittay argues that liberal conceptions of equality, modelled on independent and equally situated citizens, ignore the inevitable dependency of human life and the dependency workers who meet it {{kittay2020}}. Everyone is dependent at the beginning of life, most are dependent at its end, and many are dependent in between through illness or disability. A theory of justice that treats independence as the normal condition and dependency as an exception has, she argues, mistaken a phase of life for its whole. Because those who care for dependants become vulnerable themselves, often economically, Kittay argues that justice requires a principle of social responsibility for care, which she calls *doulia*, that supports those who care for others. Her argument draws on her own experience of caring for her severely disabled daughter.

Professional advice is not dependency work in Kittay's central sense. Her paradigm is the care of persons who cannot meet their own basic needs, and most clients of advisers are, in that sense, fully capable adults. Yet the structural point transfers. The model of the client as a sovereign consumer, who knows her own ends, purchases expertise to serve them, and needs from the adviser only information and execution, is a version of the fiction Kittay criticises. Under conditions of specialised knowledge, everyone depends on others for judgements they cannot make for themselves: about their health, their legal position, their finances, their intellectual work. This dependency is not a failure of autonomy any more than reliance on a bridge engineer is a failure of autonomy. It is a normal condition of agency in a complex society, and Frankel's observation that fiduciary relationships grow in importance in a society of specialised services makes the same point from the legal side {{frankel1983}}.

If dependency on advisers is normal, then the attention that dependency calls for is not a special concession to the weak. It is part of what makes the normal form of modern agency workable. An autonomous person who relies on an adviser does so as an exercise of her autonomy, and the reliance is reasonable only if the adviser will meet her where she is. Attention, so understood, is a condition of the client's autonomy rather than a threat to it. Chapter 6 develops this claim with the help of the literature on relational autonomy.

### 5.4.2 The adviser's own dependency

Kittay's second insight concerns the dependency worker. Those who care for dependants become vulnerable themselves, because the work absorbs time, energy and resources and because the worker's own interests are subordinated to the charge's needs {{kittay2020}}. This is why, on her view, justice must reach the conditions under which care is given, and not merely the relationship between carer and cared-for.

The adviser is not a dependency worker in Kittay's sense, but she occupies a structurally analogous position in one respect: her capacity to give attention depends on conditions she does not control. Attention takes time. It takes a caseload small enough that each client can be held in mind. It takes an institutional culture in which the adviser is rewarded, or at least not penalised, for asking questions whose answers do not fit the form. Where these conditions are absent, the adviser who tries to attend is placed in a position resembling that of Kittay's dependency worker: she bears the cost of meeting another's needs while the institution captures the benefit of her not doing so. Joan Tronto's account of care as involving responsibility and competence as well as attentiveness {{tronto1993}} already suggested that attention is only one phase of a larger practice that requires resources; Kittay's argument shows that the allocation of those resources is a matter of justice.

I flag this point here and develop it in Chapter 7. If the duty of attention is grounded in the client's dependency, and if the adviser's capacity to discharge it is itself dependent on institutional conditions, then a theory of owed attention must reach those conditions. An institution that structures advisory work so that attention is impossible does not merely fail its clients; it places its advisers in a position where they cannot do what they owe, and so wrongs them as well. That is the fourth limb of the central thesis, and Kittay's argument is one of its foundations.

## 5.5 From asymmetry to owed attention

### 5.5.1 The argument

The materials are now in place. I set out the argument as a sequence of premises, each of which has been defended in this or an earlier chapter.

1. *Entrustment.* In an advisory relationship, the client confers on the adviser discretionary power over one or more of her critical resources, in the sense of (D2) (Chapter 3; Section 5.2.1).
2. *Perceptual mediation.* The adviser can exercise that discretion only on the basis of some picture of the client's situation, ends and vulnerabilities; the quality of the exercise, for the client, depends on the accuracy of that picture (Chapter 2; Section 5.2.4).
3. *Epistemic vulnerability.* The client cannot readily monitor or correct the adviser's picture, because the asymmetry of knowledge that makes the advice valuable also prevents her from knowing what the picture should contain (Section 5.3.3).
4. *Vulnerability principle.* Where one party's interests are especially vulnerable to another's choices, the latter bears a special responsibility to protect those interests, proportional to the degree of the vulnerability and her capacity to affect it (Goodin; Section 5.3.1).
5. *Holding out.* The adviser occupies her role voluntarily and holds it out as a service whose point is the particular client's good, thereby inviting the client to accept the epistemic vulnerability of premise 3 on the strength of the role (Chapter 4; Section 5.2.2).
6. *Directedness.* A special responsibility grounded in a vulnerability that one has invited another to accept, on the strength of a role one holds out, is directed to that other: its breach wrongs her (Sections 5.2.1 to 5.2.2, 5.3.1).

Therefore: the adviser owes the client a directed duty to protect her against the epistemic vulnerability of the relationship, and since the only way to protect someone against being misperceived is to perceive her well, the content of that duty is attention. I state the conclusion as the third of the dissertation's definitions.

> **(D3) Owed attention.** An adviser A owes a client C attention if and because (a) C has conferred on A discretionary power over one or more of C's critical resources, in the sense of (D2); (b) the exercise of that power for C depends on A's perception of C's situation, ends and vulnerabilities, which C cannot readily monitor or correct; and (c) A holds out the role in which she exercises that power as a service directed to C's good. Where (a) to (c) hold, A is under a duty, owed to C, to meet the attentive standard (D1) in exercising that power, for the duration of the relationship and in proportion to what is at stake for C.

Several features of (D3) deserve comment. First, it is a *grounding* claim and not merely a correlation. The conditions in (a) to (c) are offered as what makes attention owed, not as symptoms that happen to accompany its being owed. This matters because it determines the duty's scope: where one of the conditions is absent or attenuated, the duty is absent or attenuated with it. An adviser who has no discretion, because the client has specified exactly what she wants and asked only for execution, has a correspondingly diminished duty of attention; so does one whose client can and does monitor her perception, as when two specialists consult one another as peers.

Second, (D3) builds proportionality into the duty. The attentive standard is not a fixed quantity of perceptual effort. What it requires depends on what is at stake for the client and on how much the exercise of discretion depends on particulars. A routine query about a standard form requires little; a decision about the disposal of a widow's only capital requires a great deal. This proportionality is the first part of my answer to the demandingness objection, which Chapter 7 takes up in full against the cases.

Third, (D3) locates the duty in the *exercise* of power, not in the adviser's inner life at large. The adviser is not required to attend to everything about the client, or to care about her in the sense of Nel Noddings's engrossment {{noddings2013}}, or to bring to the relationship the whole of the moral attention that Iris Murdoch describes as the work of a lifetime {{murdoch1970}}. She is required to attend to what bears on the discretion she holds. This is how (D3) honours the conclusion of Chapter 4 that the adviser's duty is role-bounded but personal: bounded by the domain of the power entrusted, personal in that within that domain it is this client, and not a type, who must be seen.

### 5.5.2 Why the duty is directed

The crucial step in the argument is premise 6, which converts a responsibility regarding the client into a duty owed to her. Why accept it? I offer three considerations.

The first is structural. The combination of invitation and acceptance in premise 5 is the same structure that grounds directed duties in the paradigm case of promising. When I promise you something, I invite you to rely on me, and your reliance, accepted on the strength of my invitation, is what makes my failure a wrong to you rather than merely a bad outcome. The adviser who holds out her role as a service to the client's good issues a standing invitation of this kind. She need not promise attention in so many words; the role, as publicly understood, does the inviting. Goodin is right that what ultimately matters is the vulnerability so created {{goodin1985}}, but the invitation explains why the vulnerability belongs to the relationship between these two people.

The second is the reactive diagnostic drawn from Jones in Section 5.2.2. If the client who discovers that she was advised by template responds with resentment, and if that resentment is apt, then she was wronged. I submit that resentment is apt in the vignette. The widow, learning that her adviser fitted her to a profile without asking about the things she would have told him if asked, does not merely regret that she chose an adviser who turned out to be average. She has a grievance against him. Her grievance is not that he was disloyal or negligent in the fiduciary sense; he was neither. It is that he treated her reliance on him as reliance on a procedure, when she had placed it in a person. That grievance is intelligible only if attention was owed to her.[^d7]

The third is Baier's expressibility test. A relationship in which the adviser relies on the client's not knowing that she has not been seen fails the test {{baier1986}}. A duty whose breach renders a relationship morally indecent in this way is not plausibly supererogatory. Supererogatory acts are those whose omission leaves the relationship intact, even if less admirable than it might be; the absence of attention, in an advisory relationship, corrupts the relationship itself by making it depend on a concealment.

## 5.6 Objections and replies

### 5.6.1 'Goodin proves too much'

The first objection targets premise 4. Goodin's vulnerability principle, the objector says, generates responsibilities wherever there is vulnerability to one's choices. If attention is owed because clients are vulnerable to advisers' perceptions, then attention is owed by everyone to everyone whose interests their choices affect: by the shopkeeper to her customers, the bus driver to his passengers, the voter to her fellow citizens. A principle with this reach cannot ground a distinctive professional duty.

The reply is that (D3) does not rest on premise 4 alone. Goodin's principle explains the *strength* of the duty and why it persists where explicit terms are thin; the directedness and the content come from the conjunction with premises 1, 2 and 5. The shopkeeper does not hold discretionary power over her customers' critical resources; the bus driver's exercise of his power does not depend on his perception of the particular passenger's ends; the voter does not hold out her vote as a service to others' good. Each lacks at least one of the conditions in (D3). The objector is right that vulnerability is everywhere, and Goodin himself uses the breadth of the principle to argue for wider social responsibilities {{goodin1985}}. But breadth at the level of the grounding principle is compatible with specificity at the level of the duty, once the principle is combined with the structure of a particular relationship. Indeed, this is just what Goodin's own treatment of clients as one class among others suggests.

### 5.6.2 'Many clients do not trust their advisers'

The second objection targets the role of trust. Some clients do not trust their advisers in Baier's or Jones's sense. The sophisticated investor who engages a broker while assuming the broker is self-interested, or the litigant who suspects his lawyer of padding fees, does not accept vulnerability to the adviser's goodwill or expect to be moved by being counted on. If trust is part of the ground of owed attention, such clients are owed none.

The reply is that (D3) grounds attention in the invitation to rely and in the resulting epistemic vulnerability, not in the client's psychological state of trust. The analysis of trust was used to identify what is entrusted and what the role invites, not to make the duty conditional on the client's attitude. A client who distrusts his adviser still confers discretion on her and still depends on her picture of his situation; he is, if anything, more epistemically vulnerable, because his distrust gives him no means to correct a picture he cannot see. O'Neill's argument is apposite here: what matters is not whether people trust but whether those they must rely on are trustworthy {{oneill2002trust}}. The adviser's duty tracks the reliance her role invites, and that reliance is present whether or not the client feels the optimism Jones describes.[^d8]

### 5.6.3 'The parties can contract out of attention'

The third objection holds that the scope of an advisory engagement is set by agreement. If the client and adviser agree that the adviser will recommend from a defined product range on the basis of a standard questionnaire, that is what the client has bought, and template advice is no breach.

I accept part of this. Parties can legitimately narrow the *domain* of advice: a client can ask only for a view on a single product, or for the execution of a decision already made. Where the domain is narrowed, condition (a) of (D3) is narrowed with it, and so is the duty. What the parties cannot do is retain the domain while stripping it of attention. An agreement that the adviser will recommend what is suitable for the client, on the basis of a questionnaire that the adviser knows does not capture what makes advice suitable for her, is an agreement whose terms the client would not accept if she understood them. It fails Baier's test. The objection therefore succeeds only in a form that leaves (D3) untouched: it shows that the duty's scope follows the scope of discretion, which (D3) already says.

### 5.6.4 'Attention is a virtue, not a duty'

The fourth objection is the most philosophically serious. Attention, as Chapter 2 characterised it following Murdoch {{murdoch1970}} and Simone Weil {{weil1951}}, is a perceptual achievement: a matter of how one sees, cultivated over time, partly independent of the will. Duties govern what we do, and what we do is under our control. It is a category mistake to speak of a duty to see accurately, as it would be to speak of a duty to find a joke funny.

The reply has two parts. First, the attentive standard (D1) is not a demand for a perceptual state on demand but a standard of conduct in exercising discretion. Each of its components has a behavioural face: receptivity shows in what questions are asked and what answers are allowed to unsettle the initial frame; accuracy shows in whether the picture formed is checked against the client and against the adviser's own self-serving tendencies; responsiveness shows in whether the advice and its manner change in light of what is learned. An adviser can be required to ask, to check and to revise, and these requirements are under her control. Second, insofar as attention also depends on cultivated capacities that are not under immediate control, the adviser who holds herself out as an adviser has a duty to have cultivated them, as a surgeon has a duty to have acquired steadiness of hand. The objection is right that attention cannot be summoned by fiat; it is wrong that this makes it unsuitable as the content of a role-based duty. The neighbouring objection, that law cannot require a perceptual virtue, concerns enforceability rather than existence, and Chapter 7 addresses it directly.[^d9]

## 5.7 Conclusion

This chapter has argued that attention is owed. The client of a professional adviser entrusts not only resources but her situation as something to be perceived; she is, in consequence, epistemically vulnerable in a way she cannot herself correct; and the adviser, by holding out her role as a service to the client's good, invites her to accept that vulnerability. From this structure, (D3) follows: the adviser owes the client attention, to the standard of (D1), in the exercise of the discretion she holds. The duty is directed, as the aptness of the client's resentment and the failure of template relationships under Baier's expressibility test both indicate; and it is proportional, scoped to the discretion entrusted and the stakes involved. Kittay's work showed that the dependency underlying the duty is a normal condition of agency rather than a deficiency, and that the adviser's own capacity to attend depends on conditions that justice must reach.

That last point leaves a pressing question. A duty to attend to another's vulnerabilities, grounded in her dependency, looks very like a licence to manage her. The more an adviser sees, the more she may be tempted to act on her own view of what the client needs. Chapter 6 confronts this worry directly. It argues that attention, properly understood, is the opposite of paternalism rather than its instrument, and it states the constraint, (D4), that keeps them apart.

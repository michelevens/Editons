# Chapter 6: Attention without Paternalism

## 6.1 Introduction: the shadow of the good parent

Chapter 5 concluded that professional advisers owe their clients attention, and stated the conditions under which they do so as (D3). The argument relied heavily on the client's dependency and vulnerability. That reliance has a cost. The history of professional ethics is in large part a history of professionals who saw their clients' vulnerabilities clearly and responded by taking decisions out of their hands: the physician who withheld a diagnosis to spare the patient distress, the lawyer who settled a case the client wanted to fight because he judged it best for her, the supervisor who rewrote a student's argument into one he found more defensible. Richard Wasserstrom's critique of the legal profession identified exactly this tendency, arguing that the lawyer-client relationship is paternalistic and tends toward the lawyer's domination of the client, and locating the problem in professionalism itself {{wasserstrom1975}}. If attention is a disciplined perception of the client's vulnerabilities, and if responsiveness means letting what is perceived shape the advice, then the attentive standard (D1) looks like a recipe for exactly the domination Wasserstrom feared.

This is the paternalism objection, the second of the four objections the dissertation undertakes to answer. It is the most important of the four for the coherence of the view, because if it succeeds the thesis is not merely weakened but inverted: what I have been calling owed attention would turn out to be a refined form of the vice it was meant to oppose. This chapter argues that the objection fails, but that answering it requires making explicit a constraint that has been implicit in the account since Chapter 2. I call it the anti-paternalism constraint and state it as (D4).

The chapter proceeds in five stages. Section 6.2 sets out the concept of paternalism, drawing on Gerald Dworkin's classic analysis and his later theory of autonomy, and identifies the specific form of paternalism to which advisers are prone. Section 6.3 turns to relational autonomy and to O'Neill's critique of individualist autonomy in bioethics, and argues that the right conception of autonomy makes attention a condition of respecting it. Section 6.4 considers the principlist framework of Tom Beauchamp and James Childress and argues that attention is the epistemic precondition of specifying its principles in particular cases. Section 6.5 uses the capabilities approach of Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum as a guide to what accuracy attends to, while showing why that guide must not be used as a ranking of the client's ends. Section 6.6 states (D4) and sets out, in Table 6.1, the contrasts between paternalistic, template and attentive advice. Section 6.7 answers objections, and Section 6.8 hands on to the cases of Chapter 7.

The central claim of the chapter can be stated briefly in advance. Paternalism and template advice, which look like opposites, share a structure: each substitutes a frame supplied by the adviser or her institution for the client's own understanding of her situation and ends. The paternalist substitutes her personal view of the client's good; the template adviser substitutes a generic profile. Attention is opposed to both for the same reason. It requires that the client's own evaluative standpoint be part of what is perceived, and so it cannot coherently override that standpoint in the name of seeing her truly.

## 6.2 Paternalism

### 6.2.1 Dworkin's analysis

Dworkin's 1972 article 'Paternalism' defines the concept as interference with a person's liberty of action justified by reasons referring exclusively to the welfare, good, happiness, needs, interests or values of that person {{dworkin1972}}. He distinguishes pure paternalism, in which the person whose liberty is restricted is the same as the person whose good is protected, from impure paternalism, in which a wider class's liberty is restricted to protect some of its members. His central argument is with Mill. Mill's anti-paternalism, Dworkin argues, combines two strands: a utilitarian argument that individuals are generally the best judges of their own interests, which supports only a presumption against interference, and an argument from the intrinsic value of free choice, which supports something stronger. Having separated them, Dworkin defends a limited paternalism, justified by what fully rational persons would consent to as a form of social insurance against their own predictable failures of judgement {{dworkin1972}}.

Two features of this analysis set the terms for what follows. The first is that the definition is framed in terms of *interference with liberty of action*. The paradigm cases are legal prohibitions and requirements: laws mandating seatbelts, prohibiting certain drugs, restricting the terms on which one may sell one's labour. Advice, by contrast, does not ordinarily interfere with anyone's liberty. The client remains free to reject it. If paternalism is restricted to interference with liberty, then advisers are rarely in a position to be paternalistic in Dworkin's sense, and the objection might seem to dissolve.

That would be too quick. The second feature of Dworkin's analysis is that its moral weight lies in the *justification* rather than in the mechanism. What makes paternalism distinctive is that the agent acts on her own judgement of another's good in a domain where the other is entitled to judge for herself. That structure is available without coercion. An adviser who presents only one option, who frames a choice so that one option is the obvious one, who withholds information she judges the client would misuse, or who uses her authority to make disagreement feel imprudent, acts on her own judgement of the client's good in a way that bypasses the client's judgement, even though the client remains formally free. I shall call this *deliberative paternalism*: shaping another's deliberation about her own affairs, without her authorisation, by means she would reject if she were aware of them, for reasons referring to her good.[^d1]

### 6.2.2 Autonomy as critical reflection

Why is deliberative paternalism objectionable, if it leaves liberty intact? Dworkin's later *The Theory and Practice of Autonomy* supplies an answer. There he develops a procedural, hierarchical account of autonomy as the capacity to reflect critically on one's first-order preferences and desires and to accept or change them in light of higher-order preferences {{dworkin1988}}. He then applies the account to practical questions including paternalism and informed and proxy consent in medicine.

On a hierarchical view, what autonomy protects is not merely the freedom to act on whatever preference one has but the process by which one comes to endorse or revise one's preferences. Deliberative paternalism is an assault on that process. The adviser who frames a choice so that the client never confronts a genuine alternative, or who withholds what the client would need to reflect on her preferences, does not prevent the client from acting; she prevents her from *reflecting*, which on the hierarchical account is the core of self-government. The client may end up acting on a preference she would have endorsed, but she has not endorsed it; the endorsement was supplied for her.

This gives us a test that does not depend on coercion. Advice is paternalistic, in the sense relevant to advisers, when it is designed to secure an outcome the adviser judges good for the client by circumventing the client's capacity for critical reflection on her own preferences, rather than by engaging it. The test is useful because it cuts across the distinction between attentive and inattentive advice. A template adviser can be paternalistic in this sense: the compliance profile that assigned the widow to an annuity encoded a judgement about what is good for persons like her, and the advice was presented in a way that left her no occasion to reflect on whether she shared it. An attentive adviser can also be paternalistic, if she sees the client's situation clearly and then manages her toward the outcome she has seen to be best. The question for this chapter is whether attention *as such* tends toward the second failure, and what prevents it from doing so.

### 6.2.3 Why attention seems to invite paternalism

The worry deserves to be stated in its strongest form. There are at least three routes by which attention might seem to lead to paternalism.

The first runs through *accuracy*. Chapter 2, following Murdoch, characterised accuracy as seeing the client's situation truly, against self-serving or role-convenient distortion {{murdoch1970}}. But seeing truly seems to require a standard of truth that is independent of the client's own view. If the client believes her situation is one thing and the attentive adviser sees that it is another, accuracy seems to require the adviser to trust her own perception over the client's. That is the epistemic structure of paternalism.

The second runs through *vulnerability*. Chapter 5 grounded owed attention partly in the client's epistemic vulnerability, her inability to know what features of her situation bear on the advice. An adviser who takes this vulnerability seriously may conclude that the client is also unable to judge what is good for her in the relevant domain, and so that the adviser's judgement should prevail.

The third runs through *responsiveness*. To let what one perceives shape the advice and the manner of giving it is to adapt one's communication to the particular client. But adapting communication to a person is also the technique of the skilled manipulator. The adviser who knows that this client is frightened of risk, and who therefore presents the option she favours in reassuring terms and the alternatives in alarming ones, is responsive in a perfectly ordinary sense, and paternalistic in Dworkin's.

Each route is real. The rest of the chapter argues that each depends on a mistaken conception of what attention perceives, of what the client's autonomy consists in, or of what responsiveness is for.

## 6.3 Relational autonomy and the conditions of self-government

### 6.3.1 Autonomy refigured

The anti-paternalist tradition has often relied on a conception of the autonomous agent as self-sufficient, whose preferences are her own just because no one else has interfered with them. Feminist philosophers have criticised this conception, and the collection edited by Catriona Mackenzie and Natalie Stoljar, *Relational Autonomy*, is the standard statement of the alternative {{mackenziestoljar2000}}. The contributors reject the claim that autonomy is inherently masculinist and instead refigure it as relational: persons are socially embedded, and their identities and capacities for agency are formed through social relationships. A corollary is that oppressive socialisation can impair autonomy, so that a person's preferences may be shaped by circumstances in ways that limit her capacity for self-government even when no one is currently interfering with her.

The relational conception has two implications for advisers, which pull in opposite directions. On the one hand, it removes the presumption that respecting a client's autonomy simply means taking her stated preferences at face value and executing them. If autonomy is a capacity formed and sustained in relationships, then the advisory relationship is one of the relationships in which the client's autonomy is exercised, and it can support or undermine that capacity. An adviser who simply records the client's first answers to a questionnaire and acts on them may be failing to respect her autonomy, if those answers do not reflect her considered ends. On the other hand, the same conception seems to license exactly the interference the paternalism objection fears. If the adviser may look behind the client's stated preferences to ask whether they reflect her autonomy, she seems licensed to override them when she judges they do not.

### 6.3.2 Pathogenic responses to vulnerability

Mackenzie, Rogers and Dodds address this tension directly. Their taxonomy of vulnerability, introduced in Chapter 5, includes among the sources of pathogenic vulnerability responses to vulnerability that are themselves paternalistic {{mrd2014}}. A response to a person's vulnerability that disregards her agency compounds the vulnerability it purports to address: it makes her more dependent, less able to act on her own behalf, and less likely to be heard next time. The volume's concern with how responses to vulnerability can respect autonomy is precisely the concern of this chapter.

This gives the relational theorist a principled answer to the worry raised at the end of Section 6.3.1. The relational conception does not license overriding a client's preferences whenever the adviser judges them less than fully autonomous. It licenses, and requires, responses that *strengthen* the client's capacity for self-government. Overriding her preferences characteristically weakens that capacity, and so is ruled out by the relational conception itself in all but exceptional cases. What the relational conception adds to the standard anti-paternalist view is not a licence to interfere but a positive task: to support the conditions under which the client can reflect on and pursue her own ends.

Attention is the first condition of that task. An adviser cannot strengthen a client's capacity to reflect on her ends without first perceiving what those ends are, what is obscuring them, and what the client needs in order to consider them. Receptivity, in the sense of (D1), is exactly the willingness to let the client's ends register as hers rather than fitting them to a template of what persons like her want. The relational conception of autonomy therefore does not merely tolerate attention; it requires it.[^d2]

### 6.3.3 O'Neill on autonomy, consent and trust

O'Neill's Gifford Lectures, published as *Autonomy and Trust in Bioethics*, reach a related conclusion from a different direction {{oneill2002bioethics}}. O'Neill argues that the conceptions of individual autonomy dominant in bioethics are philosophically and ethically inadequate and tend to undermine rather than support trust between patients, professionals and institutions. In their place she proposes a Kantian conception she calls principled autonomy, and she examines informed consent, genetic information and other practices in its light.

The relevance of O'Neill's argument here is its account of what goes wrong when respect for autonomy is reduced to the procedure of obtaining consent. A consent procedure can be impeccable while the patient remains uninformed in any meaningful sense, because the procedure tests whether information was disclosed and agreement recorded, not whether the patient understood her situation or was in a position to deliberate about it. The analogy with template advice is close. Just as the compliance regime examined in Chapter 5 records that a fact-find was completed without testing whether the adviser saw the client, a consent regime records that disclosure occurred without testing whether the patient was met as a deliberating agent.

This is the sense in which owed attention is not reducible to informed consent, the second half of limb (ii) of the central thesis (Chapter 3 having established the first half, that it is not reducible to fiduciary loyalty and care). Informed consent protects the client's authority to decide. It does not ensure that the decision is about her situation as it is, because that depends on whether the adviser's presentation of options was shaped by an accurate perception of her ends. Consent obtained on the basis of a template presentation is consent to the template. Attention is what makes consent consent to the client's own decision.[^d3]

## 6.4 Principlism and the work of specification

The principlist framework of Beauchamp and Childress's *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* organises the ethics of professional-patient relationships around four prima facie principles: respect for autonomy, nonmaleficence, beneficence and justice {{beauchampchildress2019}}. These principles are not applied mechanically but specified and balanced against one another in particular cases. The text also treats professional-patient relationships, including veracity, privacy, confidentiality and fidelity, and the moral character of health professionals.

Paternalism, in the principlist framework, appears as a conflict between beneficence and respect for autonomy: the professional's obligation to promote the patient's good comes into tension with her obligation to respect the patient's self-determination. The framework offers no algorithm for resolving the conflict; it offers instead a method of specification and balancing, attentive to the facts of particular cases.

I want to draw attention to a feature of this method that the principlist literature tends to treat as background. Specification requires *perception*. To specify how respect for autonomy bears on a particular patient, one must perceive what she values, what she understands, and what she is trying to decide. To specify how beneficence bears, one must perceive what her good consists in, which, on any plausible view, depends partly on her own ends. To balance the two, one must perceive how they actually conflict in this case, if they do. Each of these perceptual tasks can be done well or badly, and a professional who does them badly will specify and balance badly, however sound her grasp of the principles.

This has a consequence for the paternalism objection. Many apparent conflicts between beneficence and autonomy are artefacts of inattention. The professional who sees a conflict between what the patient wants and what is good for her has often failed to perceive what the patient wants, or has substituted a generic conception of the patient's good for one informed by the patient's ends. When the patient's situation is perceived accurately, the conflict frequently narrows: the patient who seems to be refusing treatment out of irrationality turns out to have a coherent set of priorities the professional had not registered, and the treatment that seemed obviously beneficial turns out not to serve them. Attention does not abolish genuine conflicts between beneficence and autonomy, but it reduces the class of apparent conflicts, and with it the occasions on which paternalism seems called for. I do not claim that Beauchamp and Childress argue this; I claim that their method of specification presupposes the perceptual work that (D1) describes, and that taking that presupposition seriously shrinks the space in which paternalism operates.[^d4]

## 6.5 What accuracy attends to: the capabilities approach as a guide

### 6.5.1 Beyond preferences and resources

The first route to paternalism identified in Section 6.2.3 ran through accuracy. If accuracy means seeing the client's situation truly, what is the situation of which there is a truth to be seen? Two answers come readily to mind, and both are inadequate. The first is that the situation is constituted by the client's preferences: to see the client truly is to register what she wants. This answer avoids paternalism, but at the price of making accuracy trivial and template advice unobjectionable, since a well-designed questionnaire can record preferences. The second is that the situation is constituted by the client's resources: her assets, her health, her legal position. This answer makes accuracy substantive, but it leaves out what the resources are *for*, and so cannot tell the adviser which features of the resources matter.

The capabilities approach offers a better answer. Sen's *Development as Freedom* argues that development should be understood as the expansion of the real freedoms people enjoy, their capabilities to lead lives they have reason to value, rather than as growth in income alone {{sen1999}}. Freedom is, on his account, both the primary end and a principal means of development. Nussbaum develops a philosophical version of the approach, building on Sen, and defends in *Women and Human Development* a list of central human capabilities as universal norms that governments should secure to a threshold for each person {{nussbaum2000}}. In *Creating Capabilities* she sets out the list of ten central capabilities, including life, bodily health, bodily integrity, practical reason, affiliation and play, argues that a just society must secure each to a threshold as a matter of human dignity, and distinguishes her version from Sen's {{nussbaum2011}}.

The capabilities approach is a theory of justice and development, not of professional ethics, and I do not suggest that its proponents intended it as a guide for advisers. But its central idea transfers. What matters about a person's situation, from the standpoint of advice, is not merely what she prefers or what she has, but what she is actually able to do and to be, and what she has reason to value doing and being. A financial adviser who attends to the widow's capabilities, rather than merely her risk preferences or her assets, will ask what her capital enables her to do: to remain in her home, to help her grandson, to sustain the relationships that give her life its shape, to keep some freedom of action against an uncertain future. These questions are neither reducible to preferences nor fixed by resources. They are what accuracy attends to.

### 6.5.2 Adaptive preferences and the limits of the guide

Nussbaum's *Women and Human Development* also answers objections from the value of preferences, arguing that preferences can adapt to injustice {{nussbaum2000}}. People who have been deprived of opportunities may come to prefer less than they would otherwise, so that satisfying their preferences perpetuates the deprivation. This is an important argument for the political theory of capabilities, and it bears on advice: a client may state preferences that reflect a narrowed sense of what is possible for her, and an attentive adviser may notice this.

But here the paternalism objection returns in its sharpest form. If the adviser may judge that a client's preferences are adaptive and therefore not authoritative, she seems licensed to substitute her own judgement of what the client would prefer under better conditions. The capabilities approach, used as a guide to accuracy, seems to supply a theory of the client's good against which her stated preferences can be measured and found wanting.

Three features of the approach, and of its transfer to advice, block this inference. First, the approach's emphasis falls on what people are *able* to do and be, not on what they actually do. Securing a capability is compatible with the person's choosing not to exercise it; indeed the approach's concern with freedom, prominent in Sen's insistence that freedom is the primary end of development {{sen1999}}, makes the person's own choice among capabilities central. An adviser guided by the approach should therefore aim to keep options open and to make them visible, not to choose among them for the client.

Second, Nussbaum's list is offered as a political standard, a set of thresholds that governments should secure for every person {{nussbaum2011}}. It is not offered as a ranking of ends for any particular individual. To use it as a template of the good life and measure the client against it would be to commit, at a higher level of sophistication, exactly the vice of template advice: fitting the particular person to a general frame. The list serves accuracy as a checklist of *dimensions* of a situation to which an adviser should be receptive, not as an answer to how the client should weigh them.

Third, the recognition that a preference may be adaptive is itself a perception that requires accuracy, and accuracy, in the sense of (D1), includes accuracy about one's own tendency to distortion. An adviser who is quick to diagnose a client's preferences as adaptive when they diverge from what the adviser would choose is exhibiting precisely the self-serving or role-convenient distortion that accuracy guards against. The more confident the adviser is that she knows what the client would want under better conditions, the more reason she has to suspect herself.[^d5]

### 6.5.3 The client's standpoint as part of the situation

These considerations point to the general answer to the first route. The client's own evaluative standpoint, her sense of what matters and why, is not an external constraint on accurate perception of her situation. It is *part of the situation*. To see the widow truly is, among other things, to see her as a person with her own understanding of her life, her own reasons for valuing what she values, and her own authority over how her capital is used. An adviser who claims to see the widow's situation more accurately than the widow does, and on that basis to override her judgement, has not achieved a superior accuracy. She has left out one of the most important facts about the situation: that it is the widow's.

This is how Murdoch's image of a just and loving gaze directed upon an individual reality should be read in the advisory context {{murdoch1970}}. The individual reality includes the individual's own perspective. In Murdoch's example of the mother-in-law who comes to see her daughter-in-law more justly, the moral progress consists in coming to see the daughter-in-law as she is rather than as the mother-in-law's prejudices had represented her; it does not consist in the mother-in-law's arriving at a view of what the daughter-in-law should do. Attention, on this reading, is a discipline of letting the other be other. It is constitutively opposed to the paternalist's confidence that she knows better.

## 6.6 The anti-paternalism constraint

### 6.6.1 Statement

The arguments of the preceding sections can now be gathered into an explicit constraint on responsiveness, the component of (D1) through which perception becomes advice.

> **(D4) The anti-paternalism constraint.** In meeting the attentive standard (D1), an adviser A may let what she perceives about a client C shape the content and manner of her advice only in ways that (i) A could disclose to C without defeating their purpose; (ii) aim to enlarge C's deliberation about C's own ends rather than to replace it; and (iii) leave to C the judgement of C's good in matters within C's authority. The sole exception is where C's capacity for deliberation in the relevant domain is itself impaired, and then A may act only so far as is necessary to restore or support that capacity, and only in ways that would satisfy (i).

Each clause answers one of the routes to paternalism identified in Section 6.2.3, and each draws on the resources assembled in this and the previous chapter.

Clause (i), the *disclosure* clause, is an application of Baier's expressibility test {{baier1986}}, introduced in Chapter 5. Responsiveness that works only if the client does not know about it fails the test, and that is the mark of manipulation. An adviser who presents an option in reassuring terms because she knows the client is frightened, and who could say so ('I am putting this gently because I know this is frightening'), is responsive in a way that respects the client. An adviser who frames a choice to exploit the client's fear, and who could not say so without the framing ceasing to work, is not. The clause answers the third route.

Clause (ii), the *enlargement* clause, gives positive content to the relational conception of autonomy defended in Section 6.3. The aim of responsiveness is to support the client's capacity for critical reflection in Dworkin's sense {{dworkin1988}}: to put before her considerations she would want to reflect on, options she had not seen, and implications she had not traced, so that her eventual decision is more fully hers. This is compatible with the adviser's offering a recommendation, even a firm one, provided the recommendation is offered as a contribution to the client's deliberation rather than a substitute for it. The clause answers the second route: the client's epistemic vulnerability is a reason to supply what her deliberation lacks, not a reason to deliberate for her.

Clause (iii), the *authority* clause, marks the domain within which the client's judgement of her own good is final. It does not say that the client's judgement is always correct, only that within that domain it is hers to make. The domain is the one that Michael Bayles's fiduciary model of the professional-client relationship already marks out, in which the professional has superior knowledge but the client retains significant authority over decisions {{bayles1981}}. The clause answers the first route: accuracy may lead the adviser to disagree with the client, and candour may require her to say so, but accuracy does not transfer the authority to decide.

The exception clause acknowledges that there are cases, such as acute confusion, severe distress, or cognitive impairment, in which a client's capacity to deliberate is itself compromised. Dworkin's 1972 defence of limited paternalism as social insurance against predictable failures of rational agency gives one account of why some intervention may be justified in such cases {{dworkin1972}}. (D4) is narrower than Dworkin's position. It permits intervention only to restore or support the capacity, not to secure the outcome the adviser judges best, and it requires that the intervention be disclosable. A supervisor who notices that a doctoral student is too exhausted to make a sound decision about withdrawing from her programme may properly suggest that the decision wait; she may not make it for her.[^d6]

### 6.6.2 Three responses compared

The central claim of this chapter is that paternalism and template advice share a structure from which attentive advice departs. Table 6.1 sets out the contrast along six dimensions.

**Table 6.1** Paternalistic and attentive responses compared.

| Feature | Paternalism | Template advice | Attentive advice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whose frame governs | The adviser's personal view of the client's good | The institution's generic profile of clients of this type | The client's situation as perceived, including her own evaluative standpoint |
| Treatment of the client's stated ends | Overridden where they diverge from the adviser's judgement | Recorded and mapped to a category; not interrogated | Taken as authoritative within her domain, and explored so that they can be reflected on |
| Information practice | Selective disclosure in the service of the preferred outcome | Standardised disclosure, the same for all clients of the type | Disclosure shaped by what this client needs to deliberate, and itself disclosable |
| Response to vulnerability | Vulnerability treated as grounds for taking the decision over | Vulnerability coded as a risk category | Vulnerability treated as grounds for supporting the client's deliberation |
| Expressibility (Baier's test) | Often fails: works only if the client does not see the steering | Often fails: works only if the client assumes she has been seen | Passes: the adviser could explain why she advised as she did |
| Characteristic harm | Pathogenic dependency; loss of the client's standing as decision-maker | Pathogenic vulnerability; decision made for someone who is not quite there | Residual risk of honest error, which the client can see and contest |

*Note.* The table compares ideal types. Real advisory practice mixes them, and a single adviser may move between them within one consultation.

Two features of the table deserve comment. The first is the parallel between the first two columns. Both paternalism and template advice substitute a frame for the client's own; they differ only in whose frame it is. This is why the two vices so often coexist: a template is, in effect, an institutionalised paternalism, a pre-formed judgement about what persons of a type need, applied without inquiry into whether this person is of that type. The widow of Chapter 1 was subjected to both at once, since the annuity profile encoded a judgement about what is good for bereaved, risk-averse older clients, and the adviser applied it without asking whether she shared it.

The second feature is the last row. Attentive advice does not eliminate error. An adviser who meets (D1) and (D4) can still misperceive, and the client can still be harmed. But the error is of a different kind. Because attentive advice passes the expressibility test, the adviser's picture of the client is available to the client, and so can be seen and contested. This connects the anti-paternalism constraint back to the epistemic vulnerability of Chapter 5. The deepest protection against being misperceived is not a perfect perceiver but a relationship in which the perceiver's picture is offered to the perceived for correction. Attention under (D4) builds that relationship.

## 6.7 Objections and replies

### 6.7.1 'The constraint makes attention idle'

The first objection is that (D4) does all the anti-paternalist work and leaves attention with nothing to do. If the client's judgement within her domain is final, and if the adviser may only enlarge her deliberation, then a well-designed disclosure regime that lays out every option and lets the client choose would satisfy (D4) without any attention at all. Attention, so constrained, seems to collapse into the informed-consent model it was meant to supplement.

The reply is that enlargement under clause (ii) is impossible without attention. To enlarge a particular person's deliberation is to put before her what *she* needs to consider, and that cannot be known without perceiving her situation. A disclosure regime that lays out every option to every client does not enlarge deliberation; it swamps it, and in practice it relies on the client's sorting the relevant from the irrelevant, which is the very task her epistemic vulnerability prevents her from performing (Chapter 5, Section 5.3.3). The widow, on the filling-out of her case in Chapter 5, did not need a list of every retirement product on the market. She needed an adviser who had registered her grandson's education, her health and her late husband's wishes, and who could therefore tell her which considerations bore on her choice. (D4) constrains how attention may issue in advice; it does not replace the perception from which advice issues.

### 6.7.2 'Accuracy presupposes contestable moral realism'

The second objection targets the Murdochian strand in the account of accuracy. Murdoch held that attention is directed upon a reality, and that the Good draws us toward it {{murdoch1970}}. Clients and advisers in a pluralist society do not share a conception of the Good. If accuracy is measured against a moral reality, then either the adviser's conception prevails, which is paternalism, or there is no fact of the matter, and accuracy is empty.

The reply is that the accuracy required by (D1) is not accuracy about the Good in general but accuracy about the client's situation, and the client's evaluative standpoint is part of that situation (Section 6.5.3). There are facts about what the widow values, what she understands, what she fears and what she is trying to do; these facts are not settled by any comprehensive moral doctrine, and an adviser can get them right or wrong regardless of whether she shares the widow's values. What Murdoch contributes is not a metaphysics the adviser must accept but an account of the obstacles to such accuracy: the ego's tendency to see others in terms convenient to itself. That account is available to any adviser whatever her view of the Good. Insofar as accuracy also requires seeing some things the client does not, such as the implications of a product's terms, these are matters of fact and expertise, not of contested value, and conveying them is enlargement under clause (ii), not paternalism.[^d7]

### 6.7.3 'Some paternalism is justified'

The third objection comes from the other direction. Dworkin argued that some paternalism is justified, namely what rational persons would consent to as insurance against their own failures {{dworkin1972}}. Principlism, too, allows that beneficence may sometimes outweigh respect for autonomy {{beauchampchildress2019}}. By ruling out all paternalism outside the narrow exception, (D4) seems too strict. There are cases in which an adviser should steer a competent client away from a disastrous choice.

I accept that (D4) is stricter than Dworkin's 1972 position, and I defend the strictness in the advisory context. Dworkin's defence of limited paternalism concerns primarily state interference through general rules, where the case for social insurance is strongest because the rules apply impartially and are publicly justified. Advisory paternalism is individual, discretionary and typically undisclosed. The adviser who steers a client away from a choice she judges disastrous is relying on her perception of this client, and the whole argument of Chapter 5 was that clients cannot readily monitor or correct that perception. To license paternalism in such conditions would be to compound epistemic vulnerability with a vulnerability to being overridden on the basis of a picture one cannot see. What (D4) permits instead is often enough: the adviser can state her view as forcefully as candour requires, explain the consequences she foresees, ask the client to take time, and, if the choice falls outside what she can in conscience facilitate, decline to act. None of these overrides the client's authority, and each passes the expressibility test.

Where the objection has force, it is captured by the exception clause. If a client's capacity to deliberate is genuinely impaired, (D4) permits action to restore it. The disagreement between (D4) and a more permissive view therefore concerns only competent clients making choices the adviser judges unwise. For such clients I hold that the adviser's role is to inform, warn and, if necessary, withdraw, not to manage.

### 6.7.4 'Relational autonomy dissolves the constraint'

The fourth objection holds that the relational conception of autonomy defended in Section 6.3 undermines clause (iii). If preferences are shaped by social relationships, including oppressive ones {{mackenziestoljar2000}}, then the client's judgement of her good may itself be a product of the very conditions an attentive adviser should help her to see beyond. Respecting it as final, the objector says, is a retreat to the individualist conception the relational view rejected.

The reply is that the relational view supplies a reason to engage the client's judgement, not to override it. An adviser who suspects that a client's stated ends reflect a narrowed sense of possibility can, under clause (ii), put the wider possibilities before her and invite her to consider them. What she cannot do, consistently with the relational view itself, is decide on the client's behalf that the client's ends are inauthentic. That would treat the client as an object of the adviser's emancipatory project rather than as a participant in her own self-government, and it would produce exactly the pathogenic vulnerability that Mackenzie, Rogers and Dodds identify in paternalistic responses {{mrd2014}}. The relational conception explains why the advisory relationship matters to the client's autonomy; it does not transfer authority over the client's life to the other party in the relationship.[^d8]

## 6.8 Conclusion

This chapter has answered the paternalism objection. It began from the worry, sharpened by Wasserstrom's critique of professionalism, that a duty to attend to clients' vulnerabilities would license their domination. It identified the form of paternalism to which advisers are prone, deliberative paternalism, and used Dworkin's hierarchical account of autonomy to explain why it is objectionable even when liberty is untouched. It then argued that the relational conception of autonomy, O'Neill's critique of consent-centred bioethics, the principlist method of specification and the capabilities approach each, in different ways, make attention a condition of respecting autonomy rather than a threat to it. The key move was the recognition that the client's own evaluative standpoint is part of the situation that accuracy perceives, so that overriding it in the name of seeing truly is self-defeating.

The anti-paternalism constraint, (D4), makes this explicit. Responsiveness must be disclosable, must aim at enlarging rather than replacing the client's deliberation, and must leave to the client the judgement of her own good within her authority, save where her capacity to deliberate is itself impaired. Table 6.1 showed that paternalism and template advice share a structure, the substitution of an external frame for the client's own, and that attentive advice under (D4) departs from both. Together with (D3), this completes the dissertation's account of what owed attention is, why it is owed, and how it is bounded. Limb (iii) of the central thesis, that respect for relational autonomy distinguishes attention from paternalism, is thereby established.

What remains is to test the account against cases, and to answer the three objections not yet fully met: demandingness, of which Chapter 5 gave only the proportionality part; role-limits, which Chapter 4 addressed in principle; and unenforceability. Chapter 7 does this through three invented cases, a financial adviser, a general practitioner and an academic supervisor, and in doing so turns to the institutional conditions under which attention is possible, and to the attention debt that institutions can incur.

# Bridge: From Measuring Risk to Testing Protection

Chapter 4 answered the question that Chapter 3 left open. The systematic review showed that heat and occupational injury were associated, but the evidence rested on weather-station exposure and was of low certainty. The case-crossover study, using worksite-level WBGT for 18,406 injury claims, showed that the association holds when exposure is estimated where people actually work. Injury risk began to rise at a daily maximum WBGT of about 26 °C and was 21% higher at 31 °C than at 24 °C (RR 1.21, 95% CI 1.12–1.31). Nearly all of the excess fell on the day of exposure, and about 4.1% of warm-season injuries were attributable to heat, most of them on days that were hot but not extreme. In this sense, the epidemiological case for protecting outdoor workers from heat is now reasonably strong in this setting. Knowing how large a risk is, and when it occurs, does not tell us how to reduce it.

Three findings from Chapter 4 shaped what the next study had to do. The first was timing. Because the excess risk was concentrated at lag 0, protection has to act within the shift: on the day, in the hours when heat builds. Measures that work over days or weeks, such as seasonal acclimatisation programmes or general health promotion, cannot by themselves address an acute effect. This pointed towards measures triggered by same-day conditions, such as work–rest schedules that tighten as WBGT rises.

The second was the threshold. Most of the attributable burden arose on moderately hot days, below the levels at which extreme-heat alerts are issued and heavy work is customarily curtailed. A protective programme therefore had to start acting at moderate WBGT and become graded, not wait for exceptional days. That in turn raised a practical question the epidemiology could not answer. Can crews and supervisors realistically run a graded, WBGT-triggered schedule day after day across a whole summer, rather than on a few memorable days?

The third finding was the most important. The excess risk was concentrated among workers under 25 and, most strikingly, among agricultural workers paid by piece rate (RR 1.38 compared with 1.13 for hourly-paid workers). The pay-basis difference persisted among older workers, so it was not simply a matter of age. The most plausible interpretation, set out in Section 4.4.3, is that heat harms people most where rest is costly, whether the cost is lost earnings, a supervisor's displeasure or a deadline. If that is right, then advice to rest, drink and seek shade, which is the core of most heat guidance {{lucas2014;kjellstrom2016}}, will be followed least by the workers who need it most. Heat protection then becomes as much a matter of work organisation as of physiology. The relevant questions are who decides when a break is taken, whether a break is paid, and whether a supervisor treats a worker's request to rest as legitimate.

Administrative data cannot study these questions. Compensation claims, once linked to employers' pay declarations, can show that an injury happened and how the worker was probably paid, but not whether rest breaks were scheduled, whether they were taken, or why not. Answering them requires being on worksites, with crews and supervisors, while a protective programme is running. It also requires asking what the programme does as well as whether it reduces measured strain, and how the people who must make it work experience it.

These considerations led to the design of Paper 3, reported in Chapter 5. It is a mixed-methods feasibility study of a heat-stress management programme for construction crews, combining WBGT-triggered work–rest schedules, shaded rest areas, hydration stations, buddy checks and supervisor training. Three features of its design follow directly from Chapter 4. The programme is triggered by same-day WBGT and graded across moderate as well as extreme heat. Fidelity, meaning whether scheduled breaks were actually taken, is a primary feasibility outcome, because Chapter 4 suggested that the gap between a schedule and its use is where protection fails. Fidelity is also examined separately on piece-rate days. And a qualitative process evaluation sits alongside the quantitative measures {{moore2015}}, so that the reasons for that gap can be heard from supervisors and workers themselves.

The study was conducted in construction rather than agriculture for the reasons given in Chapter 2, chiefly access to crews through a regional contractor, and stable crew composition across a summer. Piece-rate and task-based payment arrangements were nonetheless present on some construction days, which allowed the pay question to be explored, if not settled. A feasibility study is the appropriate next step rather than a definitive trial. Under current guidance on complex interventions, a definitive evaluation should be preceded by work that tests whether an intervention can be delivered, whether its components are acceptable and whether a full trial is practicable {{craig2008;skivington2021}}. Chapter 5 is not designed to show whether the programme prevents injuries, and injury is not one of its outcomes. With 74 workers over eight weeks, it could not be. It asks a prior question that Chapter 4 made unavoidable: whether protection that depends on rest can work in conditions where rest has a price.

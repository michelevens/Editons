# Bridge: From Evidence Synthesis to Worksite Exposure

Paper 1 (Chapter 3) answered the first research question of this thesis by showing that the published evidence links occupational heat to injury and to kidney-related outcomes in outdoor workers, but with low certainty for injury and very low certainty for kidney outcomes. Just as useful was its picture of what the existing studies could not show. This short chapter explains how that picture led to the design of Paper 2, and why Paper 2 focuses on injury rather than kidney outcomes.

## What the review left unresolved

Three gaps from Paper 1 bear directly on the next study.

The first concerns where exposure was measured. Twenty-two of the 27 included studies (81%) assigned heat exposure from fixed weather stations, either directly or through WBGT modelled from station data, and none corrected for differences between the station and the places where people actually worked. The five studies that measured WBGT at the worksite were all small, and they tended to report larger associations. Their subgroup differences were not statistically significant, but the pattern is consistent with station-based exposure introducing measurement error that weakens observed associations and adds to heterogeneity between studies. WBGT is an imperfect index even when it is measured properly {{budd2008}}; estimated far from the worksite, it may describe the conditions of an airfield better than those of a roof deck or an unshaded field. If the true worksite association is stronger than the pooled estimate of 1.05 per degree suggests, then the 28 °C reference used to harmonise studies may also sit above the point at which risk actually begins to rise.

The second concerns timing. Only three of the 16 injury studies examined lagged effects, and the little evidence available suggested that most of the excess risk fell on the day of exposure or the next. If risk is immediate, protection must be triggered by same-day conditions; if it is delayed, recovery between shifts matters as much. Distributed lag non-linear models make it possible to estimate the shape of the exposure–response relationship and its timing together {{gasparrini2010}}, but almost none of the included studies used them.

The third concerns who bears the risk. Four injury studies suggested stronger associations in younger workers, and only one mentioned how workers were paid. None asked whether payment by the piece changes the relationship between heat and injury, even though the incentive to keep working through the heat is plainest where pay depends directly on output {{lucas2014}}.

## Why injury, and why a case-crossover design

Paper 2 focuses on injury for practical and substantive reasons. Injury was the outcome for which the evidence was more certain, an exposure–response gradient was already evident, and routine data existed at a scale large enough to estimate threshold, lag and effect modification with reasonable precision. Kidney outcomes would have required clinical or laboratory data that are not routinely linked to occupational records in the study region, and the small-study effects found in Paper 1 suggested that another small kidney study would add less than a well-powered injury study.

The (fictional) Coastal Region Workers' Compensation Board holds injury claims for agricultural and construction workers over many years. Claims records, however, do not on their own give the exposure of the injured worker on the day of injury. As described in Chapter 1, the workplace address on a claim is usually the employer's registered site rather than the place where the work was being done, so worksite exposure had to be estimated rather than read from the record. Paper 2 does this by estimating daily WBGT at worksite locations from 46 weather stations with a land-surface correction, which addresses the first gap directly.

A time-stratified case-crossover design suits these data and these questions. Each injured worker serves as their own control, with exposure on the day of injury compared with exposure on other days in the same month, so characteristics that do not change over a few weeks, such as age, experience, occupation and underlying health, are controlled by design. No measure of hours worked is needed, which matters for a claims database without a working-time denominator. Combined with a distributed lag non-linear model over lags of 0 to 3 days, the design allows the threshold and the timing of risk to be estimated rather than assumed, and confounding by time-varying factors can be addressed with an explicit causal diagram {{greenland1999}}. Stratifying by age and by payment arrangement then addresses the third gap. Because the design rests on assumptions that cannot be fully tested, Paper 2 also includes sensitivity analyses, among them a negative-control outcome, to check whether the association could be produced by bias rather than by heat.

## What to look for in Chapter 4

Its estimate of the threshold at which injury risk begins to rise can be compared with the 28 °C reference of Paper 1. Its lag analysis shows whether risk is immediate. Its stratified estimates show whether risk is concentrated among younger and piece-rate workers. Together these turn the general association established in Paper 1 into a description of risk at the scale at which prevention would have to work: the worksite, the working day and the particular groups of workers involved.

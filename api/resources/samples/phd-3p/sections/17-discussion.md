# Chapter 6: Integrated Discussion

## 6.1 Introduction

The three preceding chapters were written as stand-alone journal manuscripts. Each has its own discussion, framed by the conventions of its design and addressed to the readership of the journal for which it was prepared. A thesis by publication asks something more of its final chapters: that the papers be read together, as a single programme of work, and that the whole be shown to say something that none of the parts says on its own. This chapter does that work.

It proceeds in seven steps. Section 6.2 summarises the principal findings of the three papers against the research questions set out in Chapter 1. Section 6.3 develops the integrated argument of the thesis, which can be stated briefly here: the epidemiological evidence linking occupational heat to injury and kidney outcomes is real but thin and of low certainty (Chapter 3); at the level of the individual worksite, the injury risk associated with heat is immediate and concentrated in younger workers and in those paid by the piece (Chapter 4); and a heat-stress management programme of the kind most guidance recommends protects workers only to the extent that the organisation of work allows them to rest (Chapter 5). Taken together, these findings suggest that occupational heat protection is a problem of labour organisation as much as a physiological one. Section 6.4 sets out the methodological, substantive and practical contributions that follow from this argument. Section 6.5 examines where the three studies converge and where they pull in different directions. Section 6.6 discusses the limitations of the programme as a whole, as distinct from the study-specific limitations already reported in each paper. Sections 6.7 and 6.8 consider the implications for policy, practice and research, and Section 6.9 closes the chapter and hands on to the conclusion.

Throughout, I try to keep the claims calibrated to the strength of the evidence. Two of the three studies are observational and one is a non-randomised feasibility study that was not powered to detect effects. The integrated argument is therefore offered as a well-supported interpretation of a coherent body of work, not as a demonstrated causal chain.

## 6.2 Summary of principal findings

Chapter 1 posed three research questions, one for each paper. The first asked what the existing epidemiological literature shows about the relationship between occupational heat exposure and acute kidney and injury outcomes in outdoor workers, and how much confidence that evidence deserves. The second asked how daily heat exposure at the worksite relates to the risk of occupational injury among agricultural and construction workers, over what time lag, and for whom the risk is greatest. The third asked whether a multi-component heat-stress management programme for construction crews was feasible and acceptable, and what would need to change before it could be tested in a definitive trial. Table 6.1 summarises the answer each paper gave.

Read in sequence, the table already hints at the shape of the integrated argument. Paper 1 establishes that an association exists in the literature, but also that the literature is poorly placed to say much about its magnitude at the worksite, its timing, or how it might be prevented. Paper 2 responds to the first two of those gaps directly, with exposure estimated at the worksite rather than at the nearest weather station and with an explicit model of the lag structure. Paper 3 responds to the third, the near-absence of intervention evidence, and in doing so exposes the mechanism that Paper 2 could only infer from patterns of effect modification: that the way work is paid and supervised governs whether protection is used.

## 6.3 The integrated argument

### 6.3.1 A real association, thinly evidenced

The first strand of the argument is that the heat–injury and heat–kidney associations in outdoor workers are consistent in direction but weakly characterised. This matters because the policy discussion of occupational heat has sometimes moved faster than its evidence base. The broader literature leaves little doubt that heat strain impairs health, comfort and performance, and that the burden on outdoor workers is likely to grow as the climate warms {{kjellstrom2016;flouris2018;ebi2021}}. Narrative reviews of heat and occupational injury have reached similar conclusions about the direction of risk {{spector2019}}. What Paper 1 adds is a quantitative synthesis with an explicit appraisal of certainty, and the appraisal is sobering.

For injury, the pooled estimate of a 5% increase in risk per degree of WBGT above 28 °C was precise, but heterogeneity was substantial and the certainty of evidence was rated low under GRADE {{guyatt2008}}. For kidney outcomes, the pooled odds ratio of 1.47 was larger, but heterogeneity was higher still, Egger's test suggested small-study effects {{egger1997}}, and the certainty was rated very low. Two features of the evidence base drove these ratings. First, most included studies assigned exposure from regional weather stations, which assume that a worker on an exposed roof or in an unshaded field experiences the same thermal environment as an instrument at an airport or a meteorological site. {{budd2008|n}} has described why WBGT, even when measured properly, is an imperfect proxy for heat strain; when it is estimated remotely, the imprecision is compounded. Second, outcome definitions varied widely, particularly for kidney outcomes, where the included studies used a mixture of clinically diagnosed acute kidney injury and cross-shift or cross-season changes in markers of renal function.

The point is not that the association is doubtful. The direction of effect was consistent across nearly all included studies and is physiologically plausible {{parsons2014;lucas2014}}. The point is that a reader of the literature before this thesis could say with confidence that heat is bad for outdoor workers, but could say much less about how bad, how quickly, for whom, or what would help. Those are precisely the questions that determine how workplace heat plans should be designed.

### 6.3.2 Worksite risk is immediate and unequally distributed

The second strand is that, once exposure is estimated closer to where work actually happens, the injury risk associated with heat appears earlier, at a lower threshold and with a pronounced social gradient. Paper 2 found that injury risk began to rise above a WBGT of approximately 26 °C, below the 28 °C reference point used to harmonise exposure across the studies pooled in Paper 1. The relative risk at 31 °C compared with 24 °C was 1.21, and most of the excess fell on the day of exposure. The two estimates are not directly comparable, because the reference points, exposure metrics and outcome definitions differ, but they are compatible in direction and broadly consistent in magnitude. The difference in apparent threshold is consistent with what one would expect if weather-station exposure systematically understates the heat experienced at exposed worksites and so shifts the apparent onset of risk upward. I return to this point in Section 6.5, because it is one of the places where the papers inform one another's interpretation.

The lag structure carries a practical message. The distributed lag non-linear model {{gasparrini2010}} allowed the exposure–response relationship to vary over lags of 0 to 3 days, and the effect was concentrated at lag 0. This is the pattern one would expect if heat contributes to injury through acute mechanisms (fatigue, impaired vigilance, reduced psychomotor performance, sweaty hands on tools, dehydration) rather than through cumulative physiological damage {{spector2019;flouris2018}}. It also means that protection must operate within the shift. Measures that rely on next-day recovery, or that are triggered by multi-day heatwave declarations, would miss much of the risk. The sensitivity analysis excluding heatwave days, reported in Chapter 4, points the same way: the main association did not depend on those days, which suggests that much of the heat-related injury risk arises on ordinary hot working days that would not trigger a public heat-health alert.

The effect modification results are, in my view, the most consequential findings of Paper 2. The relative risk was higher among workers under 25 (RR 1.34) and higher still among agricultural workers paid by the piece (RR 1.38). Neither finding can be fully explained within a case-crossover design, which compares each worker with themselves and so speaks to who is affected by hot days rather than why. Several mechanisms are plausible for younger workers, including less experience in self-pacing, lower status within a crew and a reluctance to be seen to stop. For piece-rate workers, the most parsimonious explanation is economic: when pay is tied to output, every rest break has a direct cost, and the rational response to a hot day may be to work through it. The attributable fraction of approximately 4.1% of injuries, estimated using methods developed for population temperature–mortality studies {{gasparrini2015}}, is a modest share of all claims, but it represents a burden concentrated in identifiable groups and, in principle, a preventable one.

### 6.3.3 Protection works only if work organisation allows rest

The third strand is supplied by Paper 3, and it gives the mechanism behind the second. The programme tested in Chapter 5 combined five components that appear, in some form, in most occupational heat guidance: WBGT-triggered work–rest schedules, shaded rest areas, hydration stations, buddy checks and supervisor training. It was developed and evaluated following Medical Research Council guidance on complex interventions {{craig2008;skivington2021}}, with a process evaluation designed to examine implementation, mechanisms and context {{moore2015}}.

The quantitative feasibility outcomes were encouraging on recruitment and retention: 89% of eligible workers consented. The exploratory outcome signals pointed in the expected direction. Heat-symptom reports in intervention crews fell from 9.8 to 5.6 per 100 shifts, against a fall from 9.1 to 8.4 in comparison crews, and the proportion of workers with a urine specific gravity of 1.020 or above at the end of the shift fell from 46% to 31% in intervention crews against 44% to 41% in comparison crews. These differences were not tested for statistical significance and the study was not designed to estimate effectiveness. Their value lies in showing that the outcomes can be measured and that the signal is large enough to make a definitive trial worth designing.

The fidelity data are where the integrated argument becomes sharpest. Overall, 71% of scheduled breaks were taken. On days when crews were working under piece-rate arrangements, this fell to 52%. The components of the programme that cost workers nothing in pay, notably the hydration stations, were used readily; the component that required workers to stop producing, the scheduled rest break, was the one that eroded under economic pressure. The qualitative findings, analysed thematically {{braun2006}} and reported against COREQ {{tong2007}}, gave voice to this pattern. Four themes were developed: "breaks cost money", "the supervisor makes the weather", "water is easy, rest is hard" and "buddy checks feel like care, not policing". The first and third of these restate, from the workers' side, the piece-rate effect modification observed in Paper 2. The second identifies supervisors as the people who in practice decide whether a heat plan is a document or a working routine. The fourth is a reminder that not every component was experienced as a constraint; peer monitoring, framed as mutual care, was welcomed rather than resented.

### 6.3.4 Heat protection as a problem of labour organisation

These three strands support the central claim of the thesis: that protecting outdoor workers from heat is as much a problem of how work is organised, paid and supervised as it is a problem of physiology. The claim is not new in outline. {{lucas2014|n}} framed occupational heat exposure as an ergonomic risk to be managed through the design of work, and {{kjellstrom2016|n}} emphasised that heat reduces work capacity in ways that have economic as well as health consequences. {{flouris2018|n}} showed that heat strain is associated both with adverse health outcomes and with productivity loss, which implies that workers and employers are making a trade-off whenever they decide whether to rest. {{ebi2021|n}} similarly placed individual physiological vulnerability alongside the social and structural conditions that shape exposure.

What this thesis contributes is empirical support for that framing from three directions at once, in a single population and with consistent definitions. The physiological account explains why heat raises injury risk and why rest, shade and hydration should reduce it {{parsons2014}}. It does not explain why the risk is greater for piece-rate workers, why breaks are skipped when pay depends on output, or why the same programme is implemented differently under different supervisors. Those patterns require an account of work organisation: who controls the pace of work, who bears the cost of rest, and what norms govern stopping.

> **Figure 6.1** Integrated model of the thesis. Environmental heat (worksite WBGT; Chapters 3 and 4) → physiological heat strain (dehydration, fatigue, impaired vigilance) → acute injury and kidney outcomes (Chapters 3 and 4). Protective measures (rest, shade, hydration, buddy checks; Chapter 5) act on the path from heat to strain. Work organisation (pay system, supervisor practice, crew norms; Chapters 4 and 5) moderates two links: how much strain a given heat exposure produces, and whether protective measures are actually used.

Figure 6.1 summarises the model. Its key feature is that work organisation appears twice. It modifies the relationship between heat and harm directly, because workers who cannot slow down accumulate more strain for the same exposure, and it modifies the effect of protective measures, because measures that require stopping are used less where stopping is costly. A physiological model alone would predict that a well-designed work–rest schedule should protect everyone to whom it is applied. The data from Paper 3 suggest instead that the schedule protected workers roughly in proportion to how far the organisation of their work allowed them to follow it.

This framing has a consequence for how heat plans are evaluated. If protection depends on implementation, and implementation depends on pay and supervision, then an effectiveness trial that randomises a heat plan without addressing pay may underestimate what the plan could achieve and overestimate what it will achieve in practice under current arrangements. Both estimates are useful, but they answer different questions, and a trial needs to be clear which one it is asking. I return to this in Section 6.8.

### 6.4.1 Methodological contributions

The first methodological contribution concerns exposure. Paper 1 identified exposure misclassification as one of the principal weaknesses of the existing literature, and most included studies assigned heat exposure from the nearest weather station. Paper 2 estimated worksite WBGT using data from 46 stations together with a land-surface correction intended to capture the difference between a meteorological site and an exposed worksite. The approach is not a substitute for on-site measurement, and it cannot capture microclimates such as an enclosed roof space or a trench. It is, however, scalable: it can be applied retrospectively to administrative claims data covering many years and many thousands of workers, which on-site measurement cannot. The fact that Paper 2 detected a rise in risk at a lower threshold than the harmonised reference in Paper 1 is at least consistent with the correction having reduced the downward bias in exposure that station-based assignment can introduce. A formal validation of the correction against on-site WBGT measurements would strengthen this claim considerably, and I list it among the priorities for future work in Section 6.8.

The second methodological contribution is the use of a negative-control outcome. Time-stratified case-crossover designs control for fixed individual characteristics and for slowly varying confounders by design, and the causal diagram used to select time-varying confounders {{greenland1999}} identified a small set (humidity, captured within WBGT; fine particulate matter; and public holidays) that were handled in the model. The residual concern in a design of this kind is that some unmeasured, day-level factor correlated with heat, such as changes in reporting behaviour, seasonal workload or the composition of the workforce on hot days, might produce an apparent association. Injuries recorded on non-work days should not be caused by occupational heat exposure on those days, but they would be subject to many of the same reporting and seasonal artefacts. Using them as a negative control offers a structured way to probe for such bias. The technique is established in other areas of epidemiology but, as far as the review in Chapter 3 found, has been little used in occupational heat research. I suggest that it should become a routine sensitivity analysis in studies that link heat to compensation data.

The third methodological contribution is more modest. Risk-of-bias tools for non-randomised studies were developed with interventions in mind, and ROBINS-I {{sterne2016}} assumes a comparison between a well-defined intervention and a comparator. Paper 1 stated explicitly how the tool was adapted for exposure studies, including how the domains for confounding and for classification of exposure were interpreted when the exposure is environmental and continuous rather than chosen. Making that adaptation explicit, rather than applying the tool loosely or substituting an unvalidated checklist, allows readers to see how judgements were reached and allows others to reproduce or contest them. Combined with GRADE, it produced certainty ratings that are, I think, more candid about the state of the evidence than the overall tone of the literature had been.

The fourth methodological contribution lies in the process evaluation of Paper 3. Fidelity is often reported as a single summary figure in feasibility studies. Recording whether scheduled breaks were taken separately on piece-rate and hourly-paid days was a design decision taken in light of the effect modification observed in Paper 2, and it turned what would have been an unremarkable fidelity figure of 71% into an informative contrast between conditions. This illustrates a broader point about how a programme of related studies can be more than the sum of its parts: a finding in one study can specify what the next study should measure.

### 6.4.2 Substantive contributions

The principal substantive contribution is the evidence, from two studies using different designs and different kinds of data, that piece-rate pay modifies the relationship between heat and harm. In Paper 2, the relative risk of injury on hot days was higher among piece-rate agricultural workers than in the population as a whole. In Paper 3, adherence to scheduled breaks was markedly lower on piece-rate days, and workers explained, in their own terms, that breaks cost them money. The two findings are of different kinds. One is an epidemiological interaction estimated from administrative data; the other is a process measure and a set of accounts from the people affected. Neither, alone, establishes that pay structure is a cause of heat-related injury. Together, they make a coherent and mutually reinforcing case that it is an important modifier, and they identify a plausible mechanism: piece-rate pay attaches a direct cost to the behaviour on which most heat protection depends.

A second substantive contribution concerns timing. The concentration of excess injury risk at lag 0 means that, for injury at least, heat acts as an acute hazard. Earlier reviews pointed in this direction {{spector2019}}, but most of the studies pooled in Paper 1 used daily or longer exposure windows without modelling the lag structure explicitly. The finding narrows the window in which prevention must operate and supports measures that respond to conditions on the day, such as WBGT-triggered work–rest schedules, over measures that respond to forecasts or declared heatwaves alone.

A third, more tentative, contribution concerns young workers. The higher relative risk among workers under 25 in Paper 2 was not examined directly in Paper 3, because the feasibility study was not designed to compare age groups. The qualitative data nonetheless contained accounts consistent with it, particularly within the theme "the supervisor makes the weather", where newer crew members described taking their cue on whether to rest from what supervisors and senior workers did. This is suggestive rather than conclusive, and I flag it as a question for future work rather than a finding.

### 6.4.3 Practical contribution

The practical contribution follows from the integrated argument. If the component of heat protection that most depends on stopping work is the component that erodes under piece-rate pay, then a heat plan that does not address pay is unlikely to deliver its intended protection to the workers at greatest risk. This points towards pay protection as an explicit component of workplace heat plans: arrangements under which workers paid by output are compensated for time spent in mandated rest during high-heat periods, or under which output targets are adjusted when WBGT-triggered schedules apply. The details of such arrangements are matters for employers, workers and regulators to negotiate, and I do not claim that this thesis shows which arrangement would work best. What it does show, within the limits of the evidence, is that the question cannot be left out of heat-plan design without undermining the rest of the plan.

The feasibility study also yielded a more immediate practical product: a specified intervention, a set of measured feasibility parameters and a set of progression criteria. These form the basis for the definitive trial proposed in Section 6.8.

Integrating findings across studies of different designs requires attention not only to where they agree but to where they do not. Table 6.3 sets out the main points of comparison.

The strongest convergence is on pay structure, discussed above. The convergence on timing is also notable, because the three studies approach it from quite different angles. Paper 1 found that timing had rarely been modelled, Paper 2 estimated it directly, and Paper 3 chose outcomes measured within the shift partly because Paper 2 suggested that the relevant window was the working day.

The most interesting divergence concerns the threshold at which risk begins to rise. The 28 °C reference point in Paper 1 was a harmonisation choice, made so that studies reporting effects over different exposure ranges could be pooled; it was not an estimated threshold. Even so, the fact that Paper 2 observed a rise in risk from approximately 26 °C invites interpretation. Three explanations are possible. The first is exposure measurement: if worksite WBGT estimated with a land-surface correction is systematically higher than station-based estimates on the same day, then a given station reading corresponds to a hotter worksite, and the apparent threshold in station-based studies would be shifted upward. The second is population: the workers in Paper 2 may differ from those in the pooled studies in acclimatisation, workload or clothing, all of which alter the relationship between environmental heat and physiological strain {{parsons2014;budd2008}}. The third is modelling: a flexible exposure–response function will detect curvature that a linear-above-threshold model cannot. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the data available to this thesis do not allow them to be separated. The divergence is nonetheless a useful caution against transferring thresholds uncritically from one setting, or one exposure metric, to another.

The least integrated part of the programme concerns kidney outcomes. Paper 1 found a pooled association with very low certainty, but neither of the subsequent studies was designed to examine kidney function. Paper 2 used injury claims, which do not capture kidney outcomes, and Paper 3 measured urine specific gravity only as a marker of hydration at the end of the shift, not as an indicator of renal injury. The reduction in the proportion of intervention-crew workers with concentrated urine at shift end is relevant to kidney health in the sense that repeated dehydration is one of the hypothesised pathways to kidney injury in hot occupations {{flouris2018;ebi2021}}, but it would be an over-reach to treat it as evidence about kidney outcomes. The kidney strand of the thesis therefore remains at the level of evidence synthesis. I regard this as a genuine limitation of the programme's scope, discussed further below.

## 6.6 Limitations of the programme as a whole

Each paper reports its own limitations, and I do not repeat them in full here. This section focuses on limitations that arise from the programme as a whole: from how the studies were linked, from features they share, and from what the combination of designs can and cannot support.

### 6.6.1 Exposure measurement

All three studies rely on WBGT, and WBGT has well-known limitations as an index of heat strain. It is an environmental index rather than a physiological one, it does not account directly for metabolic rate or clothing, and its components behave differently under different combinations of humidity, radiation and wind {{budd2008;parsons2014}}. In Paper 2, WBGT was estimated rather than measured, and although the land-surface correction is an improvement on station-based assignment, it was not validated against on-site measurements within this programme. In Paper 3, WBGT was measured on site to trigger work–rest schedules, but personal exposure varied within crews depending on task and location. The consistency of the exposure metric across studies is a strength for integration; its imperfection as a measure of what workers actually experienced is a shared weakness.

### 6.6.2 Outcome ascertainment

Paper 2 relied on workers' compensation claims, which capture injuries that were reported, accepted as work-related and severe enough to warrant a claim. Under-reporting of occupational injury is likely to be greater among precarious, migrant and piece-rate workers, who may have more to lose from reporting [source needed]. If under-reporting is greater in exactly the groups in which effect modification was observed, the true effect modification could be larger than estimated; but if reporting itself varies with heat, for instance because injuries on hot days are more readily attributed to work, the direction of bias is less clear. The case-crossover design protects against reporting differences that are stable within a person but not against those that vary from day to day. The negative-control analysis was designed to detect bias of this kind, but it cannot rule it out. In Paper 3, heat symptoms were self-reported by workers who knew which programme they were receiving, which introduces a risk of reporting bias in the direction of apparent benefit. Urine specific gravity is an objective measure but a proximal one.

### 6.6.3 Causal inference

None of the three studies was a randomised trial, and the integrated argument depends on reading observational and feasibility data together. Paper 2 was designed to support causal interpretation as far as an observational design reasonably can, using a within-person comparison, a confounder set selected from a causal diagram {{greenland1999}} and a negative control. Its effect modification estimates, however, are associations, and piece-rate pay is likely to be correlated with other characteristics, such as crop type, employer size and migrant status, that could themselves modify heat risk. In Paper 3, crews were allocated to intervention or comparison without randomisation, and with only six crews the comparison is vulnerable to differences between them that were present before the programme began. The integrated argument should therefore be read as an inference to the best explanation across consistent findings, not as a demonstration that changing pay arrangements would reduce heat-related injury. Testing that claim is the purpose of the trial proposed in Section 6.8.

### 6.6.4 Scope and generalisability

Papers 2 and 3 were conducted in a single coastal region, with its particular climate, labour market and compensation system. The construction and agricultural sectors studied there may not resemble those in hotter, drier regions, or in settings where informal employment predominates and compensation data do not exist at all. Paper 3 was conducted only with construction crews, so the piece-rate effect observed there arose in construction, while the piece-rate effect modification in Paper 2 was estimated in agriculture. That the pattern appeared in both sectors strengthens the argument that pay structure matters, but the mechanisms may differ: piece rates in agriculture are typically tied to quantities harvested, while in construction they tend to attach to completed tasks. The thesis also did not examine indoor heat exposure, the specific risks faced by women workers, or the interaction of heat with other hazards beyond adjustment for fine particulate matter.

### 6.6.5 Temporal coherence of a thesis by publication

A thesis by publication has a limitation that a monograph does not: its papers are fixed at different points in time. The literature searches for Paper 1 ended in March 2024, and Papers 2 and 3 were designed partly in response to the gaps it identified. Studies published after that date, including any intervention studies, are not included in the synthesis. In addition, the decision to examine pay structure in Paper 3 arose from findings in Paper 2, which means that the programme was sequential and adaptive rather than wholly pre-specified. I regard this as a strength in that each study was informed by the last, but it also means that the integrated argument was, to some extent, developed as the work proceeded. A reader should weigh it accordingly: the piece-rate finding in Paper 3 was a test of an idea suggested by Paper 2, not an independent discovery.

## 6.7 Implications for policy and practice

The first implication is that workplace heat plans should be judged by what they make possible on the ground, not only by what they contain. A plan that specifies WBGT-triggered rest but leaves pay and output targets unchanged may be compliant on paper and largely ineffective in practice for the workers most at risk. Regulators and employers reviewing heat plans could reasonably ask how rest is to be protected for workers paid by output, and how supervisors are equipped and expected to enforce it.

The second implication concerns supervisors. The theme "the supervisor makes the weather" suggests that the most important determinant of whether a heat plan is followed on a given day may be the person running the crew. Supervisor training was one component of the programme in Paper 3, but the qualitative data suggest that training alone is insufficient if supervisors are themselves judged on output. Aligning supervisors' incentives with heat protection is, again, a matter of work organisation rather than of knowledge.

The third implication concerns the threshold and timing of action. The finding that risk began to rise above a WBGT of approximately 26 °C, and that it was concentrated on the day of exposure, suggests that heat plans keyed to declared heatwaves or to higher thresholds may begin too late, at least in settings similar to the one studied. I do not propose a specific regulatory threshold on the basis of a single region's data. The finding does support action triggered by on-site conditions during the working day, and it supports caution about adopting thresholds derived from station-based exposure without considering how worksite conditions differ.

The fourth implication concerns the components that workers welcomed. Hydration stations were easy to adopt, and buddy checks were experienced as care rather than surveillance. These components are inexpensive and acceptable, and there seems little reason not to adopt them widely. The point of the integrated argument is not that such measures are unimportant but that they are insufficient on their own, because they leave untouched the component, rest, that most directly limits heat strain {{lucas2014;parsons2014}}.

## 6.8 Implications for research

The most direct implication for research is a definitive cluster randomised controlled trial of the heat-stress management programme, with a pay-protection component added. The feasibility study met its progression criteria for recruitment and retention and partially met them for fidelity, and the shortfall was concentrated in exactly the condition, piece-rate days, that a pay-protection component is designed to address. A trial of this kind would randomise crews or sites, include both piece-rate and hourly-paid work, and specify the pay-protection mechanism in advance in negotiation with employers and worker representatives. Following updated MRC guidance {{skivington2021}}, it should be accompanied by a process evaluation {{moore2015}} that measures fidelity by pay arrangement and examines supervisor practice directly. A multi-arm design, comparing the programme with and without pay protection, would allow the contribution of the pay component to be estimated separately, though the sample size required for such a design would need careful calculation.

Where randomisation is not feasible, for instance when pay-protection arrangements are introduced through regulation or collective agreement, the target trial framework {{hernan2016}} offers a disciplined way to use observational data. Specifying the protocol of the trial one would ideally run, including eligibility, treatment strategies, time zero and follow-up, and then emulating it with claims or cohort data, would reduce several of the biases common in before-and-after evaluations of workplace policies. The linked claims and exposure data developed for Paper 2 would be well suited to this approach if information on pay arrangements could be added to them.

A second priority is the validation of worksite exposure estimation. Deploying on-site WBGT monitors at a sample of worksites, and comparing their readings with the station-based and land-surface-corrected estimates used in Paper 2, would quantify the exposure misclassification that the thesis argues is pervasive in the literature, and would allow the threshold divergence discussed in Section 6.5 to be examined directly.

A third priority is kidney outcomes. The very low certainty of the kidney evidence in Paper 1, combined with the absence of kidney measures in Papers 2 and 3, leaves a substantial gap. Prospective cohorts of outdoor workers with repeated measures of renal function across working seasons, linked to worksite exposure, are needed to establish whether the association observed in the pooled studies reflects a causal effect of occupational heat and, if so, at what level of cumulative exposure.

A fourth priority is to examine the mechanisms behind the higher risk among young workers, and to test whether interventions directed at crew norms and supervisor behaviour, rather than at individual knowledge, reduce it.

Finally, the methodological contributions described in Section 6.4.1 suggest that occupational heat research would benefit from more routine use of explicit lag modelling {{gasparrini2010}}, negative-control outcomes and transparent risk-of-bias adaptation, together with reporting against established guidelines for observational studies {{vonelm2007}} and systematic reviews {{page2021}}. Future syntheses should also continue to examine heterogeneity explicitly {{higgins2002}} rather than treating a single pooled estimate as the answer, particularly where, as here, random-effects pooling {{dersimonian1986}} combines studies with very different approaches to exposure assessment.

## 6.9 Chapter summary

This chapter has read the three papers of the thesis together. It has argued that the evidence base for occupational heat and health outcomes in outdoor workers is consistent but of low certainty; that at the worksite, heat-related injury risk is immediate, begins at moderate levels of WBGT and is concentrated in young and piece-rate workers; and that a multi-component heat-stress management programme was feasible and acceptable but was followed least where following it cost workers pay. From these findings it has drawn the thesis's central claim: that heat protection is a problem of labour organisation as much as of physiology. It has set out the methodological, substantive and practical contributions that follow, examined where the papers converge and diverge, discussed the limitations of the programme as a whole and identified the implications for policy, practice and research. Chapter 7 concludes the thesis by stating briefly how each research question has been answered and what the thesis contributes to knowledge.

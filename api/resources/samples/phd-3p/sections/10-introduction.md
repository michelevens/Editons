# Chapter 1: Introduction

This chapter introduces the problem that the thesis addresses, explains why it matters, and sets out how the three studies that follow were designed to answer it. It begins with heat as an occupational hazard in a warming climate (Section 1.1), then describes the mechanisms through which heat harms workers and the terms used to describe them (Section 1.2). Section 1.3 explains why outdoor workers in agriculture and construction are the focus, with particular attention to the way their work is organised and paid. Section 1.4 identifies three gaps in the existing evidence, and Section 1.5 states the aim and research questions that follow from them. Section 1.6 presents the conceptual framework that holds the thesis together. Sections 1.7 to 1.10 describe the overall research approach, the study setting, my own position as a researcher, and the ethical framework. Section 1.11 explains the structure of a thesis by publication and how this one is organised, and Section 1.12 summarises the chapter.

## 1.1 Heat at work in a warming climate

Heat has always been part of outdoor work. Farmers, builders, road crews and labourers have long adjusted the rhythm of their days to the sun, starting early, resting in the hottest hours and finishing late. What has changed is the frequency and intensity of hot conditions, and the extent to which the modern organisation of work permits those older adjustments. Climate change is expected to increase the number of hours in which outdoor work is carried out under heat stress, with consequences for both health and productivity that fall most heavily on people who have the least control over when and how they work {{kjellstrom2016}}. Reviews of the epidemiological and physiological evidence describe hot weather and heat extremes as a growing threat to population health in general {{ebi2021}}, and occupational settings concentrate that threat: workers cannot always retreat indoors, they generate metabolic heat through physical effort, and they often wear clothing or protective equipment that impedes heat loss {{lucas2014}}.

The occupational dimension of heat has received less attention than its effects on the general population. Much of the heat-health literature concerns mortality during heatwaves, the vulnerability of older people and those with chronic disease, and the planning of public health responses for cities {{ebi2021;gasparrini2015}}. These are important concerns, but they leave the working population at the margins. A worker exposed to heat is typically young or middle-aged, healthy enough to be employed in manual work, and exposed not only during rare extreme events but across ordinary hot days throughout a season. The harms that follow are also different in character. They are less likely to be deaths recorded on a certificate and more likely to be injuries, episodes of heat-related illness, losses of productivity and, possibly, cumulative damage to the kidneys that may not become visible for years {{flouris2018}}.

A systematic review and meta-analysis by {{flouris2018|n}} drew this evidence together and concluded that occupational heat strain is associated with a range of adverse health outcomes and with lost productivity. Its contribution was to show that the occupational heat burden is measurable and not trivial. It also revealed the limitations of the literature on which such conclusions rest: studies vary widely in how they define and measure exposure, in the outcomes they examine and in their designs, and few examine what happens when workplaces try to protect their workers. A review focused specifically on occupational injury reached a similar position, finding that heat exposure is associated with greater injury risk while drawing attention to inconsistent exposure metrics and the scarcity of studies that could guide prevention {{spector2019}}.

Heat also has an economic dimension that bears directly on health. When workers slow down in the heat, as physiology demands, output falls; analyses of the global effects of climate change on work capacity have argued that these losses will be considerable and will fall disproportionately on hot, low-income regions and on manual occupations {{kjellstrom2016}}. Lost productivity is sometimes presented as a separate outcome from ill health, but the two are linked. A worker who slows down is protecting themselves, and the cost of that protection is borne by someone: the employer, through lower output, or the worker, through lower earnings. Who bears that cost depends on the way work is organised and paid, a theme that recurs throughout this thesis and becomes central in Chapters 4 to 6.

The policy context adds urgency. Many jurisdictions now have, or are developing, workplace heat standards and guidance, and employers are increasingly expected to have heat-stress management plans [source needed]. These instruments rest on assumptions about the temperatures at which risk begins, the populations most affected, and the protective measures that work. If those assumptions are drawn from studies that measured temperature at airports rather than worksites, or from interventions tested under conditions that differ from real work, the resulting rules may set the threshold in the wrong place, protect the wrong people, or require measures that cannot be implemented. This thesis is motivated by the view that public health protection of outdoor workers needs a firmer and more practically grounded evidence base than currently exists.

## 1.2 How heat harms workers

### 1.2.1 Heat stress, heat strain and how they are measured

Three terms recur throughout this thesis and need to be distinguished. *Heat stress* refers to the net heat load to which a person is exposed, arising from the environment (air temperature, humidity, air movement and radiant heat), from metabolic heat generated by physical work, and from clothing that limits heat loss. *Heat strain* refers to the body's physiological response to that load, including rises in core temperature and heart rate, sweating and the resulting loss of body water. *Heat-related illness* denotes the clinical consequences of excessive strain, from heat cramps and heat exhaustion to heat stroke. The distinction matters because the same environmental conditions can produce very different levels of strain in different people, depending on how hard they are working, how acclimatised they are, what they are wearing and whether they can rest and drink {{parsons2014}}.

Human thermal physiology provides the foundation for understanding these responses. The body maintains core temperature within a narrow range by balancing heat gain against heat loss through convection, radiation and, above all in hot conditions, the evaporation of sweat. When environmental temperature approaches or exceeds skin temperature, convective and radiative heat loss diminish or reverse, and evaporation becomes the dominant route of cooling. High humidity limits evaporation, which is why humid heat is particularly dangerous. Physical work adds metabolic heat that must be dissipated, and sustained sweating without adequate fluid replacement leads to dehydration, reduced plasma volume and greater cardiovascular strain {{parsons2014;lucas2014}}.

Because heat stress has several components, a single measure of it is inevitably a compromise. Air temperature alone ignores humidity, wind and radiation. Numerous composite indices have been developed to combine these factors, and the wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) is the most widely used in occupational settings. WBGT combines natural wet-bulb temperature, which reflects humidity and air movement, with globe temperature, which reflects radiant heat, and dry-bulb air temperature in outdoor conditions. It was developed for military training and later adopted in occupational standards, and its strength lies in its long history of use and its link to established work–rest guidance. {{budd2008|n}} traces this history and sets out the index's limitations, including its imperfect representation of evaporative cooling under some conditions, the difficulty of measuring it consistently, and the frequent practice of estimating it from standard meteorological variables rather than measuring it directly. These limitations are not grounds to abandon the index, which remains the reference measure for workplace guidance, but they are reasons to be explicit about how WBGT has been obtained in any given study. The question of where and how WBGT is estimated becomes central to Paper 2.

### 1.2.2 Pathways from heat to injury

The link between heat and occupational injury is less intuitive than the link between heat and heat-related illness, but it is plausible on several grounds. Heat strain impairs cognitive performance, including attention, vigilance and reaction time, and these functions are critical in tasks involving machinery, heights, vehicles and sharp tools {{lucas2014}}. Fatigue accumulates faster in hot conditions. Sweaty hands reduce grip, fogged safety glasses reduce vision, and workers may remove protective equipment that makes them hotter. Dehydration further impairs physical and cognitive performance. Heat may also change behaviour in ways that increase risk, such as rushing to complete work before the hottest part of the day or skipping breaks to meet output targets {{spector2019}}.

Several of these pathways operate quickly, which implies that injury risk should respond to heat on the same day or the next, rather than after a long delay. This has a direct implication for study design: if risk is immediate, analyses should be able to detect short lags, and exposure measured on the day of the injury at the place it occurred matters more than seasonal or annual averages. It also implies that protective measures must operate on the same timescale, through decisions taken during the working day about the pace of work, the timing of breaks and the allocation of tasks.

### 1.2.3 Pathways from heat to kidney harm

The kidney is the second outcome domain in this thesis. Repeated heat strain with dehydration reduces renal blood flow, concentrates the urine and may, through mechanisms that are still debated, lead to acute kidney injury and, with repetition over years, to chronic damage {{ebi2021}}. Interest in this pathway grew from reports of chronic kidney disease of uncertain cause among agricultural workers in hot regions, where the usual risk factors, such as diabetes and hypertension, did not explain the observed burden [source needed]. The evidence linking occupational heat to kidney outcomes is, however, more contested than that for injury. Studies frequently rely on cross-sectional measurements of serum creatinine or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), sometimes taken before and after a shift or a harvest, and these markers can change with hydration status in ways that do not necessarily indicate lasting damage. Exposure is often defined by occupation or season rather than measured, and confounding by other workplace exposures, including agrochemicals, is difficult to rule out {{flouris2018}}.

For these reasons, the thesis treats kidney outcomes with caution. Paper 1 synthesises the available evidence on kidney-related outcomes alongside injury and grades its certainty separately. Paper 3 uses urine specific gravity at the end of the shift as a feasible field marker of hydration status rather than of kidney function, and it does not claim to measure kidney injury. Paper 2 concerns injury alone, because compensation claims capture injuries far more completely than they capture kidney disease.

## 1.3 Outdoor workers in agriculture and construction

Outdoor workers are not a homogeneous group, and this thesis focuses on two sectors where heat exposure, physical workload and organisational constraints combine in particularly consequential ways: agriculture and construction. Both involve heavy physical work in direct sun, often during the hottest months of the year when demand for labour peaks. Both employ large numbers of young workers, seasonal and migrant workers, and workers on insecure contracts, groups who may have less ability to refuse unsafe work or to set the pace of their own day {{kjellstrom2016;lucas2014}}. And both sectors have well-established forms of work organisation, such as harvest schedules, project deadlines and subcontracting chains, that shape when and how hard people work independently of the weather.

Payment systems deserve particular attention. In parts of agriculture, and in some construction trades, workers are paid wholly or partly by the piece: by the bin of fruit picked, the row harvested or the square metre laid. Piece-rate pay ties income directly to output, which creates an incentive to work faster and to forgo rest. In cool conditions this may matter little for health. In hot conditions it directly opposes the main protective measure available, which is to slow down and rest. The possibility that piece-rate work modifies the effect of heat on injury has been raised in the literature {{spector2019}}, but it has rarely been examined with data that can separate the effect of pay from that of sector, task and season. Testing it is one of the specific objectives of Paper 2, and its practical implications become central to Paper 3.

Age is a second important modifier. Younger workers may be less experienced, less aware of the early signs of heat strain, less likely to speak up, and more likely to be assigned the heaviest tasks, while older workers may have reduced physiological capacity to dissipate heat {{flouris2018;lucas2014}}. Which of these considerations dominates in a given workforce is an empirical question. The thesis does not assume that any particular group is more vulnerable, but it does test whether the effect of heat on injury differs by age and payment system, because these are the dimensions along which protection could most readily be targeted.

Construction was chosen as the setting for the intervention study in Paper 3 for practical as well as substantive reasons. Construction work is organised in crews with an identifiable supervisor, which makes a crew-level intervention feasible and creates natural clusters for a future trial. Sites persist for weeks or months, allowing an eight-week intervention period. And construction crews include both hourly-paid workers and those working on piece-rate or task-based arrangements, which makes it possible to observe how payment affects the uptake of protective measures within a single setting. The choice does mean that the intervention findings apply most directly to construction rather than agriculture, a limitation discussed in Chapter 5 and again in Chapter 6.

## 1.4 The problem: three gaps in the evidence

The preceding sections suggest that heat is a credible and growing hazard for outdoor workers, that its effects on injury are biologically and behaviourally plausible, and that its effects on the kidney are plausible but contested. When I began this research, three gaps stood between that general picture and the practical question of how to protect workers. Each gap motivated one of the three papers.

### 1.4.1 An evidence base of uncertain size and certainty

The first gap concerns the quality and coherence of the evidence itself. Existing reviews established that heat is associated with adverse occupational outcomes {{flouris2018;spector2019}}, but they did not provide pooled estimates expressed in a form that can inform workplace thresholds, such as the change in injury risk per degree of WBGT above a stated level. Nor did they formally assess the certainty of the evidence for injury and kidney outcomes separately, using an approach such as GRADE that considers risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias together {{guyatt2008}}. Without such an assessment, it is difficult to know how much confidence to place in the claim that heat causes these outcomes, or how large the effect is likely to be.

The evidence also comes almost entirely from observational studies of exposure rather than randomised trials, which raises a methodological question of its own. Tools for assessing risk of bias, such as ROBINS-I, were developed for non-randomised studies of interventions {{sterne2016}}; applying them to studies of environmental exposures requires adaptation, particularly in the domains concerned with confounding and with classification of the exposure. Paper 1 addresses this first gap through a systematic review and meta-analysis reported according to PRISMA 2020 {{page2021}}, with random-effects pooling {{dersimonian1986}}, quantification of heterogeneity {{higgins2002}}, assessment of small-study effects {{egger1997}}, an adapted risk-of-bias tool and a GRADE assessment of certainty.

### 1.4.2 Exposure measured in the wrong place

The second gap concerns exposure measurement. Most epidemiological studies of heat and occupational outcomes assign exposure from the nearest weather station, often at an airport or other open site, sometimes many kilometres from where the work took place. Weather stations are sited to measure standard conditions, not the conditions on a construction site surrounded by concrete or in a field with no shade. The differences can be substantial, and they are likely to vary systematically: worksites in built-up areas may be hotter than nearby stations, while irrigated fields may be cooler or more humid. Moreover, standard meteorological records do not include WBGT, which must be estimated from air temperature, humidity, wind speed and solar radiation using models whose accuracy depends on the inputs {{budd2008}}.

Exposure misclassification of this kind generally biases associations, often but not always towards the null, and makes it harder to identify the temperature at which risk begins. It also limits the usefulness of the evidence for policy, because workplace heat standards are expressed in terms of conditions at the worksite. The temporal resolution of exposure is a related issue. If injury risk responds to heat on the day of exposure, as the pathways in Section 1.2.2 suggest, then analyses using weekly or monthly exposure will obscure the relationship. Methods exist that can model non-linear exposure–response relationships and delayed effects simultaneously, notably distributed lag non-linear models {{gasparrini2010}}, and approaches for estimating the fraction of outcomes attributable to temperature have been developed in studies of mortality {{gasparrini2015}}. Applying them to occupational injury with worksite-level exposure was the purpose of Paper 2.

### 1.4.3 Protection untested under real working conditions

The third gap concerns protection. Occupational heat guidance generally recommends a familiar set of measures: acclimatisation, water, shade, rest breaks scaled to heat stress, training and monitoring for symptoms {{parsons2014;lucas2014}}. These measures are physiologically sound. What is far less clear is whether they are implemented, and whether they work, in real workplaces where they must compete with production pressure, payment systems and workplace culture. The systematic review in Paper 1 found almost no studies evaluating protective interventions against injury or kidney outcomes, which is itself a significant finding.

Heat-stress management programmes are complex interventions in the sense used by the Medical Research Council: they have several interacting components, they depend on the behaviour of workers and supervisors, they target several levels of organisation, and their effects are likely to depend on context {{craig2008;skivington2021}}. Such interventions should not move directly to a definitive trial. MRC guidance recommends a phase of development and feasibility testing that examines whether the intervention can be delivered, whether people will take part, whether outcomes can be measured and whether progression to a full trial is justified {{skivington2021}}. A process evaluation is integral to this phase, because it shows how the intervention was implemented, through what mechanisms it might work and how context shaped it {{moore2015}}. Paper 3 is a feasibility study of this kind for a heat-stress management programme in construction.

### 1.4.4 Why the three gaps are connected

The three gaps are presented separately for clarity, but they are not independent, and the order in which this thesis addresses them reflects their dependence on one another. Uncertainty about the size of the effect (the first gap) is partly a consequence of poor exposure measurement (the second), because misclassified exposure dilutes associations and inflates heterogeneity between studies that measured temperature in different ways. Uncertainty about the threshold at which risk begins, in turn, makes it hard to design protection (the third gap): a programme that triggers rest breaks at a WBGT above which risk is already elevated will leave a band of hazardous days unprotected. Conversely, the absence of intervention studies means that even a precise estimate of risk tells a regulator little about what to require of employers.

There is also a sense in which the three gaps reflect the way the field has framed the problem. Heat has largely been studied as an environmental exposure acting on individual bodies. That framing directs attention to the weather and to physiology, and it makes the weather station a natural source of exposure data and the individual worker a natural unit of analysis. It directs attention away from the workplace as a setting with its own conditions, and from the organisational and economic arrangements that determine whether an individual worker can act on what they know about heat. An alternative framing, in which heat is an occupational hazard shaped by the design of work {{lucas2014}}, implies different priorities: measuring exposure where work happens, examining how the organisation of work modifies risk, and evaluating protection at the level at which work is organised, namely the crew, the site and the employer. The three papers can be read as successive steps from the first framing towards the second.

This progression was not entirely planned at the outset. The original doctoral proposal envisaged a review followed by two analyses of the claims data. The finding in Paper 1 that interventions had scarcely been studied, and the finding in Paper 2 that piece-rate work modified the effect of heat, together made a case for moving from describing risk to testing protection, and the third study was redesigned accordingly. The bridging chapters between the papers explain these decisions as they were made.

## 1.5 Aim and research questions

The aim of this thesis is to strengthen the evidence on how occupational heat exposure affects the health of outdoor workers and to establish what would be required to protect them, by synthesising the existing evidence, estimating heat-related injury risk with worksite-level exposure, and testing the feasibility of a workplace heat-stress management programme.

Four research questions follow from this aim. The first three correspond to the three papers; the fourth is answered by reading the papers together in Chapter 6.

**RQ1.** What is the strength and certainty of the evidence linking occupational heat exposure to injury and to kidney-related outcomes in outdoor workers, and what gaps does that evidence leave? (Paper 1, Chapter 3)

**RQ2.** How does the daily risk of occupational injury among agricultural and construction workers vary with worksite-level heat exposure, over what lags does the effect operate, and is it modified by age, sector and payment system? (Paper 2, Chapter 4)

**RQ3.** Is a multi-component heat-stress management programme for construction crews feasible and acceptable, can it be delivered with fidelity, and what factors shape its implementation? (Paper 3, Chapter 5)

**RQ4.** Taken together, what do these studies imply about the nature of occupational heat risk and the conditions under which workers can be protected from it? (Chapter 6)

## 1.6 Conceptual framework

The thesis is organised around a simple conceptual framework, shown in Figure 1.1, that links environmental conditions to health outcomes through the body and through the organisation of work. The framework draws on the physiological account of heat balance {{parsons2014}} and on the ergonomic view of occupational heat as a hazard shaped by the design of work as well as by the weather {{lucas2014;kjellstrom2016}}.

> **Figure 1.1** Conceptual framework of the thesis. Environmental heat (air temperature, humidity, radiant heat, wind; summarised as WBGT at the worksite) and metabolic heat from physical work combine with clothing to produce heat stress → heat strain (core temperature, heart rate, dehydration) → acute outcomes (heat-related illness, impaired performance, injury) and, with repetition, possible kidney harm. Work organisation (pace, payment system, break schedules, supervision, crew culture) acts at two points: it sets the metabolic load and it governs whether protective behaviours (rest, shade, hydration, mutual monitoring) can interrupt the path from stress to strain. Individual factors (age, acclimatisation, experience) modify the path from stress to strain. Paper 1 maps the evidence across the whole chain; Paper 2 estimates the link from worksite WBGT to injury and its modification by age and payment; Paper 3 intervenes on protective behaviours and examines how work organisation shapes them.

The framework makes two claims that are central to the argument developed across the thesis. The first is that exposure should be characterised where work takes place, because that is where heat stress is generated and where it must be managed. The second is that work organisation sits on the causal pathway, not merely in the background. If a worker cannot slow down or rest without losing income, the physiological protection afforded by rest is unavailable to them, however well understood it is. This second claim is a hypothesis rather than an assumption: Paper 2 tests whether payment system modifies the effect of heat on injury, and Paper 3 examines whether and how work organisation constrains the uptake of protection.

The framework also clarifies what the thesis does not attempt. It does not measure core temperature or other markers of physiological strain in large populations, it does not estimate long-term kidney outcomes prospectively, and it does not evaluate individual cooling technologies such as cooling vests. These are important questions, but they lie outside the scope that a single doctoral programme could address well.

## 1.7 Research approach

### 1.7.1 A programme of linked studies

The three studies use different designs because they answer different questions. A systematic review was the appropriate first step because a review of reviews would not have provided the pooled, exposure-specific estimates needed, and because the shape of the existing evidence would determine where new primary research could contribute most. A case-crossover design was chosen for Paper 2 because it compares each injured worker with themselves at different times, controlling by design for stable individual characteristics such as sex, underlying health and occupation, and, with time-stratified referent selection, for long-term trends and seasonality. This makes it well suited to estimating the short-term effects of a transient exposure on an acute outcome. A mixed-methods feasibility study was chosen for Paper 3 because the question at that stage was not whether the programme reduces harm, which would require a large trial, but whether such a trial could be run and what it would need to include.

The approach is pragmatic in the philosophical sense: methods were chosen to fit the questions, and quantitative and qualitative data were treated as complementary sources of evidence rather than as expressions of competing worldviews. In Paper 3 in particular, the quantitative feasibility indicators show what happened, and the qualitative interviews and focus groups help to explain why. The qualitative analysis used reflexive thematic analysis {{braun2006}}, reported against the COREQ checklist {{tong2007}}, with sample adequacy judged by information power rather than by a fixed number of interviews {{malterud2016}}.

Causal thinking runs throughout the programme, even though none of the studies is a randomised trial. In Paper 2, confounders were identified using a directed acyclic graph rather than by statistical selection {{greenland1999}}, and the analysis was reported according to STROBE {{vonelm2007}}. In Paper 3, the recommendations for future work draw on the idea of specifying the target trial that an observational analysis would aim to emulate {{hernan2016}}, so that any follow-up using routinely collected data can be designed to answer a clearly defined causal question.

### 1.7.2 Integration across the programme

Mixed methods are often discussed at the level of a single study, but in this thesis integration also happens across studies. Three points of integration were planned. The first is sequential: the findings of each paper inform the design of the next. The review's identification of weather-station exposure as a principal source of uncertainty shaped the exposure model in Paper 2, and the review's use of 28 °C WBGT as a reference threshold, reflecting common practice in the included studies, was tested rather than assumed in Paper 2's flexible exposure–response function. Paper 2's finding of effect modification by payment system shaped both the components of the intervention in Paper 3 and the questions asked in its interviews and focus groups.

The second point of integration is interpretive. Paper 2 can show that piece-rate agricultural work carries a greater heat-related injury risk, but administrative data cannot show why. Paper 3, though conducted in a different sector, provides qualitative evidence about how payment shapes decisions to rest, which offers a plausible mechanism for the quantitative finding. The two sectors differ, and the thesis does not treat construction interviews as direct evidence about agricultural workers. It does treat them as evidence about how payment and supervision interact with heat in outdoor manual work more generally, and Chapter 6 considers how far that inference can be carried.

The third point of integration is evaluative. The certainty ratings produced by GRADE in Paper 1 provide a common language for judging what each subsequent study adds. A single well-conducted case-crossover study cannot by itself move the certainty of the injury evidence from low to high, and a non-randomised feasibility study cannot establish effectiveness. Chapter 6 uses the GRADE domains as a framework for considering how the thesis as a whole changes the state of knowledge, and where the remaining uncertainty lies.

These forms of integration require that terms, measures and thresholds be used consistently across the papers. WBGT is used as the exposure metric in all three studies, and where included studies in Paper 1 reported only air temperature, this is stated. Injury is defined as an incident causing physical harm at work; in Paper 2 it is operationalised as an accepted compensation claim, and in Paper 3 it is not used as an outcome, because a feasibility study of six crews could not detect differences in injury. Paper 3 instead records heat-related symptom reports per 100 shifts and end-of-shift urine specific gravity, both as exploratory indicators, as described in Chapter 5.

### 1.7.3 Why a thesis by publication

Kestrel Bay University permits a doctoral thesis to be submitted as a series of papers framed by introductory and concluding chapters. I chose this format for three reasons. First, the three studies are methodologically distinct and each stands on its own as a contribution; writing them as papers obliged me to make each study's question, methods and findings intelligible without the rest of the thesis. Second, peer review of Paper 1 during candidature improved the later studies: reviewers' comments on exposure classification shaped the exposure estimation in Paper 2. Third, the findings are of practical interest to regulators, employers and unions, and publication is the most direct route to them.

The format also has costs, which I have tried to manage. Papers written for different journals repeat some background material, and their word limits constrain the depth of discussion. Chapters 1 and 2 therefore provide the fuller context and methodological rationale that the papers could not, and the bridging chapters between the papers explain the logic connecting each study to the next. Chapter 6 is not a summary of the three discussions but an attempt to say something that none of the papers could say on its own.

## 1.8 Study setting

Papers 2 and 3 were conducted in the Coastal Region, a jurisdiction with a warm, humid summer, a substantial agricultural sector producing fruit and vegetables, and a construction sector that expanded considerably during the study period. The region has a single public workers' compensation insurer, the Coastal Region Workers' Compensation Board, which provides no-fault cover for work-related injury and holds claims data that record the date of injury, the employer's industry, the worker's age and the location of the workplace. This combination of a single insurer, a recorded injury date and a geocodable workplace made the case-crossover analysis in Paper 2 possible.

Agricultural work in the region is seasonal and includes a substantial share of piece-rate harvesting, particularly in tree fruit and berries. Construction work is more evenly distributed across the year but intensifies in summer. Workplace heat guidance in the region at the time of the studies recommended that employers assess heat risk and provide water, shade and rest, but did not prescribe WBGT-based work–rest schedules or address payment arrangements. The regulatory context is described more fully in Chapter 2.

The region's administrative data have limitations that should be acknowledged from the outset. Compensation claims capture injuries that are reported and accepted, and under-reporting is likely to be greater among workers on insecure contracts, migrant workers and those paid by the piece, precisely the groups of most interest here [source needed]. The workplace address recorded on a claim is usually the employer's registered site, which for agricultural and construction work may differ from the field or building site where the injury occurred. And claims record the industry but not the specific task or the payment arrangement of the individual worker; piece-rate status in Paper 2 therefore had to be derived from information beyond the individual claim, and the consequences of that derivation for misclassification are examined in Chapter 4. These limitations do not undermine the case-crossover design, which is robust to many stable sources of bias, but they shape what the analysis can and cannot show.

The feasibility study in Paper 3 was conducted at three construction sites in and around the regional capital during the summer of 2025, with the cooperation of two construction companies and the regional construction union. The sites, companies and participants are described in Chapter 5, where pseudonyms are used throughout.

## 1.9 Researcher position

My interest in occupational heat began before this doctorate. I worked for several years as an occupational health adviser with a regional agricultural cooperative, where part of my role was to respond to heat-related incidents. I saw that the advice we gave, to drink water and take breaks, was widely known and frequently ignored, and that the reasons were rarely ignorance. Workers told me that they could not afford to stop. This experience shaped the questions I asked, particularly about payment and work organisation, and it is a reason for the attention given to those questions in Papers 2 and 3.

It is also a potential source of bias. I came to the research with a belief that work organisation matters, and I needed to guard against finding only what I expected. I did so in three ways. In Paper 2, the hypotheses about effect modification were specified in a protocol before the data were linked. In Paper 3, a second coder who had no fieldwork role, Dr Clara Benedetti, independently coded a subset of transcripts, and themes that contradicted my expectations were actively sought. Throughout, I kept a reflexive journal that recorded my assumptions and how they changed. My position in relation to participants in Paper 3, as a university researcher with an occupational health background, is discussed further in Chapter 5.

## 1.10 Ethics and data governance

The programme of research received ethical approval from the Kestrel Bay University Human Research Ethics Committee. Paper 1 used published data only and did not require approval. Paper 2 used de-identified administrative claims data under a data access agreement with the Coastal Region Workers' Compensation Board, which prohibited any attempt to re-identify individuals and required that cell counts below five be suppressed in published outputs. Paper 3 involved direct contact with workers and required particular care, because workers might feel pressure to take part if their employer had agreed to the study. Consent was therefore sought by the research team without managers present, participation or refusal was not reported to employers, and interviews were conducted off site or in private spaces. Urine samples were analysed for specific gravity only and discarded immediately afterwards.

A further ethical consideration arose from the non-randomised design of Paper 3. Comparison crews did not receive the full programme during the study, but all crews received the basic water and shade provisions required by regional guidance, and comparison crews were offered the supervisor training after the study ended. Details of the approvals, consent procedures and data management are given in Chapter 2.

Chapter 2 complements this introduction. It reviews the background literature in more depth than the papers permit, describes the Coastal Region setting and the data sources, and explains the methodological choices that cut across the programme, including the approach to exposure assessment, causal inference and mixed-methods integration. Its search strategy and qualitative topic guides are reproduced in Appendices A and B.

Chapter 3 presents Paper 1, the systematic review and meta-analysis. It finds that each 1 °C of WBGT above 28 °C is associated with a 5% increase in injury risk and that heat-exposed workers have higher odds of kidney-related outcomes, but that the certainty of this evidence is low for injury and very low for kidney outcomes, that exposure is rarely measured at worksites, and that protective interventions are scarcely studied. A short bridging chapter then explains why these findings led to a study of worksite-level exposure with explicit attention to lags.

Chapter 4 presents Paper 2, the case-crossover analysis of compensation claims linked to worksite WBGT. It finds that injury risk begins to rise at a lower WBGT than the review's reference threshold, that the effect is concentrated on the day of exposure, and that it is stronger among younger workers and in piece-rate agriculture. A second bridging chapter explains why these findings pointed towards an intervention that addressed work organisation, and why a feasibility study was the appropriate next step.

Chapter 5 presents Paper 3, the feasibility study of a heat-stress management programme for construction crews, combining quantitative feasibility indicators with a qualitative process evaluation. It finds that the programme was acceptable and that recruitment and retention were sufficient for a trial, but that rest breaks were the component most difficult to deliver, particularly on piece-rate days.

Chapter 6 integrates the findings across the three papers to answer RQ4, setting out the thesis's methodological, substantive and practical contributions and considering the limitations that apply across the programme. Chapter 7 concludes with direct answers to the research questions, recommendations for policy, practice and research, and reflections on the doctoral process.

## 1.12 Summary

Heat is a growing occupational hazard for outdoor workers, with plausible and partly established effects on injury and contested effects on the kidney. The evidence used to protect these workers has three weaknesses: its size and certainty have not been clearly established, its exposure measurement rarely reflects conditions where people work, and the protective measures it recommends have seldom been tested under real working conditions. This thesis addresses each weakness in turn, through a systematic review, a case-crossover study with worksite-level exposure and a feasibility study of a workplace programme, and then considers what the three studies show when read together. The central argument, developed across the chapters and stated fully in Chapter 6, is that protecting outdoor workers from heat depends as much on how work is organised and paid as on what is known about human physiology. Chapter 2 now provides the background and the methodological foundations for the studies that follow.

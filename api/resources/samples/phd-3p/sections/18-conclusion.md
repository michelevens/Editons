# Chapter 7: Conclusion

## 7.1 Introduction

This thesis set out to understand how occupational heat exposure affects the health and safety of outdoor workers, and what it would take to protect them. It approached that aim through three studies, each published or prepared as a journal manuscript: a systematic review and meta-analysis of the existing evidence (Chapter 3), a case-crossover study of worksite heat and occupational injury using linked compensation and exposure data (Chapter 4), and a mixed-methods feasibility study of a heat-stress management programme for construction crews (Chapter 5). Chapter 6 read those studies together and developed the integrated argument of the thesis. This final chapter is deliberately brief. It restates how each research question was answered, sets out the thesis's contribution to knowledge, draws together the main recommendations and closes with a reflection on what the work suggests about the direction of the field.

## 7.2 Answers to the research questions

### 7.2.1 Research question 1: what does the existing evidence show, and how certain is it?

The first question asked what the epidemiological literature shows about the relationship between occupational heat exposure and acute injury and kidney outcomes in outdoor workers, and how much confidence that evidence deserves. The systematic review in Chapter 3, conducted and reported in line with PRISMA 2020 {{page2021}}, identified 27 eligible studies, 19 of them cohort studies and 8 cross-sectional, from nine countries and including approximately 84,000 workers. For injury, the pooled relative risk was 1.05 (95% CI 1.03–1.08) per 1 °C of WBGT above 28 °C; for kidney outcomes, the pooled odds ratio comparing heat-exposed with unexposed workers was 1.47 (1.21–1.79). Both associations were consistent in direction, but heterogeneity was substantial, there was evidence of small-study effects for kidney outcomes, and the certainty of evidence was rated low for injury and very low for kidney outcomes.

The answer to the first question is therefore that the association between occupational heat and harm is real but poorly characterised. The evidence base is dominated by studies that assigned exposure from weather stations, rarely modelled timing, seldom reported who was most affected and contained almost no evaluations of protective interventions. These gaps defined the agenda for the rest of the thesis.

### 7.2.2 Research question 2: how does worksite heat relate to injury, when, and for whom?

The second question asked how daily heat exposure at the worksite relates to occupational injury in agricultural and construction workers, over what lag, and for whom the risk is greatest. Chapter 4 analysed 18,406 compensation claims from 2015 to 2023, linked to worksite WBGT estimated from 46 weather stations with a land-surface correction, using a time-stratified case-crossover design with a distributed lag non-linear model {{gasparrini2010}}.

Injury risk began to rise above a WBGT of approximately 26 °C. At 31 °C, compared with 24 °C, the relative risk was 1.21 (1.12–1.31), with most of the excess at lag 0. The relative risk was higher among workers under 25 (1.34) and among piece-rate agricultural workers (1.38), and approximately 4.1% of injuries in the study population were attributable to heat. Sensitivity analyses using alternative lag structures, excluding heatwave days and applying a negative-control outcome did not overturn the main finding.

The answer to the second question is that heat-related injury risk at the worksite is immediate, begins at moderate levels of heat and falls disproportionately on young workers and on those whose pay depends on output.

### 7.2.3 Research question 3: is a heat-stress management programme feasible, and what would a trial need?

The third question asked whether a multi-component heat-stress management programme for construction crews was feasible and acceptable, and what would need to change before it could be tested in a definitive trial. Chapter 5 reported a non-randomised feasibility study, developed and evaluated in line with MRC guidance on complex interventions {{craig2008;skivington2021}} and its guidance on process evaluation {{moore2015}}, involving six crews and 74 workers at three sites over eight weeks in summer 2025.

The programme was feasible to deliver and acceptable to most workers: 89% of eligible workers consented. Fidelity, measured as the proportion of scheduled breaks taken, was 71% overall but only 52% on piece-rate days. Exploratory outcome measures moved in the expected direction: heat-symptom reports per 100 shifts fell from 9.8 to 5.6 in intervention crews against 9.1 to 8.4 in comparison crews, and the proportion of workers with urine specific gravity of 1.020 or above at shift end fell from 46% to 31% against 44% to 41%. These comparisons were not powered and should not be read as estimates of effectiveness. The qualitative data, from 22 interviews and three focus groups, generated four themes: "breaks cost money", "the supervisor makes the weather", "water is easy, rest is hard" and "buddy checks feel like care, not policing".

The answer to the third question is that the programme is feasible, that progression criteria were met for recruitment and retention and partially met for fidelity, and that a definitive trial should be a cluster randomised trial incorporating a pay-protection component, because the shortfall in fidelity was concentrated where rest cost workers money.

### 7.2.4 The integrated answer

Each research question was answered by a single paper, but the aim of the thesis was answered only by the three together. The integrated answer, developed in Chapter 6, is that heat protection for outdoor workers is a problem of labour organisation as much as a physiological one. The evidence synthesis showed that the field could say little about who is most at risk or what protects them. The case-crossover study showed that risk is immediate and is greatest among workers whose pay depends on how much they produce. The feasibility study showed that, of the protective measures offered, those that cost workers nothing were taken up readily and the one that required them to stop working was the one that gave way under piece-rate pay. The physiological case for rest, shade and hydration is not in question {{parsons2014;lucas2014}}. What the thesis questions is the assumption, implicit in much heat guidance, that specifying these measures is enough to secure them.

This answer has a particular status. It is an inference across three studies of different designs, none of them randomised, conducted largely in one region. It is coherent, in that each study's findings are consistent with and help to explain those of the others, and it is specific enough to be tested. It is not, on its own, proof that changing pay arrangements would reduce heat-related harm. The thesis ends, appropriately, by proposing the trial that would test it.

## 7.3 Contribution to knowledge

The original contribution of the thesis lies less in any single finding than in what the three studies show together. Read in sequence, they move from a thin and uncertain evidence base, through a precise estimate of when and for whom worksite heat is dangerous, to a direct observation of why protection fails where it fails. The thread that runs through all three is work organisation. The literature on occupational heat has long recognised that heat strain carries health and productivity costs {{kjellstrom2016;flouris2018;lucas2014}} and that its effects depend on social as well as physiological conditions {{ebi2021}}. This thesis provides convergent empirical evidence, from an epidemiological interaction and from a process evaluation in the same region, that the way work is paid shapes both the risk heat poses and the uptake of the measures meant to reduce it.

Three more specific contributions follow. Methodologically, the thesis demonstrates a scalable approach to estimating worksite heat exposure for linkage with administrative injury data, shows how a negative-control outcome can be used to probe residual confounding in occupational heat epidemiology, and makes explicit an adaptation of ROBINS-I {{sterne2016}} for environmental exposure studies that, combined with GRADE {{guyatt2008}}, yields transparent certainty ratings. Substantively, it identifies piece-rate pay and younger age as modifiers of heat-related injury risk and locates most of that risk on the day of exposure. Practically, it makes the case that pay protection belongs in workplace heat plans, and it supplies the feasibility parameters and progression evidence needed to design a definitive trial.

## 7.4 Recommendations

The recommendations of the thesis are set out in full in Sections 6.7 and 6.8. In summary, for employers, regulators and those who write heat guidance, the work suggests four things. First, heat plans should address how rest is to be protected for workers paid by output, because a schedule that workers cannot afford to follow will not protect them. Second, supervisors should be supported, and assessed, in ways that make enforcing rest compatible with their other responsibilities. Third, protective action should be triggered by conditions at the worksite during the working day rather than only by declared heatwaves, and thresholds derived from station-based data should be applied with caution. Fourth, low-cost and well-accepted measures such as hydration stations and buddy checks should be adopted widely, but not treated as a substitute for rest.

For researchers, the priorities are a cluster randomised trial of the programme with a pay-protection component, accompanied by a process evaluation that measures fidelity by pay arrangement; the use of target trial emulation {{hernan2016}} to evaluate pay-protection policies where randomisation is not possible; validation of worksite exposure estimates against on-site measurement; prospective cohorts with repeated renal measures to address the very low certainty of the kidney evidence; and studies of the mechanisms behind young workers' higher risk. More broadly, occupational heat research would benefit from routine lag modelling, negative-control analyses, explicit treatment of heterogeneity {{higgins2002}} and consistent reporting against established guidelines {{vonelm2007;tong2007}}.

## 7.5 The limits of these conclusions

The limitations of the programme are discussed in Section 6.6, and four of them bear directly on how far the conclusions above can be taken. The first is exposure. All three studies used WBGT, an environmental index that does not capture metabolic heat or clothing {{budd2008}}, and in Chapter 4 it was estimated rather than measured at the worksite. The land-surface correction is an improvement on station-based assignment, but until it is validated against on-site measurement the threshold of approximately 26 °C should be treated as specific to this region and this method.

The second is outcome ascertainment. Compensation claims capture only reported and accepted injuries, and under-reporting may be greatest among exactly the workers in whom effect modification was found. Heat symptoms in Chapter 5 were self-reported by workers who knew which programme they were receiving. Both limitations could bias estimates, though not always in a predictable direction.

The third is causal inference. The piece-rate finding rests on an epidemiological interaction and on process and qualitative data from a small, non-randomised study. Pay structure is likely to be bound up with other features of work, such as employer size, contract type and migrant status, that could also shape heat risk. The conclusion that pay organisation matters is therefore stronger than any conclusion about which specific change to pay would help most.

The fourth is scope. The kidney strand of the thesis did not progress beyond evidence synthesis, so the conclusions about prevention apply to injury and heat symptoms rather than to kidney outcomes. The work was also conducted in a single coastal region with a formal compensation system, and its findings may not transfer directly to settings where outdoor work is largely informal.

None of these limitations undermines the central argument, but each marks a boundary around it, and each points to a piece of further work identified in Section 6.8.

## 7.6 Reflections on the programme of work

Writing a thesis by publication has shaped this work in ways worth acknowledging. The format encouraged each study to stand on its own, with its own question, methods and conclusions, and that discipline improved the individual papers. It also made the connections between them more visible than they might have been in a monograph, because each bridge chapter had to justify the next study by reference to what the last had found. The decision to measure fidelity separately on piece-rate days in Chapter 5, for instance, came directly from the effect modification observed in Chapter 4. The cost of the format is some repetition across chapters, and a degree of temporal unevenness: the review in Chapter 3 reflects the literature to March 2024, while the feasibility study was conducted in 2025.

The programme also taught me something about the limits of any single method. The case-crossover design could show that piece-rate workers were at greater risk on hot days but could not say why. The interviews and focus groups could say why, in workers' own terms, but could not say how much it mattered for injury across a region. Neither alone would have supported the central claim of the thesis with any confidence. Together, and with appropriate caution about what each can bear, they make a case that I believe is both well founded and useful.

Working across methods also changed how I understand the relationship between numbers and accounts. Early in the programme I regarded the qualitative component of Chapter 5 mainly as a way of explaining the quantitative feasibility outcomes. By the end, the relationship ran in both directions: the theme "breaks cost money" made sense of a fidelity figure, but the fidelity figure also gave that theme a weight that the interviews alone could not. A process evaluation designed in this way {{moore2015}}, with its measures chosen in light of earlier epidemiological findings, seems to me one of the more useful lessons of the thesis for others planning programmes of applied health research.

## 7.7 Closing statement

Heat is usually discussed as a matter of weather and physiology: how hot it is, how the body responds and what limits it can tolerate {{parsons2014;budd2008}}. Those questions matter, and the physiological account explains why heat harms outdoor workers and why rest, shade and water should help. What this thesis adds is evidence that whether workers actually rest, drink and shelter depends heavily on the conditions of their work, above all on whether stopping costs them pay and on whether the person running the crew makes stopping possible. As outdoor work becomes hotter {{kjellstrom2016;spector2019}}, protecting workers will require attention to the organisation of work as well as to the thermal environment. The definitive trial proposed here is one way of testing whether that attention, made concrete as pay protection within a heat plan, delivers the protection that the physiology promises.

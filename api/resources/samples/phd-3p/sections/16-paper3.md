# Chapter 5: Paper 3 — A Heat-Stress Management Programme for Construction Crews: A Feasibility Study

> **Publication status.** In preparation for submission to the (fictional) *Journal of Workplace Health Interventions*. Authors: Rao NS, Haddad L, Mwangi E, Benedetti C. The manuscript is presented here as prepared for submission, with section numbering and table and figure numbering adapted to the thesis. References are consolidated in the thesis reference list. The candidate's contribution to this paper is set out in the Statement of Contribution.

This chapter reports the third and final study of the thesis. Chapter 3 found that the published evidence linking occupational heat to injury and kidney outcomes was consistent in direction but of low or very low certainty, and that almost no studies had evaluated protective interventions. Chapter 4 showed, using worksite-level exposure estimates, that injury risk in the Coastal Region rose steeply above a wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) of 26 °C, acted mainly on the day of exposure, and was concentrated among workers under 25 years and those paid by piece rate. As argued in the second Bridge, these findings point away from a purely physiological framing of heat protection and towards the organisation of work: if risk arrives on the same day and falls hardest on those whose pay depends on output, then protection depends on whether the working day can actually make room for rest. The study reported here tests whether a programme built on that premise can be delivered, accepted and evaluated on real construction sites, and what would need to change before a definitive trial.

## Abstract

**Background.** Outdoor construction workers face rising occupational heat exposure, yet evidence on workplace interventions to reduce heat strain is sparse. Work–rest schedules are a central recommendation of heat-stress guidance, but whether they are followed in practice, particularly where pay is linked to output, is largely unknown. We assessed the feasibility and acceptability of a multicomponent heat-stress management programme for construction crews and identified the conditions that a definitive trial would need to address.

**Methods.** We conducted a non-randomised mixed-methods feasibility study with a nested process evaluation, developed using Medical Research Council guidance on complex interventions. Six crews (n = 74 workers) at three construction sites in the Coastal Region took part over eight weeks in summer 2025: four crews received the programme and two acted as comparison crews. The programme comprised WBGT-triggered work–rest schedules, shaded rest areas, hydration stations, buddy checks and supervisor training. Feasibility outcomes were recruitment, retention, fidelity and data completeness, assessed against pre-specified progression criteria. Exploratory outcomes were self-reported heat symptoms per 100 shifts and shift-end urine specific gravity (USG). Qualitative data comprised 22 semi-structured interviews and three focus groups with workers, supervisors and site managers, analysed thematically.

**Results.** Of eligible workers, 89% consented and 92% were retained to the end of follow-up. Scheduled breaks were taken on 71% of occasions overall, falling to 52% on days when crews were paid by piece rate. Heat-symptom reports per 100 shifts fell from 9.8 to 5.6 in intervention crews and from 9.1 to 8.4 in comparison crews; the proportion of shift-end USG samples at or above 1.020 fell from 46% to 31% and from 44% to 41%, respectively. These comparisons were exploratory and the study was not powered to detect effects. Four themes described how the programme worked in practice: "breaks cost money", "the supervisor makes the weather", "water is easy, rest is hard" and "buddy checks feel like care, not policing".

**Conclusions.** A WBGT-triggered heat-stress programme was acceptable and could be delivered and evaluated on construction sites, meeting progression criteria for recruitment and retention and partially meeting the criterion for fidelity. The loss of fidelity on piece-rate days indicates that rest cannot be protected while it remains unpaid. A cluster randomised trial is warranted, provided the programme incorporates a pay-protection component.

**Keywords:** occupational heat stress; construction; work–rest schedules; feasibility study; process evaluation; piece rate; complex intervention.

## 5.1 Introduction

Heat is among the most widespread physical hazards of outdoor work, and its importance is growing as hot days become more frequent and more intense {{kjellstrom2016;ebi2021}}. Workers in construction and agriculture carry a particular share of this burden because their work is physically demanding, often performed in direct sun, and organised around deadlines and output targets that leave little discretion over pace {{lucas2014}}. A synthesis of field and laboratory studies found that occupational heat strain is associated with a range of adverse health outcomes and with lost productivity {{flouris2018}}, and a review focused on injury reached a consistent conclusion that hot conditions increase the risk of occupational injury {{spector2019}}. The systematic review reported in Chapter 3 of this thesis extended that picture to kidney-related outcomes and quantified the association with injury, while also showing how weak the evidence base remains: the pooled estimates rested on studies that mostly measured exposure at distant weather stations, and certainty was rated low for injury and very low for kidney outcomes.

Two features of that literature shaped the present study. The first is the near-absence of intervention research. Of the 27 studies in the review, none evaluated a workplace programme to reduce heat exposure or its consequences, and the broader heat-health literature, while rich in physiological recommendations, says comparatively little about whether those recommendations survive contact with the working day {{ebi2021}}. The second feature is the growing evidence that heat-related harm is shaped by the organisation of work and not by temperature alone. In the case-crossover analysis reported in Chapter 4, the relative risk of injury on hot days was highest among workers aged under 25 and among agricultural workers paid by piece rate. Piece-rate payment creates a direct financial cost for every minute spent resting, and young workers are less likely to have the seniority or confidence to stop work when they feel unwell. Both observations suggest that heat protection depends on what workers are able, permitted and paid to do.

The physiological basis for heat-stress management is well established. Heat strain arises when metabolic heat production and environmental heat gain exceed the body's capacity to dissipate heat, and the main levers available at work are reducing metabolic load, reducing environmental heat gain, and supporting the body's own cooling through hydration and rest in cooler conditions {{parsons2014}}. The WBGT index integrates air temperature, humidity, radiant heat and air movement into a single value and remains the most widely used basis for work–rest guidance, despite well-documented limitations, including its insensitivity to the effects of clothing and its imperfect representation of evaporative cooling in very humid conditions {{budd2008}}. Work–rest schedules triggered by WBGT thresholds therefore have a reasonable physiological rationale. What is not known is whether they can be implemented on a construction site, where work is sequenced across trades, concrete pours and crane slots are fixed in advance, supervisors are measured on progress, and some crews are paid by the square metre.

These questions are characteristic of complex interventions: programmes with several interacting components, that require changes in behaviour by several groups, and whose effects depend heavily on context {{craig2008}}. The updated Medical Research Council (MRC) framework emphasises that, before a definitive evaluation, researchers should develop a programme theory, engage stakeholders, identify key uncertainties, and test feasibility, including whether the intervention can be delivered as intended and whether an evaluation design is workable {{skivington2021}}. Process evaluation, which examines implementation, mechanisms of impact and context, is central to that task because it explains why a programme does or does not work in a particular setting {{moore2015}}. A feasibility study that reports only recruitment rates would miss the most important uncertainty in this field, which is whether rest is taken when the schedule says it should be.

This study therefore aimed to assess the feasibility and acceptability of a WBGT-triggered heat-stress management programme for construction crews, and to identify the modifications needed before a definitive trial. The specific objectives were to:

1. estimate recruitment, retention and data completeness against pre-specified progression criteria;
2. measure the fidelity with which the programme's components, and in particular scheduled work–rest breaks, were delivered, and identify the conditions under which fidelity fell;
3. explore changes in heat-symptom reports and shift-end hydration status in intervention and comparison crews, to inform outcome selection and sample size for a future trial; and
4. understand, from the perspectives of workers, supervisors and site managers, how the programme was received, how it interacted with site routines and pay arrangements, and what would need to change.

## 5.2 Methods

### 5.2.1 Design and reporting

We conducted a non-randomised, controlled, mixed-methods feasibility study with a nested process evaluation. The design was convergent: quantitative feasibility and exploratory outcome data and qualitative interview and focus-group data were collected over the same period, analysed separately, and then integrated to address each objective. The study corresponds to the feasibility and evaluation phase of the MRC framework {{skivington2021}}, following an earlier development phase described in Section 5.2.3. Allocation was not randomised because the participating employers were willing to host the programme only on sites where the site manager had agreed to lead it, and because a feasibility study of this size could not have supported a meaningful randomised comparison at cluster level. The comparison crews were included to test the logistics of collecting outcome data from crews not receiving the programme, and to give a first indication of secular change in the outcomes over the summer, not to estimate effectiveness.

Reporting of the qualitative component follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) {{tong2007}}. The completed checklist is held with the study records. The interview and focus group guides are reproduced in Appendix B.

### 5.2.2 Setting and participants

The study took place in the Coastal Region, the same region from which the workers' compensation claims analysed in Chapter 4 were drawn. Construction work in the region is seasonal in intensity, with the busiest period coinciding with the hottest months. We chose construction rather than agriculture for this study for practical reasons: construction crews work at fixed sites under a named supervisor for weeks at a time, which made it possible to deliver a crew-level programme and observe it, and construction contractors were willing to host the pilot. Agricultural work in the region is more dispersed and seasonal labour more mobile. Three active construction sites took part, operated by two mid-sized contractors (pseudonymised here as Contractor H and Contractor C):

- **Site 1 (Harbour Road)**, a medium-rise residential development operated by Contractor H, at which crews A and B received the programme;
- **Site 2 (Northgate)**, a civil works and drainage project operated by Contractor C, at which crews C and D received the programme; and
- **Site 3 (Linden Park)**, a commercial building project operated by Contractor H, at which crews E and F acted as comparison crews.

Placing the comparison crews on a separate site was intended to reduce contamination, since heat-stress measures introduced on one part of a site tend to be noticed and copied elsewhere. Linden Park was chosen as the comparison site because it had a similar mix of trades and work stages to Harbour Road and was run by the same contractor, so that company-level policies, pay structures and induction procedures were held constant between one intervention site and the comparison site.

A crew was defined as a group of workers under a single supervisor (a foreman or leading hand) who worked together for most of their shifts. Crews were eligible if they were expected to work outdoors on the site for the whole eight-week study period and if their supervisor agreed to take part. Six crews were invited and all six agreed. Within participating crews, workers were eligible if they were aged 18 years or over, worked at least three outdoor shifts per week, and were able to give informed consent in English or in one of the two other languages commonly spoken on the sites, for which translated materials and interpreters were provided. Workers employed through labour-hire agencies and subcontractors were eligible on the same terms as direct employees, since excluding them would have removed many of the youngest and most precariously employed workers from the study. Two crews (B at Harbour Road and D at Northgate) included subcontracted blocklaying and pipe-laying teams who were paid by piece rate on some days, depending on the stage of work and the terms of the subcontract that week. Crew F at the comparison site included a subcontracted formwork team paid on a similar basis.

Supervisors, site managers and the contractors' safety advisers were invited to take part in interviews and focus groups, as were consenting workers. Qualitative sampling is described in Section 5.2.9.

### 5.2.3 Intervention development

The programme was developed between October 2024 and April 2025 through three linked activities. First, we drew on the findings of the systematic review (Chapter 3) and the case-crossover analysis (Chapter 4) to identify the exposure range at which protection was most needed and the groups at highest risk. Second, we reviewed established guidance on thermal environments and heat strain {{parsons2014;lucas2014}} to identify the physiological levers that a site programme could plausibly influence. Third, we held two co-design workshops with a stakeholder group of eleven people: four workers, two supervisors, two site managers, a safety adviser from each contractor, and a representative of a regional construction workers' union. The workshops worked through draft versions of each component, tested them against the practical constraints of site work, and agreed the WBGT thresholds and the content of supervisor training.

The workshops changed the programme in several ways. Workers argued that hydration measures alone would be welcomed but would not address the core problem, which several described as not being able to stop. Supervisors argued that any schedule had to be simple enough to run from a single reading and a laminated card, and that breaks had to be organised by crew, not by individual, so that work sequences were not broken. Site managers asked that the programme specify what would happen to scheduled tasks such as concrete pours on high-WBGT days, which led to the inclusion of a planning element in supervisor training. The union representative raised the issue of piece-rate pay at the first workshop. At that stage the contractors were not prepared to alter subcontract payment terms for a pilot, and the programme team decided to proceed without a pay-protection component and to examine its absence explicitly in the process evaluation. That decision is central to the interpretation of the findings below.

The resulting programme theory is summarised in Figure 5.1. It proposed that a visible, objective trigger (the WBGT reading) would shift the decision to rest from the individual worker to the crew schedule; that supervisor training would give supervisors the authority and the planning tools to honour the schedule; that shade, water and buddy checks would make rest more restorative and make early signs of heat strain more likely to be noticed; and that, together, these would reduce physiological heat strain and heat-related symptoms. The theory identified two contextual conditions as likely moderators: production pressure, including payment by output, and the stance of site management.

> **Figure 5.1** Programme theory and logic model. Inputs (WBGT monitor and trigger card; shade structures; hydration stations; buddy pairs; supervisor training) → mechanisms (rest decision moved from individual to crew schedule; supervisor authority to pause work; peer recognition of early heat strain) → proximal outcomes (scheduled breaks taken; fluid intake; early reporting) → distal outcomes (reduced heat strain, fewer heat symptoms, better hydration status) → long-term outcomes (fewer heat-related injuries and illnesses). Contextual moderators shown alongside: production pressure and piece-rate payment; site management support; crew composition and language.

### 5.2.4 The heat-stress management programme

The programme had five components, delivered at crew level throughout the six-week intervention period (Table 5.1).

**Table 5.1** Components of the heat-stress management programme

| Component | Description | Delivered by | Fidelity indicator |
|---|---|---|---|
| WBGT-triggered work–rest schedule | WBGT measured at the work area each hour from 09:00 with a portable monitor; schedule tier read from a laminated trigger card (Table 5.2) and announced to the crew | Supervisor, with site safety adviser | Proportion of scheduled breaks taken, in full, within 10 minutes of the scheduled time |
| Shaded rest areas | Portable shade structures with seating placed within 50 m of each crew's work area; relocated as work moved | Site management | Shade available within 50 m on observation days |
| Hydration stations | Insulated cool-water containers refilled at least twice per shift, with disposable cups and electrolyte sachets; personal bottles issued | Site management | Station stocked and within 50 m on observation days |
| Buddy checks | Workers paired at the start of each shift; buddies checked each other at each break using a short prompt card (thirst, headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, cramps) | Workers, prompted by supervisor | Buddy pairs assigned and check performed at observed breaks |
| Supervisor training | Two-hour session before the intervention period and a 30-minute refresher in week 5, covering heat physiology, the trigger card, planning heavy tasks around forecast heat, recognising heat illness and first aid | Research team and safety adviser | Attendance; knowledge check at end of session |

*Note.* Observation days were two randomly selected shifts per crew per week, on which a member of the research team was present on site.

The WBGT thresholds were set to begin below the level at which Chapter 4 found injury risk to start rising, so that crews would be in a heightened state of attention before the steepest part of the risk curve. They were adapted from the general structure of work–rest guidance for heat {{parsons2014}} and agreed with stakeholders at the co-design workshops; they are the programme's own and should not be read as a reproduction of any published standard. The schedules assumed moderate to heavy work, which describes most of the tasks undertaken by the participating crews. WBGT has known limitations as a guide to strain, particularly in very humid conditions and for workers in heavy protective clothing {{budd2008}}, and supervisors were trained to move a crew up one tier when workers were in full coveralls or when the crew had been working in an enclosed space.

**Table 5.2** WBGT trigger card used by supervisors

| Tier | Worksite WBGT (°C) | Work–rest pattern per hour | Additional actions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green | < 26 | Normal breaks | Water available; buddy pairs assigned |
| Amber | 26.0–27.9 | 50 min work / 10 min rest in shade | Hydration reminder at each break; buddy check at each break |
| Red | 28.0–29.9 | 45 min work / 15 min rest in shade | As amber; heaviest tasks moved to before 11:00 where possible |
| Purple | 30.0–31.9 | 30 min work / 30 min rest in shade | As red; no new heavy tasks started; supervisor checks each worker |
| Black | ≥ 32.0 | Heavy work stops; light work 15 min / 45 min rest | Site manager informed; work resumes when tier falls |

*Note.* Supervisors moved a crew up one tier when workers wore full coveralls or worked in enclosed spaces.

### 5.2.5 Comparison condition

Comparison crews continued with usual practice. At both contractors, usual practice comprised a general heat advisory issued by the head office at the start of summer, drinking water provided at the site amenities block, and discretion for supervisors to allow additional breaks. There was no measurement of WBGT, no structured schedule, and no dedicated shade beyond the amenities block. Comparison crews were offered the full programme, including shade structures and supervisor training, at the end of the study period, and both crews took this up for the remainder of the 2025 summer.

### 5.2.6 Feasibility outcomes and progression criteria

The primary feasibility outcomes were recruitment (the proportion of eligible workers who consented), retention (the proportion of consenting workers who remained in the study and contributed outcome data in the final two weeks), fidelity of the work–rest schedule, and completeness of the exploratory outcome data. Progression criteria were specified before data collection using a traffic-light system, in which green indicated that a definitive trial could proceed with the existing protocol, amber that it could proceed with modifications, and red that it should not proceed without substantial redesign (Table 5.3).

**Table 5.3** Pre-specified progression criteria

| Criterion | Green (proceed) | Amber (proceed with modification) | Red (do not proceed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recruitment: eligible workers consenting | ≥ 70% | 50–69% | < 50% |
| Retention: consenting workers contributing data in weeks 7–8 | ≥ 80% | 60–79% | < 60% |
| Fidelity: scheduled breaks taken as specified | ≥ 75% | 50–74% | < 50% |
| Data completeness: daily symptom logs returned | ≥ 80% | 60–79% | < 60% |
| Acceptability: workers rating the programme acceptable or very acceptable | ≥ 70% | 50–69% | < 50% |

Fidelity of the work–rest schedule was the most demanding criterion to measure. On each shift the supervisor recorded the hourly WBGT reading, the tier, and the times at which breaks began and ended, on a paper log designed during the workshops. On observation days, a researcher independently recorded the same information and noted whether each worker took each break. A scheduled break was counted as taken if the crew stopped work within ten minutes of the scheduled time and rested in shade for at least 80% of the scheduled duration. We compared supervisor logs with researcher observations on observation days to assess the accuracy of the logs, and we report fidelity from researcher observations as the primary estimate, since supervisor logs were expected to overstate compliance. For each observed shift we recorded whether any members of the crew were paid by piece rate that day, using information supplied by the subcontractors.

### 5.2.7 Exploratory outcomes

Two exploratory outcomes were collected to test their feasibility as trial outcomes and to give a first indication of the direction and size of any change. Occupational injury was not an outcome of this study: injuries are too rare in six crews over eight weeks to be informative, and injury is addressed in Chapters 3 and 4.

**Heat symptoms.** At the end of each shift, workers completed a brief symptom log (paper or a text-message form, at the worker's choice) recording whether they had experienced any of six symptoms during the shift: headache, dizziness or light-headedness, nausea, muscle cramps, excessive fatigue, or confusion. A heat-symptom report was defined as the report of two or more of these symptoms on a shift with a maximum worksite WBGT of 26 °C or higher. The outcome was expressed as reports per 100 worker-shifts. The two-symptom threshold was chosen in the workshops to reduce the counting of isolated headaches or tiredness unrelated to heat; it has not been validated and is treated here as a pragmatic indicator.

**Hydration status.** On observation days, workers who agreed provided a urine sample at the end of the shift, and urine specific gravity (USG) was measured on site with a handheld refractometer. Samples were analysed for specific gravity only, with no other tests, and were discarded immediately afterwards. We report the proportion of samples with USG of 1.020 or higher, a commonly used indicator of inadequate hydration. USG is influenced by diet, recent fluid intake and individual factors, and a single shift-end sample is a crude measure of hydration over a working day.

The study period comprised a two-week baseline (weeks 1–2), during which all crews followed usual practice and outcome data were collected, and a six-week intervention period (weeks 3–8), during which crews A to D received the programme. Supervisor training for intervention crews took place at the end of week 2.

### 5.2.8 Process evaluation

The process evaluation followed MRC guidance, which frames process evaluation around implementation (what was delivered and how), mechanisms of impact (how participants responded and how this produced change), and context (the external factors that shaped implementation and outcomes) {{moore2015}}. Implementation data came from the supervisor logs, observation-day records, records of training attendance, and logs kept by the site safety advisers of shade, water and equipment. Data on mechanisms and context came primarily from the qualitative component, supplemented by an acceptability questionnaire completed by workers in week 8, which asked about each component on a five-point scale from "very unacceptable" to "very acceptable". The site safety advisers and the research team also kept field notes recording events that affected the programme, such as schedule pressures, equipment failures and changes in subcontract arrangements.

### 5.2.9 Qualitative data collection and analysis

We conducted semi-structured interviews with workers, supervisors and site managers in weeks 6 to 9, and three focus groups in weeks 8 and 9: two with workers and one with supervisors. Interview participants were sampled purposively from consenting participants to include intervention and comparison crews, a range of ages, roles and lengths of experience, and workers who were and were not paid by piece rate. Workers who had been observed frequently missing breaks, and those who had reported heat symptoms, were deliberately included. Comparison-crew participants were interviewed to understand usual practice and the context in which the programme would be introduced.

Sample size was guided by information power {{malterud2016}}. The aim of the qualitative component was relatively narrow (to explain how a defined programme operated in a defined setting), the participants were highly specific to that aim, the analysis was informed by an explicit programme theory, and the interviewer had extended contact with the sites, which supported a strong dialogue. These features suggested that a modest sample would hold sufficient information power. We planned for around 20 interviews and three focus groups, and reviewed the adequacy of the data during analysis; in the final interviews no new considerations relevant to the programme theory were being raised, and the planned sample was retained.

Interviews were conducted by the candidate (NSR), a woman public health researcher with training in qualitative methods and no prior employment relationship with either contractor, either in a site office or by telephone. NSR had been present on site during observation days, which helped participants to talk concretely about the programme but may have led some to present their compliance favourably; she emphasised at each interview that she was not employed by the contractors and that nothing said would be reported to them in identifiable form. Focus groups were co-facilitated by NSR and LH. Interviews lasted between 25 and 58 minutes, and focus groups between 55 and 80 minutes. All were audio-recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim; interviews in languages other than English were conducted with an interpreter and translated during transcription.

Analysis used reflexive thematic analysis following the six phases described by {{braun2006|n}}: familiarisation, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and writing up. NSR coded all transcripts inductively, with codes then mapped onto the programme theory to examine where the data supported, extended or contradicted it. CB, who had not been involved in data collection, acted as independent second coder and coded a subset of six transcripts, not to calculate agreement but to open alternative readings; differences were discussed and used to refine theme definitions. Field notes were used to contextualise accounts. Themes were developed through repeated discussion with CB, LH and EM. Analysis was conducted in NVivo.

### 5.2.10 Quantitative analysis

Feasibility outcomes are reported descriptively as proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated by the Wilson method. Fidelity is reported overall, by crew, by WBGT tier, and by whether piece-rate payment applied on the day. Exploratory outcomes are reported for each arm and period as rates (heat-symptom reports per 100 worker-shifts) and proportions (USG ≥ 1.020). To indicate the uncertainty of the exploratory comparisons and to inform the design of a future trial, we fitted a Poisson model for symptom reports with terms for arm, period and their interaction, an offset for log worker-shifts, and cluster-robust standard errors at crew level, and estimated the intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC) for symptom reports from a mixed model with a random intercept for crew. With six clusters these estimates are highly imprecise and are reported to inform sample size calculations, not to test hypotheses. Analyses were conducted in R.

### 5.2.11 Integration

Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated at the interpretation stage using a joint display, in which each feasibility and process indicator was set alongside the qualitative findings that explained or qualified it, and a meta-inference was recorded for each. Integration was used in particular to examine the fall in fidelity on piece-rate days, since the quantitative data could show that it occurred but not why.

### 5.2.12 Ethics

The study was approved by the Kestrel Bay University Human Research Ethics Committee (fictional reference KBU-HREC-2025-041). Both contractors agreed in writing that participation or non-participation, and anything said in interviews, would have no bearing on employment or subcontract decisions. Workers gave written informed consent; information sheets and consent forms were available in English and the two other site languages. Participants were told that the study did not replace their employer's duty to protect them from heat and that they should stop work and seek help if they felt unwell, regardless of the schedule. Any worker with a shift-end USG of 1.030 or higher, or reporting three or more symptoms, was advised to rehydrate and to report to the site first aider. Supermarket vouchers were offered to interview and focus group participants in recognition of their time, outside working hours.

## 5.3 Results

### 5.3.1 Participants and heat conditions

Across the six crews, 83 workers met the eligibility criteria at the start of the study, of whom 74 (89%) consented: 49 of 55 eligible workers in the four intervention crews and 25 of 28 in the two comparison crews (Figure 5.2). The nine workers who declined most often said that they expected to move to another site during the summer (n = 4) or did not wish to provide urine samples, not appreciating that this was optional (n = 3); two gave no reason. Sixty-eight participants (92%) remained in the study and contributed data in weeks 7 and 8: 45 of 49 in intervention crews and 23 of 25 in comparison crews. Of the six who left, three left their employer, two were transferred to sites outside the study, and one withdrew without giving a reason. No crew withdrew.

WBGT was not monitored at the comparison site as part of usual practice; readings taken by the research team on observation days at Linden Park were similar to those at Harbour Road on the same days.

Data completeness was lower for USG than for symptom logs. On observation days, about half of the workers present provided a shift-end urine sample (Section 5.3.4), and uptake was lower among workers who left site immediately at the end of the shift to travel home or to a second job. Supervisor logs were returned for 97% of intervention-crew shifts and were complete for WBGT readings and tier on 93% of those, but they overstated adherence: on observation days, supervisors recorded 88% of scheduled breaks as taken, compared with 71% in researcher observations.

### 5.3.3 Implementation and fidelity

All four intervention supervisors and two relief leading hands attended the initial training session, and five of the six attended the refresher in week 5. The mean score on the end-of-session knowledge check was 8.6 out of 10. Shade structures were in place within 50 metres of the crew's work area on 91% of observation-day checks, and hydration stations were stocked and within 50 metres on 96%. The main reason for missing shade was that the crew had moved to a new work area and the structure had not yet followed; at Northgate, where crews worked along a linear drainage trench, this occurred more often than at Harbour Road. Buddy pairs were assigned at the start of 83% of observed shifts, and a buddy check was observed at 58% of observed breaks, more often at red tier and above than at amber.

Fidelity of the work–rest schedule varied systematically by crew, by tier and, most markedly, by payment arrangement (Table 5.6). The two crews without piece-rate workers (A and C) took 79% of scheduled breaks, whereas the two crews that included piece-rate teams (B and D) took 63%. Within crews B and D, breaks scheduled on days when the piece-rate team was working took place on 52% of occasions, compared with 75% on days when the same crews were paid by the hour. Across all four crews, fidelity was 78% on non-piece-rate days and 52% on piece-rate days. Fidelity also declined as the schedule became more demanding: it was highest at amber tier, where breaks were short, and lowest at purple tier, where the schedule asked crews to rest for half of every hour. At black tier, heavy work stopped as specified on all but one of the five observed occasions, but the light-work schedule was followed less consistently.

### 5.3.4 Exploratory outcomes

Heat-symptom reports per 100 worker-shifts fell from 9.8 at baseline to 5.6 during the intervention period in intervention crews, and from 9.1 to 8.4 in comparison crews (Table 5.7). In the Poisson model, the ratio of the change in symptom rate in intervention crews to that in comparison crews was 0.62 (95% CI 0.38–1.01). These findings should be read as an indication that the outcomes can be collected and that they move in the expected direction, not as evidence that the programme was effective. Seasonal acclimatisation, changes in the type of work as projects progressed, and differences between sites could each account for part of the pattern, and a regression-to-the-mean contribution cannot be excluded in crews selected partly because their managers were concerned about heat.

### 5.3.5 Qualitative findings

Twenty-two semi-structured interviews were conducted: with 15 workers (11 from intervention crews and four from comparison crews), five supervisors (three intervention, two comparison) and two site managers. Seven of the interviewed workers were paid by piece rate on some days, and six were aged under 25. Three focus groups were held: two with workers (Harbour Road, six participants; Northgate, seven participants) and one with supervisors and leading hands from the intervention crews (five participants). Some focus-group participants had also been interviewed. We developed four themes, which are presented below with illustrative quotations; participants are identified by a code, role, and where relevant age or payment arrangement.

#### Theme 1: "Breaks cost money"

For workers paid by piece rate, the schedule asked them to give up income, and most were clear that this was the central obstacle. They did not dispute the purpose of the breaks and many described feeling the effects of heat, but they counted rest in metres of pipe or blocks laid.

> Fifteen minutes every hour, that's a quarter of the day. Nobody's paying me for a quarter of the day sitting under a tent. I know it's for us, I'm not stupid, but the rent doesn't know it's hot. (W11, blocklayer, piece rate, 27)

> On hourly days I sit down with everyone. On metre days, I drink while I'm working and I keep going. If I stop, the boys on the team are waiting for me, so it's not just my money. (W14, pipe-layer, piece rate, 33)

The second quotation identifies a feature of piece-rate work that the programme theory had not anticipated: teams were paid collectively for their output, so a worker who rested imposed a cost on colleagues. Several described an informal pressure to keep pace that operated through the team, not through the supervisor. Younger workers, in particular, described being reluctant to be the person who stopped.

> I'm the youngest on the gang. If I'm the one sitting down, that's the first thing they'll say. So I wait for someone else to go first. (W07, labourer on piece-rate team, 22)

Younger workers and those new to their crews described taking their cue on resting from supervisors and senior workers: they rested when the supervisor or the most experienced members of the crew rested, and kept working when they did not. This dependence links the first theme to the second.

Hourly-paid workers in the same crews were aware of the difference and sometimes uncomfortable with it. Supervisors of mixed crews described the schedule as straightforward to run on hourly days and as something they could recommend but not require on piece-rate days, since the subcontractor, not the supervisor, controlled the pay terms. Site managers acknowledged the problem and attributed it to subcontract arrangements outside their control.

> I can tell them to stop, and on the red days I do, but I can't make the sub pay them for it. So they nod and then they're back on the trench. (S4, supervisor, crew D)

#### Theme 2: "The supervisor makes the weather"

Workers across all crews described the supervisor's stance as the main determinant of whether the schedule meant anything. The phrase that gave this theme its name came from the Harbour Road focus group, where a worker explained that the WBGT reading told you how hot it was but the supervisor decided whether it counted. Where supervisors announced the tier, called the break and sat down with the crew, workers described rest as legitimate; where supervisors treated the card as advisory, workers read this as a signal that production came first.

> The number's the number, but it's [the supervisor] who makes the weather on this site. If he stops and sits down, we all sit down. If he looks at his watch and says "you lot go if you want", nobody goes. (FG1, worker, Harbour Road)

Supervisors themselves described being caught between the programme and the expectations of site management. Several said the training had given them confidence to stop work, and some valued having an objective reading to point to when questioned by managers. The trigger card was repeatedly described as useful because it shifted responsibility from the supervisor's judgement to a rule.

> Before, if I stopped the lads, it was me being soft. Now I can say, it's purple, that's the card, and the manager signed off on the card. It takes it off my shoulders. (S1, supervisor, crew A)

However, supervisors were also clear that the support of site management varied with the programme of works. On days with concrete pours, crane bookings or inspections, the schedule was more likely to be adjusted or abandoned, and supervisors described a tacit understanding that the card applied "unless it's a pour day". The planning element of supervisor training, which encouraged moving heavy tasks to the cooler morning, was used successfully at Harbour Road for some tasks, but supervisors noted that many deadlines were set by others. The comparison-crew supervisors described usual practice in similar terms: they allowed extra breaks at their discretion, which in practice meant rarely, and only when someone appeared unwell.

> We don't have a number to go by. You look at the boys and if someone's gone grey you send him to the shed. That's the system. (S5, supervisor, comparison crew E)

#### Theme 3: "Water is easy, rest is hard"

Participants distinguished sharply between the components that asked nothing of production and those that did. Hydration stations and shade were almost universally welcomed, were described as visible evidence that the company cared, and were the components that workers most wanted to keep. Cool water close to the work area was particularly valued, compared with walking to the amenities block, and several workers said that they drank more because it was easy.

> Before, the water was in the shed, it was warm, and it was five minutes' walk. Now it's cold and it's right there. I drink twice as much, easy. (W02, labourer, 24)

Rest was another matter. Workers who were enthusiastic about water often described the schedule as the part that did not really work, not because they disagreed with it but because it collided with the pace of the job. Several described drinking as a substitute for resting: on hot piece-rate days, workers took water at the station and returned to work without sitting in the shade. Supervisors and managers made a similar distinction, describing water and shade as easy wins and the schedule as the component that would require a change in how work was planned and paid.

> Water's free and nobody loses anything. The breaks, that's where it costs, that's where you see who's serious. (M2, site manager, Northgate)

This theme helps to explain the pattern in the exploratory outcomes, where the proportion of concentrated urine samples fell in intervention crews even among workers whose break-taking was inconsistent, while symptom reports remained comparatively high on piece-rate days.

#### Theme 4: "Buddy checks feel like care, not policing"

Buddy checks had been the component that stakeholders were least sure about during the co-design workshops, with some concern that workers would find them intrusive or embarrassing. In practice they were described positively by most participants, and particularly by younger workers and workers whose main language was not English, who said that a buddy made it easier to admit to feeling unwell.

> You don't want to tell the foreman you've got a headache, he'll think you're soft. But your buddy asks you, and it's just him, so you say it. Then he says to the foreman, so it's not you complaining. (W09, labourer, 21)

> My buddy speaks my language. He asks me, are you dizzy, is your head okay, and I can tell him properly. (W16, steel fixer, 38, interviewed with interpreter)

Participants contrasted buddy checks with what they imagined monitoring by management would feel like. Several said they would have resented being checked by a supervisor or safety adviser but accepted being checked by a workmate, and described the checks as an expression of the solidarity that already existed in crews. Fidelity of buddy checks nevertheless fell away as the summer progressed, and workers acknowledged that checks were often reduced to a quick question at the water station. Two supervisors suggested that the prompt card should be cut to two or three questions to make it sustainable.

## 5.4 Discussion

### 5.4.1 Principal findings

This study found that a WBGT-triggered heat-stress management programme could be introduced on construction sites, that workers were willing to take part and to stay in the study, and that the programme was broadly acceptable. The progression criteria for recruitment and retention were met, as were those for data completeness and acceptability. The criterion for fidelity was only partially met: just under three-quarters of scheduled breaks were taken as specified, and on days when crews were paid by piece rate, only about half were. Exploratory outcomes moved in the expected direction, with a larger fall in heat-symptom reports and in the proportion of concentrated shift-end urine samples in intervention crews than in comparison crews, but the study was neither designed nor powered to test effectiveness and these differences should not be interpreted as evidence that the programme works. The qualitative findings explained the fidelity gap. Water and shade were easy to provide and to accept; rest was hard, because it cost money for piece-rate workers and their teams, and because its legitimacy depended on whether supervisors, and above them site managers, treated the schedule as binding.

### 5.4.2 Comparison with previous research

There is little directly comparable intervention research. The systematic review in Chapter 3 identified no evaluations of workplace heat-protection programmes among the 27 included studies, and reviews of occupational heat strain have drawn attention to the gap between physiological recommendations and evidence on their implementation {{flouris2018;lucas2014}}. The present findings are consistent with the argument that occupational heat protection is an ergonomic and organisational challenge, not only a matter of individual physiology {{lucas2014}}. They also align with reviews suggesting that the injury risks of heat are shaped by characteristics of work such as workload, pace and payment {{spector2019}}, and with the broader view that the burden of heat on working populations will grow with climate change unless the organisation of work adapts {{kjellstrom2016}}.

The finding that fidelity collapsed on piece-rate days connects directly with Chapter 4. In the case-crossover analysis, the relative risk of injury on hot days was highest among piece-rate agricultural workers (RR 1.38) and among workers under 25 (RR 1.34). The present study, in a different sector, suggests a mechanism that could account for part of that pattern: piece-rate workers in this sample were the least likely to take scheduled rest, and the qualitative data show why. Younger workers also described a reluctance to be the first to stop. These are observations from a small number of crews and cannot establish that reduced rest mediates the higher injury risk seen in Chapter 4, but they are consistent with it and they identify a testable hypothesis. They also suggest that the effect modification seen in Chapter 4 is not a fixed property of particular workers but a consequence of the conditions under which they work, which can in principle be changed.

The improvement in hydration status among intervention crews, even where break-taking was inconsistent, is plausible given the physiological literature, which identifies fluid replacement as one of several partially independent means of limiting heat strain {{parsons2014}}. However, the qualitative finding that some workers drank instead of resting raises a concern. Hydration alone does not prevent heat strain when metabolic heat production remains high {{parsons2014;ebi2021}}, and a programme that succeeds in increasing water intake while failing to secure rest might give a misleading impression of protection. This is one reason why a future trial should treat fidelity of the work–rest component as a key process outcome and not only as a measure of delivery.

### 5.4.3 Interpretation in relation to the programme theory

The programme theory proposed that an objective trigger would move the decision to rest from the individual to the crew schedule. The data partly support this mechanism. Supervisors valued the trigger card precisely because it made stopping work a matter of following a rule, and workers in crews whose supervisors honoured the card described rest as legitimate. But the theory underestimated two contextual factors. First, the decision to rest was not purely an individual or crew matter: for piece-rate teams it was a collective economic decision, enforced by peers whose pay depended on it. Moving the trigger from the individual to the schedule did not change that calculation, because the schedule did not change the pay. Second, the authority that the card conferred on supervisors was bounded by site management's priorities, which reasserted themselves on days with fixed production commitments. In the terms of the MRC guidance, context did not merely moderate the intervention; it determined which components could function at all {{skivington2021;moore2015}}.

These findings point to a revised programme theory in which pay protection (payment for scheduled heat breaks at a rate that compensates for lost output, or adjustment of piece-rate targets on high-WBGT days) and management commitment (written rules on scheduling heavy work and on what happens to fixed tasks when the tier rises) are included as programme components, not left as contextual conditions. The workshop decision to proceed without pay protection was pragmatic, but the findings suggest that it removed the component on which the rest schedule depended most heavily for those at greatest risk.

### 5.4.4 Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths. The programme was developed with workers, supervisors, managers and a union representative, and was grounded in the exposure–response findings of Chapter 4, so that the trigger thresholds reflected local evidence on where risk begins to rise. Fidelity was measured by independent observation, not only by supervisor report, which revealed a substantial overstatement in supervisor logs that would otherwise have gone unnoticed. The mixed-methods design allowed the main quantitative finding, the fall in fidelity on piece-rate days, to be explained, and the sample included the subcontracted and agency workers who are often excluded from workplace research.

The limitations are also substantial. Allocation was not randomised and the comparison crews were at a different site, so differences between arms in the exploratory outcomes may reflect site characteristics, types of work, or the selection of intervention sites by managers who were already concerned about heat. With six crews, the study could not provide useful estimates of between-crew variation, and the intra-cluster correlation estimated here should be treated with great caution. The heat-symptom outcome relied on self-report using an unvalidated definition, and workers in intervention crews, who had been trained to recognise heat symptoms and were asked about them by buddies, may have reported differently from comparison-crew workers; the direction of any such bias is uncertain, since training could increase recognition or create a wish to report improvement. Shift-end USG was collected from only about half of workers on observation days and is a crude marker of hydration. The presence of researchers on observation days may have increased adherence on those days, so observed fidelity may overstate fidelity on unobserved days, although the gap between supervisor logs and observations suggests that observation did not eliminate non-adherence. The study took place in a single region over one summer, with two contractors who volunteered to take part, and the findings may not transfer to smaller firms or to sites without dedicated safety advisers. Finally, the candidate conducted the interviews and was known to participants from observation days, which may have shaped what participants were willing to say about non-compliance, although the frankness of many accounts of skipped breaks suggests this effect was limited.

### 5.4.5 Implications for a definitive trial

The findings support progression to a definitive evaluation, with modifications. We recommend a cluster randomised controlled trial, with crews or sites as the unit of randomisation, to avoid contamination between workers who share work areas and supervisors. The programme for such a trial should add a pay-protection component, negotiated with contractors and subcontractors before randomisation, under which time spent in scheduled heat breaks is paid and piece-rate targets are adjusted on purple- and black-tier days. It should also include written management commitments on rescheduling fixed heavy tasks, a simplified buddy-check prompt, and procedures for relocating shade on linear worksites. Fidelity should be measured by independent observation on a random sample of shifts, with supervisor logs used only as a secondary source. Heat-symptom reports per 100 worker-shifts appear suitable as a primary outcome, given the completeness of symptom logs, with USG on a subsample and recordable injuries as secondary outcomes; the latter will require a much larger sample or linkage to compensation data of the kind used in Chapter 4. Sample size calculations should draw on the event rates observed here but should assume a more conservative intra-cluster correlation than the imprecise estimate from six crews.

A trial of this kind will not be possible in every setting, and some contractors in the Coastal Region have since begun introducing their own heat plans of varying content. This creates an opportunity for observational follow-up that complements a trial. Using linked claims and worksite WBGT data of the kind described in Chapter 4, an observational study could be designed explicitly to emulate a target trial {{hernan2016}}: specifying eligibility, the treatment strategies being compared (for example, heat plans with and without pay protection), the start of follow-up, outcomes and the causal contrast before analysis, so that the observational comparison inherits as much as possible of the logic of a randomised trial and avoids common design errors such as misaligned time zero. Such a study could not replace randomisation but could provide evidence on whether pay protection matters at a scale that a trial could not reach.

### 5.4.6 Implications for practice and policy

Although the study was not designed to evaluate effectiveness, two practical implications follow from the feasibility and process findings. First, providing water and shade, while worthwhile and well received, should not be mistaken for a heat-protection programme; the component that protects against strain under heavy work, rest, is the one most likely to be omitted in practice. Second, heat plans that set work–rest schedules without addressing how rest is paid will tend to protect hourly-paid workers more than piece-rate workers, who on the evidence of Chapter 4 may be at greater risk. Employers, principal contractors and regulators setting heat-stress requirements should consider whether the payment terms of subcontracts allow the rest that those requirements specify. These implications are tentative and would need to be confirmed in a definitive evaluation.

## 5.5 Conclusions

A WBGT-triggered heat-stress management programme for construction crews was feasible to deliver and evaluate and was acceptable to workers and supervisors. Recruitment, retention and data completeness were high, but scheduled rest was taken less often than intended, and least often when workers were paid by output. Water and shade were readily adopted; rest depended on whether pay and supervision allowed it. A cluster randomised trial is justified, provided the programme includes protection of pay during heat breaks and firm management commitments on the scheduling of heavy work, and observational studies designed to emulate a target trial could extend the evidence on these organisational components.

This chapter completes the empirical work of the thesis. Taken together with the evidence synthesis in Chapter 3 and the case-crossover analysis in Chapter 4, it suggests that protecting outdoor workers from heat depends as much on the organisation and payment of work as on physiology. Chapter 6 draws the three papers together, considers what they contribute jointly, and examines the limitations of the programme of research as a whole.

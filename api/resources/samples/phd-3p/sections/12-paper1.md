## Abstract

**Background.** Outdoor workers are exposed to environmental heat that is projected to increase with climate change. Injury and kidney outcomes are the two acute and subacute consequences of occupational heat most often studied, but the evidence has not been synthesised quantitatively with an explicit appraisal of risk of bias and certainty.

**Methods.** We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis reported according to PRISMA 2020. MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science and CINAHL were searched from inception to March 2024. We included cohort and cross-sectional studies of outdoor workers that reported a quantitative association between heat exposure, measured as wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) or ambient temperature, and occupational injury or kidney-related outcomes (acute kidney injury or reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate). Two reviewers screened, extracted and assessed risk of bias using an adaptation of ROBINS-I for exposure studies. Estimates were pooled with DerSimonian–Laird random-effects models; heterogeneity was quantified with I², small-study effects with Egger's test, and certainty with GRADE.

**Results.** Of 3,812 records, 27 studies (19 cohort, 8 cross-sectional) from 9 countries, including approximately 84,000 workers, met the inclusion criteria. Risk of occupational injury increased by 5% per 1 °C WBGT above 28 °C (pooled relative risk 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.08; 9 studies; I² = 62%). Heat-exposed workers had higher odds of kidney-related outcomes than unexposed workers (pooled odds ratio 1.47, 95% CI 1.21–1.79; 11 studies; I² = 71%). Egger's test gave p = .09 for injury and p = .04 for kidney outcomes, the latter suggesting small-study effects. Most studies assigned exposure from weather-station data rather than at the worksite, and no study evaluated a protective intervention. Certainty of evidence was low for injury and very low for kidney outcomes.

**Conclusions.** Occupational heat exposure is associated with increased risk of injury and of kidney-related outcomes in outdoor workers, but the evidence is of low to very low certainty. Studies with worksite-level exposure assessment, explicit modelling of lagged effects, and evaluations of protective interventions are needed.

**Keywords:** occupational heat stress; wet-bulb globe temperature; occupational injury; acute kidney injury; outdoor workers; meta-analysis.

## 3.1 Introduction

Outdoor work places people in direct contact with the thermal environment for long periods, often while performing physically demanding tasks and wearing clothing or protective equipment that limits heat loss. The combination of environmental heat and metabolic heat production can raise core body temperature, increase cardiovascular strain and deplete body water, with consequences that range from discomfort and reduced work capacity to heat illness and, in extreme cases, death {{parsons2014;lucas2014}}. Climate change is expected to increase both the frequency and the intensity of hot conditions in many of the regions where outdoor work is concentrated, and occupational heat exposure has been identified as one of the routes through which a warming climate is likely to affect health and economic output {{kjellstrom2016;ebi2021}}.

Two groups of outcomes have attracted particular attention in the occupational literature. The first is injury. Heat strain impairs vigilance, decision-making and fine motor control, increases fatigue, and can produce physical effects such as sweaty hands, fogged eyewear and reduced grip, all of which plausibly raise the probability of an accident {{parsons2014;spector2019}}. Because injuries are recorded routinely by employers, insurers and workers' compensation systems, they also lend themselves to large observational studies that link records to weather data. The second group is kidney-related outcomes. Sustained sweating without adequate fluid replacement reduces plasma volume and renal blood flow, and heavy exertion in the heat can cause muscle breakdown that places a further load on the kidney. Repeated episodes of this kind have been proposed as a pathway to acute kidney injury and, possibly, to longer-term loss of kidney function in heavily exposed workers {{flouris2018;ebi2021}}. Kidney outcomes test a different part of the causal chain from injury: where injury primarily reflects behavioural and cognitive effects of heat, kidney outcomes reflect its cumulative physiological cost.

Existing syntheses have established that the direction of association is consistent. A broad systematic review and meta-analysis of occupational heat strain found that working in heat-stress conditions was associated with a range of adverse health outcomes and with productivity loss {{flouris2018}}. A narrative review focused on injury concluded that heat exposure was associated with increased injury risk across a variety of settings and study designs, while noting large differences in how exposure was characterised {{spector2019}}. Neither review set out to provide a harmonised dose–response estimate for injury on a common heat-stress scale, and neither combined injury and kidney outcomes within a single framework that appraised risk of bias and certainty of evidence in a way suited to observational exposure studies.

Three methodological features of the literature motivated the present review. First, heat exposure has been measured in many different ways: as daily maximum or mean air temperature, as heat indices that combine temperature and humidity, and as WBGT, which also incorporates radiant heat and air movement and is the index most widely used in occupational heat-stress guidance {{budd2008;parsons2014}}. Estimates based on different metrics cannot be pooled without harmonisation, and harmonisation requires explicit assumptions. Second, most occupational studies have assigned exposure from meteorological stations, which may represent conditions at the worksite poorly; the resulting measurement error is likely to vary between studies and could contribute to heterogeneity. Third, tools for appraising risk of bias in non-randomised studies were designed with interventions in mind, and their application to environmental exposures requires adaptation that has rarely been made explicit.

The aims of this review were therefore (i) to estimate the association between occupational heat exposure and occupational injury in outdoor workers, expressed where possible on a common WBGT scale; (ii) to estimate the association between occupational heat exposure and kidney-related outcomes in outdoor workers; (iii) to examine sources of heterogeneity, including the method of exposure assessment; (iv) to appraise risk of bias and the certainty of the evidence; and (v) to identify gaps in the evidence, including the extent to which protective interventions have been evaluated.

## 3.2 Methods

### 3.2.1 Protocol and reporting

The review followed a protocol written before searches began and registered prospectively, before screening started, in a public register of systematic review protocols (the registration identifier is withheld in this sample). The protocol specified the eligibility criteria, outcomes, effect measures, synthesis methods and planned subgroup analyses described below. Two deviations from the protocol occurred and are reported in Section 3.2.10. The review is reported according to the PRISMA 2020 statement {{page2021}}, and a completed PRISMA checklist was submitted with the published paper.

### 3.2.2 Eligibility criteria

Eligibility was defined in terms of population, exposure, comparator, outcomes and study design, summarised in Table 3.1.

**Table 3.1** Eligibility criteria

| Element | Included | Excluded |
|---|---|---|
| Population | Adult workers whose main tasks were performed outdoors (agriculture, construction, road and utility maintenance, mixed outdoor occupations) | Indoor workers; military personnel in training; athletes; general population samples without occupational stratification |
| Exposure | Environmental heat measured or estimated as WBGT, or as ambient air temperature (daily maximum, mean or shift-time), at the worksite or from meteorological sources | Heat defined only by calendar season without a temperature measure; core or skin temperature alone (a measure of strain, not exposure) |
| Comparator | Lower levels of the same heat metric (continuous or categorical), or unexposed workers in the same population | No internal comparator |
| Outcomes | Occupational injury (recorded claims, employer records, medical records, or validated self-report); kidney-related outcomes (acute kidney injury by clinical or laboratory criteria; reduced eGFR or cross-shift creatinine rise) | Heat illness only; productivity only; chronic kidney disease ascertained without a defined link to exposure period |
| Design | Cohort (including time-series and case-crossover analyses of cohorts or registries) and cross-sectional studies reporting a quantitative effect estimate | Case reports and series; qualitative studies; reviews; modelling studies without primary data; conference abstracts without full data |

Time-series and case-crossover analyses of compensation or employer registries were classified as cohort designs, because they follow a defined working population over time and compare outcome occurrence across levels of exposure within that population. This classification was agreed in the protocol to avoid an arbitrary separation of designs that address the same question with similar data. Studies of acute kidney injury that measured creatinine before and after shifts or across a harvest season were also classified as cohort designs. No language restrictions were applied; one French-language and two Spanish-language papers were assessed with the help of fluent colleagues.

### 3.2.3 Information sources and search strategy

MEDLINE (via Ovid), Embase (via Ovid), Web of Science Core Collection and CINAHL were searched from inception to March 2024. The strategy combined three concept blocks with the Boolean operator AND: heat exposure (for example, heat, hot weather, temperature, WBGT, heat stress, heatwave), outdoor or occupational populations (for example, worker, occupational, agricultural, construction, outdoor), and the outcomes of interest (for example, injury, accident, wound, fracture, acute kidney injury, creatinine, glomerular filtration). Controlled vocabulary was used where available and adapted for each database. The full MEDLINE strategy, with the adaptations for the other databases, is given in Appendix A. Reference lists of included studies and of relevant reviews were screened, and forward citation searching was conducted for all included studies.

### 3.2.4 Study selection

Records were de-duplicated in reference management software and then screened in a web-based screening platform. Two reviewers (NSR and CB) independently screened all titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria, after a calibration exercise on 200 records. Agreement at the title and abstract stage was good (Cohen's kappa 0.82). Full texts of all records judged potentially eligible by either reviewer were retrieved and assessed independently by the same two reviewers. Disagreements at either stage were resolved by discussion, with recourse to a third reviewer (EM) for 7 full-text decisions. Where several papers reported on the same cohort, the paper with the most complete exposure–outcome data was included and the others were used for supplementary information only.

### 3.2.5 Data extraction

A standardised extraction form, piloted on five studies, was used to record study identifiers, country and setting, sector, design, period of data collection, population size and characteristics, exposure metric and its source (onsite instrument, single station, regional average or modelled grid), exposure contrast and lag, outcome definition and ascertainment, confounders adjusted for, effect measure and estimate, and any information on protective measures or interventions in place. NSR extracted all data and CB independently checked every numerical entry against the source. Authors of four studies were contacted for clarification of exposure units or for estimates not given in the paper; three replied, and the information they supplied was used.

Reporting of exposure in particular varied widely. We used the STROBE statement {{vonelm2007}} as a reference for what an observational study should report about exposure assessment, and we recorded for each study whether the source, spatial resolution and timing of exposure measurement were described. These items were not used to score studies but informed the risk-of-bias judgements on exposure classification.

### 3.2.6 Effect measures and exposure harmonisation

For injury, the primary effect measure was the relative risk (RR) per 1 °C increase in WBGT above a reference of 28 °C. This reference was chosen because it lies within the range of WBGT values at which occupational guidance commonly begins to recommend protective measures for moderate work {{parsons2014}}, and because most included WBGT studies reported thresholds or category boundaries between 26 °C and 30 °C. Rate ratios, incidence rate ratios and hazard ratios were treated as approximations of the RR, which is reasonable for an outcome that is rare on any given worker-day. Where a study reported a log-linear estimate above a threshold within the 26–30 °C range, that estimate was used directly, on the assumption that the slope above the threshold was approximately constant. Where a study reported estimates for categories of WBGT, a per-degree slope above 28 °C was approximated by fitting a log-linear trend across the category midpoints above the reference, weighted by the inverse variance of the category estimates. Where a study reported estimates per 5 °C or per interquartile range, these were rescaled to 1 °C.

Studies that reported only ambient air temperature could not be placed on the WBGT scale without information on humidity, radiant heat and wind that was not available in the papers. They were therefore synthesised in a separate stratum, with the effect measure expressed as the RR per 1 °C increase in daily maximum temperature above the study-specific threshold. We did not attempt to convert air temperature to WBGT, because the error introduced by such a conversion would vary with climate in ways that could not be quantified from the published data {{budd2008}}.

For kidney-related outcomes, the effect measure was the odds ratio (OR) comparing heat-exposed with unexposed workers. Most kidney studies defined exposure by occupational group, task or season within the same population, combined with a temperature or WBGT measure that characterised the exposed condition; few reported continuous exposure–response estimates. Risk ratios from cohort studies of an outcome with low cumulative incidence were treated as approximating the OR. Where a study reported both acute kidney injury and reduced eGFR, the more clinically specific outcome (acute kidney injury) was used in the primary analysis.

### 3.2.7 Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using an adaptation of ROBINS-I {{sterne2016}}. ROBINS-I was designed to assess non-randomised studies of interventions by comparison with a hypothetical target randomised trial. Heat exposure is not an intervention that an investigator can assign, but the same logic can be applied by imagining an idealised study in which comparable workers are allocated to different thermal conditions and followed for the outcome of interest. We made four adaptations, agreed in the protocol and piloted on five studies:

1. The domain "bias in classification of interventions" was reframed as **bias in exposure classification**. It addressed the source and spatial resolution of the exposure measure (onsite instrument, nearby station or regional average), its timing relative to the work shift, and whether misclassification was likely to differ by outcome status.
2. The domain "bias due to deviations from intended interventions" was reframed as **bias due to exposure modification at the worksite**. It addressed whether protective measures, such as work–rest schedules or shift changes on hot days, could have altered the true exposure in a way that was unmeasured and related to the measured exposure.
3. For the confounding domain, we specified in advance a minimum set of confounders for each design using causal diagrams {{greenland1999}}. For studies comparing days or periods within the same population (time-series and case-crossover), the minimum set was long-term trend, seasonality, day of the week and public holidays; air pollution was regarded as a potential confounder where it was plausibly a common cause of injury or kidney stress and of temperature variation. For studies comparing individuals, the minimum set was age, sex and workload, and for kidney outcomes also diabetes, hypertension and the use of nephrotoxic medicines. Humidity was not treated as a confounder in WBGT studies, because it is a component of the exposure.
4. Signalling questions about co-interventions were omitted, as they had no meaningful analogue.

Each of the seven domains (confounding, selection of participants, exposure classification, exposure modification at the worksite, missing data, outcome measurement, selection of the reported result) was rated as low, moderate, serious or critical risk of bias, and an overall judgement was assigned according to the worst rating in any domain, as recommended for ROBINS-I {{sterne2016}}. Two reviewers (NSR and CB) assessed each study independently and reached consensus by discussion. Because ROBINS-I calibrates its ratings against a hypothetical randomised trial, an overall rating of "low" is rarely attainable for an environmental exposure study, and a rating of "moderate" indicates a study that is sound for an observational design.

### 3.2.8 Synthesis methods

Effect estimates were pooled on the log scale using random-effects meta-analysis with the DerSimonian–Laird estimator of between-study variance {{dersimonian1986}}. A random-effects model was chosen because the included studies differed in population, sector, climate, exposure assessment and outcome definition, so that a single common effect was implausible. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, the I² statistic {{higgins2002}} and the between-study variance τ², and 95% prediction intervals were calculated to indicate the range of effects that might be expected in a new comparable setting. Following the usual interpretation, I² values around 25%, 50% and 75% were taken to indicate low, moderate and high heterogeneity {{higgins2002}}, while recognising that I² describes the proportion of variability due to heterogeneity rather than its absolute magnitude.

Studies were pooled only if they reported, or allowed derivation of, an estimate on the relevant scale. Studies that could not be pooled were synthesised narratively, with attention to the direction and approximate size of their reported associations.

### 3.2.9 Subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses and small-study effects

Pre-specified subgroup analyses examined sector (agriculture, construction, mixed outdoor), method of exposure assessment (onsite measurement versus station-based or modelled data) and, for kidney outcomes, outcome definition (acute kidney injury versus reduced eGFR). Differences between subgroups were tested with a Q-test for subgroup differences. Because the number of studies in each subgroup was small, these analyses were regarded as exploratory.

Sensitivity analyses repeated the primary analyses after excluding studies at serious or critical overall risk of bias, after removing each study in turn (leave-one-out), and, for kidney outcomes, after restricting to studies with at least 1,000 participants. Small-study effects were assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots and by Egger's regression test {{egger1997}}, with p < .10 taken as suggestive of asymmetry. We note that Egger's test has limited power when fewer than ten studies are pooled, which applied to the injury analysis.

### 3.2.10 Certainty of evidence and deviations from protocol

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach {{guyatt2008}}. Evidence from observational studies started at low certainty and could be downgraded for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias, or upgraded for a large effect, a dose–response gradient, or residual confounding that would be expected to reduce the observed effect. Ratings were made by NSR and checked by EM.

Two deviations from the protocol occurred. First, the protocol anticipated pooling all injury studies on a single scale; the separate stratum for ambient-temperature studies was introduced once it became clear that conversion to WBGT could not be justified from the published information. Second, the size-restricted sensitivity analysis for kidney outcomes was added after Egger's test suggested small-study effects; it is reported as a post hoc analysis.

All analyses were conducted in R using standard meta-analysis packages. Analysis code and the extraction dataset were deposited with the published paper.

## 3.3 Results

### 3.3.1 Study selection

The database searches identified 3,812 records: 1,104 from MEDLINE, 1,486 from Embase, 812 from Web of Science and 410 from CINAHL. After removal of 906 duplicates, 2,906 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 2,788 were excluded. The full texts of the remaining 118 reports were assessed, and 91 were excluded: 24 because the population was not occupational or not outdoor, 21 because heat exposure was not quantified, 18 because neither outcome of interest was reported, 15 because no comparator or effect estimate was available, 9 because they were conference abstracts without retrievable full data, and 4 because they reported on a cohort already included. Twenty-seven studies were included. Citation searching identified 14 further records, none of which met the eligibility criteria. The flow of records is shown in Figure 3.1.

Fourteen studies reported injury outcomes only, 11 reported kidney-related outcomes only, and 2 reported both, so that 16 studies contributed to the injury domain and 13 to the kidney domain. Exposure assessment is the feature of the evidence base most relevant to the rest of this thesis. Sixteen studies used WBGT and 11 used ambient air temperature. Only 5 studies (S07, S16, S19, S20 and S27) measured exposure with instruments at or near the worksite, and all five were small, with between 310 and 1,240 participants. The remaining 22 studies (81%) assigned exposure from fixed meteorological stations, either directly or through WBGT modelled from station variables, and in 2 of these the exposure was a regional average across several stations. None of the station-based studies reported the distance between the station and the worksites, and none applied a correction for differences in surface type or urban form between station and worksite. Most studies reported exposure on the day of the outcome only; among the 16 injury studies, 3 (S06, S12 and S14) examined lagged effects over several days, 2 of them using distributed lag non-linear models {{gasparrini2010}}.

Information on the modifiers that might matter most for prevention was sparse. Four of the 16 injury studies reported estimates stratified by age, and all four found stronger associations in younger workers, though with overlapping confidence intervals. Only one study (S26) mentioned the payment system of the workforce, noting in its description of the setting that most agricultural workers were paid by the piece; it did not report estimates stratified by payment type. Sex-stratified estimates were reported in 5 studies and showed no consistent pattern.

### 3.3.3 Risk of bias

No study was judged to be at low overall risk of bias. Nine studies were at moderate risk, 15 at serious risk and 3 at critical risk (Table 3.3). The domains that most often drove serious ratings were exposure classification, reflecting reliance on distant or regional station data, and confounding, reflecting incomplete adjustment for workload in between-person comparisons or for seasonality in some time-series analyses. All three studies rated at critical risk (S09, S15 and S21) were cross-sectional studies that compared groups defined by job title or self-reported conditions, with crude or minimally adjusted analyses. Exposure modification at the worksite was rated at moderate risk for most studies, because few reported whether protective practices changed on hot days; where such practices existed, they could have reduced the true exposure of workers on the hottest days and so attenuated the observed associations. Outcome measurement was generally at lower risk in registry-based injury studies than in studies using self-reported injury, and lower in studies using laboratory-confirmed acute kidney injury than in those relying on a single eGFR measurement.

### 3.3.4 Heat exposure and occupational injury

Of the 16 studies in the injury domain, 9 reported or allowed derivation of an estimate on the WBGT scale and were included in the primary meta-analysis (S03, S06, S07, S08, S11, S12, S14, S26 and S27). Five reported estimates for ambient air temperature only and were pooled in the separate stratum (S01, S02, S05, S10 and S13). Two cross-sectional studies (S04 and S09) reported only categorical comparisons of self-reported injury and were synthesised narratively.

In the primary analysis, the risk of occupational injury increased by 5% for each 1 °C of WBGT above 28 °C (pooled RR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.08). Heterogeneity was moderate to high (Q = 21.1, df = 8, p = .007; I² = 62%; τ² = 0.0005), and the 95% prediction interval (0.99–1.12) included the possibility of no association in a new setting while extending to a 12% increase per degree. Every individual study estimate lay above 1, and eight of the nine had confidence intervals that excluded 1 (Table 3.4; Figure 3.2). The two studies with onsite exposure measurement (S07 and S27) reported the largest per-degree estimates, although they were also the least precise.

In the ambient-temperature stratum, the risk of injury increased by 2% per 1 °C of daily maximum air temperature above the study-specific threshold (pooled RR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03; 5 studies; I² = 48%). The thresholds used in these studies ranged from 25 °C to 32 °C, so this estimate is not directly comparable with the WBGT estimate. The two cross-sectional studies synthesised narratively both reported a higher prevalence of self-reported injury among workers in the hottest exposure category: S04 reported an adjusted prevalence ratio of 1.38 comparing the highest with the lowest tertile of seasonal mean WBGT, and S09 an unadjusted odds ratio of 1.6 for workers reporting frequent work in very hot conditions. Both were at serious or critical risk of bias and add little beyond confirming the direction of association.

Among the three studies that modelled lagged effects (S06, S12 and S14), the excess risk was concentrated on the day of exposure and the following day, with estimates for lags of two days or more close to the null. One of these (S14) reported a non-linear exposure–response relationship with risk increasing from around 27 °C WBGT; the others assumed linearity above a threshold. With only three studies, it was not possible to synthesise lag structures quantitatively.

### 3.3.5 Heat exposure and kidney-related outcomes

Of the 13 studies in the kidney domain, 11 reported a comparison between heat-exposed and unexposed workers and were pooled (S15, S16, S17, S18, S19, S20, S21, S23, S24, S26 and S27). The other two (S22 and S25) were registry-based cohort studies reporting the association between daily temperature or WBGT and the rate of insurance-recorded acute kidney injury. Both found higher rates on hotter days (S22: RR 1.03 per 1 °C Tmax above 30 °C; S25: RR 1.06 per 1 °C WBGT above 28 °C) and were synthesised narratively.

Heat-exposed workers had 47% higher odds of a kidney-related outcome than unexposed workers (pooled OR 1.47, 95% CI 1.21–1.79). Heterogeneity was high (Q = 34.5, df = 10, p < .001; I² = 71%; τ² = 0.04), and the 95% prediction interval was wide (0.88–2.46). Study estimates ranged from 1.12 to 2.40 (Table 3.5; Figure 3.3). The four largest studies (S17, S18, S26 and S27, each with more than 1,000 participants) reported ORs between 1.19 and 1.31, while several of the smallest studies reported ORs close to or above 2.

### 3.3.7 Small-study effects

The funnel plot for injury showed mild asymmetry, with the two small onsite studies lying to the right of the pooled estimate (Figure 3.4). Egger's test gave an intercept of 1.21 (p = .09), which met our pre-specified threshold for suggestive asymmetry but should be interpreted cautiously, since the test has little power with nine studies and since the small studies were also those with the most accurate exposure measurement. Asymmetry produced by better exposure assessment in small studies is not the same as asymmetry produced by selective publication, and the two cannot be distinguished with these data.

For kidney outcomes, the funnel plot was clearly asymmetric, with small studies reporting larger ORs and no small studies reporting estimates well below the pooled value. Egger's test gave an intercept of 1.87 (p = .04). Together with the attenuated estimate in the size-restricted analysis, this suggests that the pooled kidney estimate may be inflated by small-study effects, which could reflect publication bias, more intensive exposure in the populations chosen for small field studies, or both.

### 3.3.8 Certainty of evidence

The GRADE assessment is summarised in Table 3.7. For injury, the evidence started at low certainty as observational evidence. It was not downgraded for risk of bias, because the studies contributing most weight were at moderate risk and the sensitivity analysis excluding studies at serious risk gave a similar estimate. It was downgraded one level for inconsistency, because of moderate to high heterogeneity and a prediction interval that included the null. It was upgraded one level for a dose–response gradient, since the primary estimate is itself an exposure–response slope and the three studies that modelled the shape of the relationship found risk increasing progressively with WBGT. The overall certainty was therefore low.

For kidney outcomes, the evidence started at low certainty and was downgraded for risk of bias (more than half the weight came from studies at serious or critical risk), for inconsistency (I² = 71% and a wide prediction interval) and for suspected publication bias. No reason for upgrading was identified, since few studies reported an exposure–response gradient. The overall certainty was very low.

### 3.3.9 Evidence on protective interventions

No included study evaluated a protective intervention. Three studies (S07, S16 and S24) described the protective measures in place at the study sites, such as scheduled rest breaks or the provision of drinking water, but none measured adherence or analysed whether the association between heat and the outcome differed according to these measures. One study (S24) reported, as a secondary descriptive finding, that the proportion of workers with a cross-season creatinine rise was lower in the season after the employer introduced shaded rest areas and water stations than in the season before; there was no comparison group, the seasons differed in temperature, and the analysis was not suitable for pooling. The searches also identified 6 intervention studies that were excluded at the full-text stage because they reported only physiological strain, heat-illness symptoms or productivity outcomes; none reported injury or kidney outcomes.

## 3.4 Discussion

### 3.4.1 Principal findings

This review brought together 27 studies of approximately 84,000 outdoor workers in 9 countries and found that occupational heat exposure was associated with an increased risk of injury and of kidney-related outcomes. For injury, each 1 °C of WBGT above 28 °C was associated with a 5% increase in risk, an estimate that was consistent in direction across all nine pooled studies but heterogeneous in size. For kidney outcomes, heat-exposed workers had 47% higher odds of acute kidney injury or reduced eGFR than unexposed workers, but this estimate was more heterogeneous, showed evidence of small-study effects, and fell to 29% higher odds when the analysis was restricted to the largest studies. Certainty of evidence was low for injury and very low for kidney outcomes. Beyond these pooled estimates, the review identified two structural features of the evidence base: exposure was almost always assigned from weather stations rather than measured where people worked, and no study evaluated an intervention intended to protect workers from these outcomes.

### 3.4.2 Comparison with previous reviews

The direction of both associations agrees with earlier syntheses. A broad meta-analysis of occupational heat strain reported that work in heat-stress conditions was associated with adverse health outcomes, including kidney-related outcomes and injury, as well as with productivity loss {{flouris2018}}, and a review focused on injury concluded that heat was associated with higher injury risk across a range of settings {{spector2019}}. The present review differs from these in three respects. First, it expressed the injury association on a single WBGT scale, as an exposure–response slope above a stated reference, which gives a quantity that can be compared directly with the thresholds used in occupational guidance. Second, it kept ambient-temperature studies separate rather than pooling them with WBGT studies, on the grounds that the two metrics describe different aspects of the thermal environment and cannot be converted reliably from published data {{budd2008;parsons2014}}. Third, it appraised risk of bias with a tool adapted explicitly for exposure studies and graded certainty with GRADE {{guyatt2008}}, which allowed the low certainty of the kidney evidence to be made explicit rather than left implicit in a pooled estimate that, on its own, appears substantial.

The size of the injury association deserves comment. A 5% increase in risk per degree is modest for an individual worker on a single day. Outdoor workers in hot climates, however, may spend many working days several degrees above 28 °C, and the risk accumulates across a large workforce and a long season. The practical importance of the estimate therefore depends on the distribution of exposure in a given population, which the published studies rarely reported in enough detail to calculate attributable burden. This is a gap that a worksite-level study with a full exposure distribution can fill, using methods for estimating the fraction of outcomes attributable to temperature that have been developed for population mortality {{gasparrini2015}}.

### 3.4.3 Interpretation of heterogeneity

The heterogeneity observed in both analyses is unlikely to have a single source. For injury, the prediction interval included the null, meaning that in some settings the association may be negligible. Plausible contributors include differences in climate and acclimatisation, in the type and intensity of work, in the injury definitions used by different compensation systems, and in the protective practices in place. The subgroup analyses point towards one further contributor that is particularly relevant to this thesis: the method of exposure assessment. The two injury studies that measured WBGT at the worksite reported per-degree estimates roughly twice as large as the pooled estimate from station-based studies, and the four kidney studies with onsite measurement reported a larger pooled OR than the others. Neither difference was statistically significant, and the onsite studies were few and small. The pattern is, however, what would be expected if station-based exposure introduced measurement error that is largely non-differential with respect to the outcome and so attenuates associations towards the null. A station sited in an open, grassed enclosure may record conditions that differ systematically from those on a concrete construction site, under a roof deck or in a crop field without shade, and this difference is likely to vary from one study region to another. Exposure measurement error of this kind could therefore both attenuate the pooled estimate and contribute to heterogeneity between studies.

For kidney outcomes, the heterogeneity is more likely to reflect differences in outcome definition and population. The included studies used cross-shift creatinine changes, cross-season changes, single eGFR measurements and clinically recorded acute kidney injury, and these capture different points on a spectrum from transient physiological change to clinical disease. Several of the smaller studies were conducted in populations selected because they were thought to be at high risk, which may explain both their larger estimates and the funnel-plot asymmetry. The suggestion that recurrent heat stress might contribute to longer-term loss of kidney function {{flouris2018;ebi2021}} cannot be tested with cross-sectional data of this kind, and the review provides no evidence on chronic outcomes.

### 3.4.4 Exposure timing and effect modification

Only three injury studies examined effects beyond the day of exposure, and they suggested that most of the excess risk occurred on the same day or the next. This is consistent with the mechanisms through which heat is thought to cause injury, which act through fatigue, impaired attention and physical discomfort within the working day {{parsons2014;lucas2014}}, rather than through cumulative damage. A distributed lag framework allows these short-term patterns to be estimated jointly with a non-linear exposure–response relationship {{gasparrini2010}}, but most included studies assumed a single lag and a linear or threshold-linear relationship, so the shape and timing of injury risk at the worksite remain poorly described.

Information on who is most at risk was similarly thin. The four studies that stratified by age all found stronger associations in younger workers, a pattern that may reflect inexperience, a greater tendency to keep up the pace of work, or the allocation of younger workers to more exposed tasks. Only one study mentioned how workers were paid, and none examined whether payment by the piece modified the association. This is a notable omission. Where earnings depend on output, workers have a direct financial reason not to slow down or rest in the heat, and the ergonomic advice to adjust work pace to thermal conditions {{lucas2014}} may be difficult to follow. Whether the injury risk associated with heat is concentrated among piece-rate workers is a question the existing evidence cannot answer.

### 3.4.5 The absence of intervention evidence

Perhaps the most important finding for practice is negative. Despite extensive guidance on protective measures such as work–rest schedules, shade and hydration {{parsons2014;lucas2014}}, no study in this review evaluated whether such measures reduce injury or kidney outcomes in outdoor workers. The six intervention studies identified by the searches reported only physiological strain, symptoms or productivity, and were therefore excluded. It may be that protective measures are effective and their effectiveness has simply not been studied against these outcomes; it may also be that they are recommended more often than they are implemented. Either way, the gap means that occupational heat policy currently rests on physiological reasoning and on associations between heat and harm, rather than on evidence that the protections recommended actually reduce that harm. Interventions of this kind are complex, depending on supervisors, crews, schedules and pay as much as on equipment, and their development and evaluation would benefit from the established frameworks for complex interventions {{craig2008;skivington2021}}.

### 3.4.6 Strengths and limitations

The review has several strengths. It followed a registered protocol and was reported according to PRISMA 2020 {{page2021}}. It searched four major databases without language restriction and used independent duplicate screening and checking of extraction. It made explicit the assumptions required to harmonise exposure, and kept incompatible metrics apart rather than forcing them onto a common scale. It adapted ROBINS-I for exposure studies in a way that is documented and could be reused, and it used prediction intervals as well as I² to describe heterogeneity, which conveys more clearly what the pooled estimates mean for a new setting.

The limitations are, in part, limitations of the evidence base, and in part of the review itself. First, the harmonisation of injury estimates to a per-degree slope above 28 °C assumed approximate log-linearity above the reference. Where studies reported categories, the slope was derived from category midpoints, which introduces error that was not propagated into the pooled confidence intervals. Second, the number of studies in each analysis was small, so subgroup comparisons had little power and Egger's test {{egger1997}} was underpowered for injury. Third, effect measures were treated as interchangeable approximations of the relative risk (rate ratios, hazard ratios and, for kidney outcomes, odds ratios and risk ratios), which is reasonable for uncommon outcomes but less so for reduced eGFR in heavily exposed populations, where prevalence may be high enough for the OR to overstate the risk ratio. Fourth, the adaptation of ROBINS-I {{sterne2016}} for exposure studies, while explicit, has not been validated, and other reviewers might reasonably make different judgements, particularly on the exposure-modification domain. Fifth, the classification of time-series and case-crossover analyses as cohort designs was a pragmatic decision that affects how the evidence base is described but not the pooled estimates. Sixth, the kidney analysis combined outcomes of differing clinical significance; the subgroup analysis by outcome definition did not suggest a difference, but it was underpowered to detect one. Finally, publication bias cannot be excluded, especially for kidney outcomes, and the review did not search grey literature from employers, insurers or compensation boards, where relevant analyses may exist without being published.

### 3.4.7 Implications for research

Three priorities for research follow from these findings. The first is better exposure assessment. Studies that estimate WBGT at the worksite, or that correct station-based estimates for the characteristics of the worksite, would reduce the measurement error that probably attenuates current estimates and contributes to heterogeneity. Large registry-based studies are well placed to do this if worksite locations can be linked to exposure surfaces at a finer spatial scale than a single station. The second is better characterisation of timing and effect modification. Designs that compare the same workers across days, such as the case-crossover design, control for stable individual characteristics by design, and when combined with distributed lag models {{gasparrini2010}} and with confounder selection informed by causal diagrams {{greenland1999}} they allow the shape and timing of risk to be estimated with fewer assumptions. Analyses stratified by age and by payment system would show whether risk is concentrated in identifiable groups. The third is intervention research. Evaluations of protective programmes that measure injury or kidney outcomes, or validated intermediate outcomes such as heat-symptom reports and hydration markers, are needed, together with process evaluations that can explain why programmes are or are not implemented. Where randomised trials are not feasible, observational analyses designed to emulate a target trial {{hernan2016}} offer a disciplined way of using routine data to estimate the effects of protective policies.

## 3.5 Conclusions

Occupational heat exposure is associated with an increased risk of injury and of kidney-related outcomes in outdoor workers. For injury, the risk increases by about 5% per 1 °C of WBGT above 28 °C; for kidney outcomes, heat-exposed workers have higher odds of acute kidney injury or reduced eGFR, though the size of this association is uncertain and may be inflated by small-study effects. The certainty of the evidence is low for injury and very low for kidney outcomes. The evidence base relies heavily on weather-station exposure data, rarely examines the timing of effects or the characteristics of the most affected workers, and contains no evaluations of protective interventions against these outcomes. Studies that estimate exposure at the worksite, model lagged and non-linear effects, examine effect modification by age and payment system, and evaluate protective programmes are needed.

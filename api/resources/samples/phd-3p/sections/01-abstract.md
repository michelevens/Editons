# Abstract

Outdoor workers in agriculture and construction carry much of the health burden of a warming climate, yet the evidence on which workplace heat protection rests is thin, its exposure measurement is coarse, and protective measures are rarely evaluated in the conditions under which they must operate. This thesis by publication asks how occupational heat exposure affects the health of outdoor workers and what it would take to protect them. It does so through three linked studies.

Paper 1 is a systematic review and meta-analysis of occupational heat exposure and injury and kidney-related outcomes. Twenty-seven studies (19 cohort, 8 cross-sectional) from 9 countries, covering approximately 84,000 workers, were included. Injury risk rose by 5% for each 1 °C of wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) above 28 °C (pooled relative risk 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.08; I² = 62%), and heat-exposed workers had higher odds of kidney-related outcomes (pooled odds ratio 1.47, 95% CI 1.21–1.79; I² = 71%). Certainty was low for injury and very low for kidney outcomes. Exposure was almost always taken from weather stations rather than worksites, and protective interventions were scarcely studied.

Paper 2 addressed the exposure gap with a time-stratified case-crossover analysis of 18,406 workers' compensation injury claims in agriculture and construction from 2015 to 2023, linked to worksite WBGT estimated from 46 weather stations with land-surface correction. Injury risk increased above a WBGT of 26 °C; the relative risk at 31 °C compared with 24 °C was 1.21 (95% CI 1.12–1.31), concentrated on the day of exposure. Effects were stronger in workers younger than 25 years (RR 1.34) and in piece-rate agricultural work (RR 1.38). Approximately 4.1% of injuries were attributable to heat.

Paper 3 was a mixed-methods feasibility study of a heat-stress management programme for construction crews, combining WBGT-triggered work–rest schedules, shaded rest, hydration stations, buddy checks and supervisor training. Six crews (n = 74) took part over eight weeks. Consent was high (89%), but fidelity to scheduled breaks was 71% overall and 52% on piece-rate days. Exploratory outcomes moved in the expected direction. Interviews and focus groups showed that water was easy to provide and rest was hard to take, because breaks cost money and supervisors set the norms.

Read together, the studies suggest that heat protection for outdoor workers is a problem of labour organisation as much as of physiology. The thesis contributes worksite-level exposure methods with negative-control checks, evidence that payment systems modify heat risk, and the practical case for pay protection within workplace heat plans, to be tested in a cluster randomised trial.

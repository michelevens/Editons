# Chapter 2: Background and Methods Overview

## 2.1 Purpose and structure of this chapter

Chapter 1 set out the problem this thesis addresses, its aim and its three research questions. This chapter does two jobs that the papers themselves cannot do well within the word limits of a journal manuscript. First, it provides the substantive background that all three papers share: what heat does to a working body, how heat exposure is measured and why the choice of metric matters, what is already known about the health and safety consequences of working in the heat, and how protection is currently conceived. Second, it gives an overview of the methods used across the programme of research, explaining why a systematic review, a case-crossover study and a mixed-methods feasibility study were chosen, how they fit together, and what assumptions each makes. Detailed methods for each study are reported in the papers (Chapters 3, 4 and 5); the aim here is to justify the sequence rather than to repeat it.

The chapter is organised in two parts. Sections 2.2 to 2.6 cover the background: thermal physiology and heat strain (Section 2.2), the measurement of occupational heat exposure (Section 2.3), the evidence on health, injury and productivity outcomes (Section 2.4), approaches to protection and the place of work organisation within them (Section 2.5), and the conceptual framework that links these strands and organises the thesis (Section 2.6). Sections 2.7 to 2.11 cover the methods: the overall research design and its philosophical stance (Section 2.7), the three study designs in turn (Sections 2.8 to 2.10), and the cross-cutting matters of setting, ethics, data management and reflexivity (Section 2.11). Section 2.12 summarises and hands over to Paper 1.

## 2.2 Heat, the working body and heat strain

### 2.2.1 Heat balance

The starting point for any account of occupational heat is the heat balance of the human body. Core body temperature is held within a narrow range by balancing heat gained from metabolism and from the environment against heat lost to the environment {{parsons2014}}. Metabolic heat production rises steeply with physical work: a labourer carrying blocks or cutting cane generates several times the heat of a person at rest, and almost all of that energy appears as heat rather than mechanical work. The environment can add to the load through radiation from the sun and from hot surfaces, and through convection when air temperature exceeds skin temperature. The body loses heat by convection and radiation when the surroundings are cooler than the skin, and, crucially in hot conditions, by the evaporation of sweat.

{{parsons2014|n}} describes how these exchanges depend on four environmental variables (air temperature, humidity, air movement and radiant temperature) and two personal ones (metabolic rate and clothing). The distinction matters for this thesis because the outdoor workers studied here are exposed to all four environmental variables at once and have limited control over the personal ones. Their metabolic rate is set largely by the pace of work, which is set in turn by how they are paid and supervised; their clothing is often set by safety requirements that add insulation and impede evaporation. In other words, two of the six terms in the heat balance are partly determined by work organisation rather than by physiology or weather. This observation recurs throughout the thesis and is developed in Section 2.6.

### 2.2.2 From heat stress to heat strain

The occupational literature distinguishes heat stress, the external thermal load imposed by environment, work and clothing, from heat strain, the physiological response to that load {{parsons2014;lucas2014}}. Heat strain shows itself as rising core and skin temperature, rising heart rate, sweat loss and, if fluid is not replaced, progressive dehydration. The distinction is not merely terminological. Two workers exposed to the same heat stress can experience very different strain depending on acclimatisation, fitness, hydration, age, medication and the pace at which they work. Exposure metrics, which estimate stress, are therefore imperfect proxies for the biological dose that matters for health.

{{lucas2014|n}} frame excessive occupational heat exposure as an ergonomic challenge as much as a medical one: the mismatch between the demands of a job and the capacity of the body to shed heat. Their framing is useful here because it places the solution partly in the design of work. When the environment does not allow sufficient heat loss, the only physiological way to stay in balance is to reduce metabolic heat production, which in practice means working more slowly or stopping. Workers do this spontaneously when they can, a behaviour sometimes described as self-pacing. The capacity to self-pace is not evenly distributed: it depends on whether the worker controls the pace of work, whether rest is paid, and whether stopping is socially acceptable on the crew. Paper 3 (Chapter 5) examines this directly.

### 2.2.3 Acute and cumulative effects

The acute clinical consequences of heat strain run along a spectrum from heat rash, cramps and syncope to heat exhaustion and, at the severe end, heat stroke, a medical emergency in which thermoregulation fails {{ebi2021}}. Short of these clinical endpoints, heat strain impairs cognitive and psychomotor performance: attention, reaction time and judgement deteriorate, and fatigue accumulates faster {{parsons2014}}. These effects provide the main hypothesised pathway between heat and occupational injury, discussed in Section 2.4.2.

Heat also has effects that accumulate over days and seasons. Repeated episodes of dehydration and heat strain place load on the kidney, and a growing literature has examined whether recurrent occupational heat exposure contributes to acute kidney injury and to longer-term decline in kidney function {{flouris2018;ebi2021}}. The plausibility of this pathway rests on the physiology of volume depletion, reduced renal blood flow and, in severe cases, muscle breakdown, but the epidemiological evidence linking these mechanisms to measured kidney outcomes in workers is, as Chapter 3 shows, of low certainty. Paper 1 therefore examines injury and kidney outcomes side by side, because they represent the two most-studied acute and subacute consequences of occupational heat and because they test different parts of the causal chain: injury the cognitive and behavioural pathway, kidney outcomes the physiological one.

### 2.2.4 Individual susceptibility

Several individual characteristics modify the relationship between heat stress and strain. Acclimatisation over the first one to two weeks of heat exposure improves sweating efficiency and cardiovascular stability, which is why new and returning workers are often described as being at particular risk {{lucas2014;parsons2014}}. Older age, some chronic conditions and some medications reduce thermoregulatory capacity {{ebi2021}}. Yet in occupational settings the expected age gradient may be reversed: younger workers are more often new to the job, less able to refuse work, more likely to be paid by output and more likely to take risks. Paper 2 (Chapter 4) found effect modification by age in exactly this direction, with stronger associations in workers under 25 years. The point for this chapter is that susceptibility in working populations is shaped by social position as well as by biology, and an adequate framework must hold both.

## 2.3 Measuring occupational heat exposure

### 2.3.1 Why measurement matters

Every quantitative estimate in this thesis depends on how heat exposure was measured. A study that uses daily maximum air temperature from an airport weather station is measuring something different from a study that uses a wet-bulb globe temperature estimated at the worksite, and the two cannot be pooled without care. Exposure measurement error in environmental epidemiology tends, under common conditions, to bias associations towards the null, but it can also distort the shape of an exposure–response relationship and the location of apparent thresholds [source needed]. Because threshold location has direct practical consequences for when protective measures are triggered, measurement is not a technical detail here but part of the substance of the research.

### 2.3.2 Simple meteorological measures

The simplest exposure measures are those reported routinely by weather services: air temperature (daily mean, maximum or minimum), relative humidity, and composite indices that combine temperature and humidity into a single "apparent" or "feels-like" temperature. Their advantages are availability, long time series and wide spatial coverage, which is why most of the epidemiological literature on heat and health uses them {{ebi2021;gasparrini2015}}. Their disadvantage for occupational research is that they omit two of the environmental variables that matter most for outdoor workers, namely solar radiation and air movement, and they say nothing about metabolic rate or clothing. A construction worker on an exposed roof at midday and a clerk in an air-conditioned office experience the same airport temperature.

### 2.3.3 The wet-bulb globe temperature

The wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) was developed to address exactly this gap. {{budd2008|n}} traces its origin to military training, where it was introduced as a practical index for deciding when strenuous outdoor activity should be modified or stopped. For outdoor conditions with solar load, WBGT is computed as a weighted sum of three measurements:

WBGT = 0.7 × T_nw + 0.2 × T_g + 0.1 × T_a

where T_nw is the natural wet-bulb temperature (reflecting humidity and air movement), T_g is the black globe temperature (reflecting radiant heat), and T_a is the dry-bulb air temperature {{budd2008;parsons2014}}. The heavy weighting on the natural wet-bulb temperature reflects the dominance of evaporative cooling in hot conditions: when humid air limits evaporation, the body's main route of heat loss is closed.

WBGT has become the most widely used occupational heat index and is embedded in many occupational guidance frameworks, which relate permissible work–rest regimes to WBGT and metabolic rate {{parsons2014}}. Its advantages are that it integrates the four environmental variables, that it has decades of field use behind it, and that its values can be linked directly to work–rest recommendations. Paper 3 exploits this last property: the work–rest schedules in the intervention were triggered by on-site WBGT readings.

### 2.3.4 Limitations of WBGT

WBGT is not a perfect index, and {{budd2008|n}} sets out its limitations clearly. It was derived empirically rather than from a full heat-balance model; it underweights the effect of air movement in some conditions; it can underestimate stress when evaporation is restricted by clothing or very high humidity; and it does not, by itself, account for metabolic rate or clothing, which must be handled separately through adjustments or the accompanying guidance. Rational indices built on full heat-balance models address some of these problems but require inputs, such as individual metabolic rate and clothing properties, that are rarely available in large epidemiological datasets {{parsons2014}}.

For this thesis, the practical consequence is a trade-off. WBGT is better than air temperature as a measure of the environmental component of occupational heat stress, and it connects to the protective guidance that workplaces actually use. But it remains an environmental measure, and the gap between WBGT and the strain experienced by a particular worker on a particular day is filled by work organisation, clothing and individual factors. The thesis treats WBGT as the best available common currency rather than as a measure of dose.

### 2.3.5 Measured and modelled WBGT

WBGT can be measured directly with instruments that house a natural wet-bulb sensor, a black globe and a shielded air thermometer. Direct measurement is the reference standard for workplace assessment but is rarely available at the scale needed for population studies. The alternative is to model WBGT from routinely measured meteorological variables, using a physical model to estimate natural wet-bulb and globe temperatures from air temperature, humidity, wind speed and solar radiation [source needed]. Modelled WBGT has the advantage of coverage but inherits the spatial limitations of the weather stations whose data it uses: a station sited in an open grassy field may represent conditions poorly at a construction site surrounded by concrete or in a sheltered field of crops.

Paper 1 found that almost all included studies assigned exposure from weather-station data, usually from a single station or a regional average, rather than at the worksite. This was one of the two key gaps that motivated Paper 2, which estimated worksite WBGT from 46 weather stations in the Coastal Region with a land-surface correction intended to account for differences in surface type and urban form between station and worksite. The approach is summarised in Section 2.9.3 and reported in full in Chapter 4.

## 2.4 Health, safety and productivity consequences of occupational heat

### 2.4.1 The broader evidence base

The literature on heat and health is dominated by population studies of mortality and hospital admissions in the general population, which have established robust associations between high ambient temperature and adverse outcomes {{ebi2021;gasparrini2015}}. These studies developed many of the analytical tools used in this thesis, including time-series and case-crossover designs, distributed lag models and attributable-fraction methods {{gasparrini2010;gasparrini2015}}. But general-population studies say little about workers specifically. Working-age adults are under-represented among heat deaths in most settings, and the main health burden of occupational heat may lie less in mortality than in injury, illness, kidney effects and lost capacity to work {{kjellstrom2016;flouris2018}}.

{{kjellstrom2016|n}} argued that occupational heat exposure is a central, and often overlooked, channel through which climate change affects human health and economic activity. Their review emphasised that the populations most exposed are those doing heavy physical work outdoors or in hot indoor environments without cooling, that these workers are concentrated in agriculture, construction and other manual sectors, and that rising temperatures are likely to increase both health effects and productivity losses. This framing positions occupational heat as a question of both public health and labour economics, a combination that this thesis takes seriously.

### 2.4.2 Heat and occupational injury

The hypothesised pathways from heat to injury are several. Heat strain impairs attention, reaction time and coordination; sweaty palms reduce grip on tools; fogged safety glasses reduce vision; fatigue accumulates; and workers may remove protective equipment because it is uncomfortable in the heat {{spector2019;parsons2014}}. Dehydration and heat exhaustion can cause dizziness and falls. Each of these is plausible, and together they predict that the risk of injury should rise on hotter days, most strongly on the same day, and more steeply among workers doing heavier work or with less control over its pace.

{{spector2019|n}} reviewed the literature on heat exposure and occupational injury and concluded that the evidence consistently pointed to increased injury risk with heat, while noting substantial heterogeneity in exposure metrics, outcome definitions and study designs. Their review also highlighted the scarcity of studies that measured exposure at the worksite, examined effect modification by work characteristics, or evaluated interventions. These are the gaps that Papers 1, 2 and 3 respectively take up.

### 2.4.3 Heat and kidney outcomes

The relationship between occupational heat and kidney health has attracted growing attention, partly because of reports of chronic kidney disease of uncertain cause in agricultural communities in hot regions. The evidence connecting heat exposure to acute kidney injury, markers of reduced kidney function and longer-term decline is less developed than the injury evidence {{flouris2018;ebi2021}}. Studies vary widely in how they define kidney outcomes, using clinical diagnosis of acute kidney injury in some, cross-shift changes in serum creatinine or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in others, and urinary markers in still others. Exposure is often defined by occupation or season rather than measured. These features make kidney outcomes harder to synthesise, and they help explain why Paper 1 graded the certainty of evidence for kidney outcomes as very low despite a pooled association that pointed clearly in one direction (Chapter 3).

### 2.4.4 Productivity and the economic framing

{{flouris2018|n}} synthesised evidence on occupational heat strain and found that working in heat-stress conditions was associated both with adverse health outcomes and with reduced productivity. The productivity finding is relevant here in two ways. First, it supports the physiological account of self-pacing: output falls because workers, consciously or not, reduce metabolic heat production. Second, it explains why heat protection collides with economic incentives. If rest reduces output, and output is what workers or employers are paid for, then protection carries a visible cost while its benefits (fewer injuries, less illness) are diffuse and delayed. This tension is central to the interpretation of Papers 2 and 3. Paper 2 found the strongest heat–injury association among piece-rate agricultural workers, and Paper 3 found that fidelity to scheduled breaks fell sharply on piece-rate days.

### 2.4.5 Summary of the background evidence

Taken together, the background literature establishes that occupational heat is physiologically hazardous, that associations with injury are consistent, that associations with kidney outcomes are plausible but uncertain, and that productivity losses create incentives that work against protection {{kjellstrom2016;flouris2018;spector2019}}. What the literature had not established at the start of this programme was how strong and how certain the pooled evidence was once study quality was formally assessed, what the exposure–response relationship looked like when exposure was estimated at the worksite, which groups of workers bore the greatest risk, and whether a practical protective programme could be implemented on real construction sites. These four questions map onto the three papers.

## 2.5 Protection against occupational heat

### 2.5.1 The hierarchy of controls applied to heat

Occupational health practice organises protective measures in a hierarchy, from eliminating the hazard, through engineering and administrative controls, to personal protective equipment, with measures higher in the hierarchy generally preferred. Applied to outdoor heat, elimination is rarely possible, but some work can be rescheduled to cooler times of day or year. Engineering controls include shade structures, mechanisation of the heaviest tasks and cooled rest areas. Administrative controls include work–rest schedules, acclimatisation programmes, hydration provision, buddy systems, training and heat-alert procedures. Personal measures include cooling garments and lighter protective clothing where safety allows {{lucas2014;parsons2014}}.

Most of these measures are well known, inexpensive and described in guidance. What is less well known is whether they are implemented, by whom and under what conditions. {{lucas2014|n}} noted that the ergonomic principles for heat are long established but that their application lags behind, particularly where workers lack control over the pace of their work. Paper 1 found that almost no studies meeting its inclusion criteria evaluated protective interventions, which means the evidence for the effectiveness of these well-known measures in real outdoor workplaces is largely indirect.

### 2.5.2 Work–rest schedules and their preconditions

Work–rest schedules are the administrative control most directly tied to WBGT. Guidance tables typically specify the proportion of each hour that should be spent resting at a given WBGT and metabolic rate {{parsons2014}}. In principle, this is a simple and powerful lever: rest reduces metabolic heat production, allowing core temperature to recover. In practice, a work–rest schedule is only as good as the conditions that allow workers to follow it. Rest must be possible (there must be somewhere shaded to rest), permitted (supervisors must allow it), affordable (it must not reduce pay) and acceptable (it must not mark the worker as weak or lazy in the eyes of the crew). Each of these preconditions is a matter of work organisation rather than physiology.

This observation shaped the design of Paper 3. The programme bundled the work–rest schedule with shaded rest areas, hydration stations, buddy checks and supervisor training, on the reasoning that a schedule without these supports would be unlikely to be followed. The process evaluation was designed to test that reasoning by measuring which components were delivered and taken up, and by asking workers and supervisors why.

### 2.5.3 Payment systems and heat

Piece-rate payment, in which workers are paid by the quantity of output rather than by time, is common in agriculture and present in some construction trades. Piece rates create an incentive to work faster and to forgo breaks, which in hot conditions directly increases metabolic heat production and reduces recovery time. The argument that piece rates may amplify heat risk is longstanding in the occupational health literature, but direct quantitative evidence is limited {{spector2019}}. The thesis contributes on this point from two directions: Paper 2 estimates effect modification of the heat–injury association by payment type, and Paper 3 describes, through both fidelity data and interviews, how piece-rate payment shaped the uptake of scheduled rest.

### 2.5.4 The policy context

Heat protection sits at the intersection of occupational health regulation, public health heat-action planning and climate adaptation {{ebi2021;kjellstrom2016}}. In many jurisdictions, heat-health action plans are oriented towards the general population, especially older people, and pay limited attention to workers. Occupational regulation of heat varies widely, from general duties of care to specific WBGT-linked requirements. This thesis does not undertake a policy analysis, but Chapter 6 draws out the policy implications of the findings, in particular the case for including pay protection, meaning that workers do not lose income for taking heat-related rest, in heat plans and in the design of a definitive trial.

## 2.6 Conceptual framework

The background reviewed above suggests a framework in which heat-related harm to workers arises from the interaction of environmental heat, the body and the organisation of work. Figure 2.1 presents the framework used to organise the thesis.

> **Figure 2.1** Conceptual framework of the thesis. Environmental heat (air temperature, humidity, radiant heat, air movement; summarised as WBGT) and work demands (metabolic rate, clothing, pace) combine as heat stress. Heat stress produces heat strain, moderated by individual factors (acclimatisation, age, hydration, health). Heat strain leads to acute outcomes (heat illness, cognitive impairment, injury) and subacute outcomes (dehydration, kidney effects). Work organisation (payment system, supervision, rest provision, crew norms) acts at two points: it sets work demands, and it determines whether protective measures (work–rest, shade, hydration, buddy checks) can be taken up. Paper 1 summarises evidence on the heat → outcome arrow; Paper 2 estimates it at worksite level and tests modification by age and payment; Paper 3 intervenes on the protection and work-organisation boxes.

Three features of the framework deserve comment. First, work organisation appears twice: as a determinant of exposure, through the pace and intensity of work, and as a determinant of protection, through whether rest and water are practically available. This dual role is the main theoretical claim that the thesis develops. It is consistent with the ergonomic framing of {{lucas2014|n}} and with the productivity evidence of {{flouris2018|n}}, but the thesis tries to make it empirically concrete.

Second, the framework distinguishes the arrows that the three papers address. Paper 1 asks how strong and how certain the published evidence is for the arrow from heat to outcomes. Paper 2 estimates the same arrow at a finer exposure resolution and asks whether it varies by worker characteristics that proxy for work organisation (age, payment type). Paper 3 moves from observation to intervention, asking whether a programme acting on protection and work organisation can be delivered and whether it shows promise.

Third, the framework is deliberately simple. It omits feedback loops (for instance, workers who feel unwell may slow down, reducing exposure) and longer-term pathways (such as chronic kidney disease, which none of the studies could examine). These omissions are acknowledged as limitations in Chapter 6.

The framework also clarifies the research questions stated in Chapter 1. Research question 1 concerns the strength and certainty of existing evidence for the heat–outcome arrow (Paper 1). Research question 2 concerns the shape, timing and distribution of worksite-level risk (Paper 2). Research question 3 concerns the feasibility and acceptability of protection that acts through work organisation (Paper 3).

## 2.7 Overall research design

### 2.7.1 A sequential, multi-method programme

The thesis is a programme of three linked studies, each designed to answer a question raised by the one before. Table 2.1 summarises them. The sequence moved from synthesis, to observation, to intervention: first establishing what the published evidence could and could not support, then producing new observational evidence designed to close the most important gaps, and finally testing whether a protective programme informed by both could be delivered in practice.

The logic of the sequence is set out in the two bridging chapters that separate the papers. In brief, Paper 1 showed that the evidence base was consistent in direction but of low or very low certainty, that exposure was nearly always assigned from weather stations rather than worksites, and that interventions were almost entirely unstudied. Paper 2 addressed the exposure gap and examined who was most at risk, finding that risk was immediate and concentrated in young and piece-rate workers. That finding pointed towards work organisation as the lever for protection, which Paper 3 tested.

A thesis by publication imposes its own discipline on this sequence. Each paper had to stand alone for a journal readership, which meant restating some background and methods in each, while the thesis as a whole had to show a cumulative argument. I have tried to keep the papers as they were submitted, with only minor changes for consistency of terminology and cross-referencing, and to carry the cumulative argument in this chapter, the bridges and Chapter 6. Where the papers' own framing differs slightly from the thesis framing, for instance where Paper 1 describes its gaps in terms of the review literature rather than the conceptual framework in Section 2.6, I have left the paper unchanged and drawn the connection in the linking text.

### 2.7.2 Philosophical stance

The programme combines quantitative and qualitative methods, and it is worth being explicit about the assumptions that make this combination coherent. I adopted a pragmatic stance in which the choice of method follows from the question rather than from a prior commitment to a single paradigm [source needed]. Papers 1 and 2 work within a broadly realist epidemiological tradition: they assume that there is a causal effect of heat on injury and kidney outcomes that can be estimated, with error, from observational data, and they use explicit tools to reason about bias. Paper 3 adds an interpretive element. Its qualitative component treats workers' and supervisors' accounts as evidence about how the programme was experienced and why it was or was not taken up, not as measurements of a single objective reality.

The two strands are integrated at the level of interpretation rather than data. In Paper 3, feasibility indicators and qualitative themes are read together to explain, for example, why fidelity fell on piece-rate days. Across the thesis, the integrated discussion in Chapter 6 brings the quantitative evidence of Papers 1 and 2 into conversation with the qualitative explanation from Paper 3. This form of integration is modest, and its limits are discussed in Chapter 6.

### 2.7.3 Causal reasoning across the programme

Each study had to address a different version of the same problem: how to move from association towards a causal claim without a randomised trial. The thesis uses three tools for this. Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) make assumptions about confounding explicit and guide the selection of adjustment variables, distinguishing confounders, which should be adjusted for, from mediators and colliders, which in general should not {{greenland1999}}. Self-controlled designs, in which each case serves as its own control, remove confounding by stable individual characteristics. And the target trial framework, in which an observational analysis is designed to emulate a hypothetical randomised trial with a specified population, intervention, comparator, outcome and time zero, clarifies what question an analysis is really answering and exposes design flaws such as immortal time {{hernan2016}}. These tools are introduced briefly here and applied in the relevant papers.

## 2.8 Paper 1: systematic review and meta-analysis

### 2.8.1 Rationale

A systematic review was the right first step because the literature on occupational heat had grown quickly and unevenly, and because existing reviews had either focused on one outcome, not formally assessed certainty, or pooled studies with very different exposure metrics without examining the consequences {{spector2019;flouris2018}}. A new review that assessed injury and kidney outcomes together, applied a consistent risk-of-bias assessment and graded certainty would establish a clear baseline for the rest of the thesis. It would also show, systematically rather than impressionistically, where the gaps lay, so that the design of Papers 2 and 3 could be justified against the evidence rather than against my own reading of it.

### 2.8.2 Design choices

The review was reported according to PRISMA 2020 {{page2021}}. Searches of MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science and CINAHL ran from inception to March 2024; the full strategy is in Appendix A. The review included observational studies of outdoor workers that reported a quantitative association between heat exposure, measured as WBGT or ambient temperature, and either occupational injury or kidney-related outcomes (clinically diagnosed acute kidney injury or reduced eGFR markers).

Three methodological choices are worth highlighting here because they bear on how the results should be read. First, pooling used random-effects models {{dersimonian1986}}, on the expectation that the true effect would vary across settings, populations and exposure metrics. Heterogeneity was quantified with the I² statistic {{higgins2002}}, which expresses the proportion of variability in estimates due to between-study differences rather than chance. Second, small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression test {{egger1997}}, which detects asymmetry in the funnel plot that may indicate publication bias among other explanations. Third, risk of bias was assessed with an adaptation of ROBINS-I {{sterne2016}}. ROBINS-I was designed for non-randomised studies of interventions, so its domains were adapted for exposure studies: the "intervention" was reframed as the heat exposure, and the domain on deviations from intended interventions was replaced with a domain on exposure measurement and its timing relative to the outcome. The adaptation is described in Chapter 3. Certainty of evidence for each outcome was then rated with GRADE {{guyatt2008}}, which starts observational evidence at low certainty and allows downgrading for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias, and upgrading for features such as a dose–response gradient.

A fourth choice, harmonising exposure metrics, deserves mention because it shaped what could be pooled. Injury studies that reported associations per unit of WBGT above a threshold could be combined directly, whereas studies reporting ambient temperature or categorical comparisons required conversion or separate analysis. Kidney studies more often compared heat-exposed with unexposed workers (by job, season or shift) than reported a continuous exposure metric, which is why the kidney result is expressed as an odds ratio for a binary comparison. The consequence is that the two pooled estimates are not directly comparable in magnitude, a point taken up in Chapter 3.

### 2.8.3 What Paper 1 found

Paper 1 included 27 studies (19 cohort and 8 cross-sectional) from 9 countries, with a total of about 84,000 workers. Fourteen studies reported injury outcomes and 11 reported kidney outcomes, with 2 studies reporting both. The pooled relative risk of injury was 1.05 (95% CI 1.03–1.08) per 1 °C increase in WBGT above 28 °C, with I² = 62%. For kidney outcomes, the pooled odds ratio comparing heat-exposed with unexposed workers was 1.47 (1.21–1.79), with I² = 71%. Egger's test gave p = .09 for injury and p = .04 for kidney outcomes, the latter suggesting small-study effects. Certainty was graded low for injury and very low for kidney outcomes. Almost no included studies evaluated protective interventions, and exposure was nearly always assigned from weather stations rather than measured at worksites. These results, and the gaps they exposed, are the starting point for Paper 2.

## 2.9 Paper 2: time-stratified case-crossover study

### 2.9.1 Why a case-crossover design

Paper 2 needed a design that could estimate the short-term effect of daily heat exposure on injury risk using routinely collected injury records, without data on the workers who were not injured. The case-crossover design meets this need. Each injured worker contributes one case day (the day of injury) and several control days, and exposure on the case day is compared with exposure on the control days for the same person. Because each worker is compared with themselves, characteristics that do not change over the short comparison window, such as sex, underlying health, occupation and usual workplace, cannot confound the association.

The time-stratified variant selects control days from the same calendar month and the same day of the week as the case day. This choice controls by design for long-term trends, seasonality and day-of-week patterns in both heat and injury, and it avoids the overlap bias that can arise with some other control-selection schemes [source needed]. The design can be analysed equivalently as conditional logistic regression or as conditional Poisson regression on daily counts stratified by month, year and day of week; Paper 2 used the latter because it accommodates overdispersion and integrates easily with distributed lag models.

An alternative would have been a cohort design using employer records of hours worked as the denominator. Such records were not available for most workers in the claims data, and where they existed they were incomplete for seasonal agricultural labour. The case-crossover design avoids the need for a denominator, at the cost of assuming that the population at risk does not change sharply within each month-by-weekday stratum. That assumption is plausible for most of the year but may be strained at the start and end of harvest, a point examined in the sensitivity analyses.

### 2.9.2 Exposure–lag–response modelling

The relationship between heat and injury may be non-linear (risk may be flat at moderate temperatures and rise above a threshold) and delayed (heat on one day may raise risk on the next, through fatigue, sleep loss or dehydration). Distributed lag non-linear models (DLNMs) estimate both dimensions at once by building a cross-basis that describes how risk varies across exposure levels and across lags simultaneously {{gasparrini2010}}. Paper 2 modelled lags of 0 to 3 days. Attributable fractions, the proportion of injuries attributable to heat above the reference temperature, were estimated using methods developed for temperature–mortality research {{gasparrini2015}}. Translating a relative risk into an attributable fraction is useful for policy because it expresses the burden in terms of injuries that might be prevented, but it depends on the choice of reference temperature and on the assumption that the estimated association is causal; both caveats are stated in Chapter 4.

### 2.9.3 Setting, data and exposure estimation

The study used workers' compensation claims for 2015–2023 from the Coastal Region Workers' Compensation Board, covering agriculture and construction. There were 18,406 injury claims: 7,920 in agriculture and 10,486 in construction. Each claim recorded the date and location of injury, the worker's age, the industry and, in agriculture, whether the worker was paid by piece rate.

Worksite WBGT was estimated from 46 weather stations across the region. For each station-day, WBGT was modelled from air temperature, humidity, wind speed and solar radiation, and then interpolated to the worksite location with a land-surface correction that adjusted for the difference in surface characteristics between the station and the worksite. This approach aimed to address the main exposure gap identified in Paper 1: the reliance on a single station or regional average that may not represent conditions where work actually takes place. It still yields modelled rather than measured exposure, and it cannot capture individual-level variation such as time spent in shade, which is discussed as a limitation in Chapters 4 and 6.

Workers' compensation data have well-known limitations as a source of injury outcomes. Not all injuries are reported, and reporting may depend on the severity of the injury, the worker's employment status and their confidence that a claim will not cost them their job. Under-reporting is a threat to validity mainly if it varies with temperature on a day-to-day basis within a stratum, which seems less likely than overall under-reporting but cannot be excluded. The negative-control analysis described below was designed in part to detect this.

### 2.9.4 Confounding and sensitivity analyses

Confounders were identified with a DAG {{greenland1999}}. Because humidity is a component of WBGT, it was not adjusted separately; doing so would have removed part of the exposure of interest. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5), which co-varies with hot weather and may affect injury risk through respiratory and cognitive effects, and public holidays, which affect both work patterns and claim reporting, were included as time-varying covariates.

Three sensitivity analyses tested the robustness of the main estimate. Alternative lag structures checked that the conclusion did not depend on the choice of lag 0–3. Excluding heatwave days checked whether the association was driven by a few extreme events. And a negative-control outcome, injuries occurring on non-work days, tested for residual confounding or reporting artefacts: if heat were associated with non-work injuries to the same degree as work injuries, this would suggest that something other than occupational exposure was driving the association. The study was reported according to STROBE {{vonelm2007}}.

### 2.9.5 What Paper 2 found

Injury risk rose above a WBGT of about 26 °C. The relative risk at 31 °C compared with 24 °C was 1.21 (1.12–1.31), with most of the effect at lag 0. The association was stronger in workers under 25 years (RR 1.34) and in piece-rate agricultural workers (RR 1.38). About 4.1% of injuries were attributable to heat. The immediacy of the effect and its concentration in groups with the least control over the pace of work framed the design of Paper 3.

## 2.10 Paper 3: mixed-methods feasibility study

### 2.10.1 Why a feasibility study

Papers 1 and 2 established that worksite heat increases injury risk and that the risk is concentrated where work organisation limits rest. The natural next question is whether a programme that changes work organisation can reduce heat strain. Answering that question definitively would require a cluster randomised trial, which is costly and depends on uncertainties that are best resolved first: whether crews and workers can be recruited, whether the programme can be delivered as intended, whether outcomes can be measured, and whether the programme is acceptable to those who must deliver and receive it. The Medical Research Council guidance on complex interventions recommends a feasibility phase precisely to address such uncertainties before a full evaluation {{craig2008;skivington2021}}.

### 2.10.2 Developing the programme

The programme was developed with reference to the MRC framework {{craig2008;skivington2021}}. The updated framework emphasises core elements that run across all phases of research: considering context, developing and refining programme theory, engaging stakeholders, identifying key uncertainties, refining the intervention and considering economic aspects {{skivington2021}}. In this study, context was understood through Papers 1 and 2 and through early conversations with site managers and worker representatives; programme theory followed the conceptual framework in Section 2.6; and the key uncertainties concerned fidelity and acceptability, especially under piece-rate pay.

The programme had five components: WBGT-triggered work–rest schedules, shaded rest areas, hydration stations, buddy checks (in which paired workers watched each other for signs of heat illness), and training for supervisors. The bundle reflects the argument in Section 2.5.2 that a schedule alone is unlikely to be followed without the material and social conditions that make rest possible. In MRC terms, it is a complex intervention: it has several interacting components, it requires behaviour change by both workers and supervisors, and its effects are likely to depend on context.

### 2.10.3 Design and measures

The study took place over 8 weeks in summer 2025 with 6 construction crews (n = 74 workers) at 3 sites. Four crews received the programme and 2 served as comparison crews; allocation was not randomised. The comparison crews were included to test the logistics of outcome measurement in a non-intervention setting, not to estimate effectiveness, and all comparisons between arms are exploratory. Construction rather than agriculture was chosen because the partner sites could commit to the full eight weeks and because construction crews have stable supervisors, which made the supervisor-training component practical; this choice limits the direct transfer of findings to agricultural piece-rate work, a limitation discussed in Chapter 6.

Feasibility was assessed through recruitment (proportion of eligible workers consenting), retention, fidelity (proportion of scheduled breaks taken) and the completeness of outcome measures. Candidate outcomes for a future trial were heat-symptom reports per 100 shifts and urine specific gravity at the end of the shift, with a value of 1.020 or above taken as indicating inadequate hydration.

### 2.10.4 Process evaluation and qualitative methods

The process evaluation followed MRC guidance, which directs attention to implementation (what was delivered and how), mechanisms of impact (how participants responded) and context (what external factors shaped implementation and outcomes) {{moore2015}}. Quantitative process data (fidelity logs, rest-area use, water consumption) were combined with qualitative data from 22 semi-structured interviews and 3 focus groups with workers and supervisors. The topic guides are reproduced in Appendix B.

The qualitative sample size was guided by information power {{malterud2016}}, which holds that the number of participants needed depends on the study aim, the specificity of the sample, the use of established theory, the quality of dialogue and the analysis strategy. A narrow aim, a sample with specific experience of the programme, and an analysis informed by the conceptual framework all suggested that a moderate sample would hold sufficient information power. Data were analysed using thematic analysis following the six phases described by {{braun2006|n}}: familiarisation, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report. Reporting followed COREQ {{tong2007}}.

### 2.10.5 Progression criteria

Progression criteria are pre-specified thresholds that determine whether a feasibility study supports moving to a definitive trial, supports moving with modifications, or does not support moving. Criteria were set in advance for recruitment, retention and fidelity, using a traffic-light scheme in which green indicates proceed, amber proceed with changes, and red do not proceed without substantial redesign. Their application is reported in Chapter 5. Because Paper 3 also raised the possibility of observational follow-up across a larger number of sites, it drew on the target trial framework {{hernan2016}} to set out how such follow-up could be designed to emulate the trial that it could not yet run.

## 2.11 Cross-cutting matters

### 2.11.1 Synthetic data

> **Sample note.** The data reported in this thesis are synthetic, created to illustrate how such a study is reported. No participants were recruited.

### 2.11.2 Ethics and governance

Paper 1 used only published data and did not require ethical approval. Paper 2 used de-identified administrative claims released under a data-sharing agreement with the Coastal Region Workers' Compensation Board and was approved by the Kestrel Bay University Human Research Ethics Committee. Paper 3 involved direct contact with workers and was approved by the same committee. Particular care was taken over voluntariness: because workers may feel unable to decline a study endorsed by their employer, consent was sought by the research team without supervisors present, workers were told that participation or refusal would not be reported to the employer, and interviews took place away from the work area. Workers who declined the research could still use the shade and water provided at intervention sites, because withholding basic protection from some members of a crew would have been unethical.

### 2.11.3 Data management

Claims data were held on a secure university server with access restricted to the analysis team, and no record contained names or claim numbers. Interview and focus group recordings were transcribed, checked, pseudonymised and then deleted. Analysis code for Papers 1 and 2 was version-controlled, and the review in Paper 1 followed a protocol registered before screening began.

### 2.11.4 Reflexivity

My position as a researcher shaped the programme in ways worth stating. I came to this work from occupational hygiene, a discipline that tends to think about heat in terms of exposure standards and controls. That background made the WBGT-based exposure work of Paper 2 natural to me, but it also inclined me, at first, to treat non-compliance with work–rest schedules as a problem of knowledge or attitude. The interviews in Paper 3 unsettled that view. Workers and supervisors described the decision to rest as an economic and social one, and the theme "breaks cost money" became central to the analysis. I kept a reflexive journal throughout the fieldwork and discussed emerging interpretations with my supervisors and with a worker representative, partly to check that my disciplinary habits were not overriding what participants were saying.

## 2.12 Summary

This chapter has set out the shared background and methods for the thesis. Occupational heat harms workers through physiological and behavioural pathways, and is measured most usefully, though imperfectly, by WBGT. The evidence on injury is consistent, on kidney outcomes plausible but uncertain, and on protection almost absent. Work organisation shapes both how much heat a worker experiences and whether protective measures can be taken up, and the conceptual framework in Section 2.6 places it at the centre of the thesis. The three studies move from synthesis, through worksite-level observation, to a feasibility test of protection, each using a design chosen to answer the question left by the one before.

Chapter 3 presents the first of these studies: a systematic review and meta-analysis of the evidence linking occupational heat exposure to injury and kidney outcomes in outdoor workers.

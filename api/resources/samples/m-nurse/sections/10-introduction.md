# Chapter 1: Introduction

This report describes a quality improvement (QI) project that introduced purposeful hourly rounding on 5 West, an adult medical-surgical unit at Kestrel Bay General Hospital, with the aim of reducing inpatient falls. The report follows the Standards for QUality Improvement Reporting Excellence, SQUIRE 2.0, which ask authors of improvement work to make explicit the theory behind their changes, the context in which the work took place, and how the intervention itself was studied {{ogrinc2016}}. In keeping with that structure, this first chapter sets out the problem, summarizes in brief what was already known, explains why purposeful rounding was chosen as the change to test, and states the specific aim against which the project was judged. The evidence is examined in detail in Chapter 2.

## 1.1 Problem Description

### 1.1.1 Falls as a nursing-sensitive harm

A fall in hospital is rarely a single event with a single cause. It is usually the meeting point of a patient's vulnerability, such as an unsteady gait, a new sedative, nocturia or delirium, with a moment when the environment and the care team fail to anticipate a need. Because nurses and nursing assistants are the clinicians present around the clock, falls have long been treated as a nursing-sensitive indicator: a measure of harm that the organization and delivery of nursing care can plausibly influence {{currie2008;oliver2010}}. National data from the United States show that falls occur across every type of adult inpatient unit, but that medical units carry some of the highest rates {{bouldin2013}}. The patients on such units tend to be older, more acutely unwell and more often confused than those admitted for planned surgery, and they spend long periods alone in unfamiliar rooms.

The consequences of a fall reach beyond the immediate injury. A minority of falls cause fractures or head injuries, and some of these lead to death, but even falls without physical harm can lengthen a stay, prompt further investigations and erode a patient's confidence in walking, which in turn hastens deconditioning {{oliver2010;tjc2015}}. Falls also carry a resource cost for hospitals, much of it driven by longer admissions rather than by the treatment of injury itself {{morello2015}}. For nurses, a fall on their shift is a source of distress and, increasingly, of scrutiny through incident review. For all of these reasons, fall prevention has become a standard expectation of accrediting bodies and a recurring item on hospital safety dashboards {{tjc2015}}.

### 1.1.2 The local problem on 5 West

5 West is a 32-bed adult medical-surgical unit in Kestrel Bay General Hospital, a 410-bed community teaching hospital. The unit admits mainly older adults, with a mean patient age of 67 years, and receives a mixture of medical admissions from the emergency department and post-operative patients from general and orthopedic surgery. At the start of the project the unit employed 48 registered nurses (RNs) and 22 nursing assistants (NAs). I worked on the unit as a clinical nurse throughout the project, which gave me a close understanding of its routines and also, as Chapter 5 discusses, a potential source of bias.

The hospital sets an internal target of 3.0 falls per 1,000 patient-days for adult inpatient units. During the baseline year, from July 2023 to June 2024, 5 West recorded 46 falls over 10,159 patient-days, a rate of 4.53 falls per 1,000 patient-days. Nine of those falls resulted in injury, a rate of 0.89 injurious falls per 1,000 patient-days. No month in the baseline year met the hospital target: the lowest monthly rate was 3.48 (August 2023) and the highest 6.04 (September 2023), and the median monthly rate was 4.71. Figure 1.1 shows the baseline rate aggregated by quarter, which smooths the month-to-month variation and makes plain that the excess over target was persistent rather than the product of one or two bad months.

The unit was not without fall-prevention measures. In line with hospital policy, nurses scored every patient on the Morse Fall Scale at admission and once each shift {{morse1989;morse2009}}. Patients judged to be at high risk were given a bed or chair alarm, and all patients at risk wore yellow non-slip socks. These measures identified risk and signaled it to staff, but they did little to change what happened between assessments. Rounding, in the sense of checking on patients at regular intervals, existed only informally. Experienced staff looked in on their patients as workload allowed, but there was no agreed interval, no shared script, no division of labor between RNs and NAs and no documentation. Rounds tended to cluster around medication passes and to thin out during admissions, discharges and the night.

A review of baseline incident reports, undertaken with the site mentor before the project began, pointed to two patterns. First, approximately 70% of baseline falls occurred between 22:00 and 06:00, when staffing was lowest and patients were most likely to wake disoriented and attempt to walk to the bathroom alone. Second, toileting was the most common activity recorded at the time of the fall, and many of the patients who fell had scored as high risk on the Morse Fall Scale. In other words, the unit was already identifying the patients most likely to fall, but it lacked a reliable process for meeting their predictable needs before they acted on them unassisted.

Two further baseline observations shaped the project. Call-light activations averaged 6.8 per patient-day, suggesting that patients were frequently having to summon help for needs that could have been anticipated. In a short pre-project survey, RNs on the unit (n = 41 of 48) gave a mean rating of 3.4 on a five-point agreement scale to the statement "I know which patients are at high risk of falling," indicating that knowledge of risk, though present, was not consistently shared across the team. On the unit's internal patient experience survey, 71% of respondents gave the top-box rating to the item asking whether staff responded promptly to their needs. That figure was not poor by the hospital's standards, but it meant that close to three patients in ten did not feel their needs were met quickly, a perception that the bedside staff recognized from their own experience of busy shifts.

The cost of this pattern was felt at several levels. For patients, each fall brought pain, investigations and, in some cases, a longer stay and a more cautious discharge plan. For staff, every fall triggered an incident report and a review that, before the project, tended to focus on whether the Morse Fall Scale had been completed and the alarm switched on, rather than on why the patient had got up alone. Several nurses described these reviews as dispiriting: the required boxes had been ticked, and yet the patient had still fallen. For the hospital, the unit's persistent position above target drew attention at the monthly patient safety committee and placed 5 West among the units expected to submit an improvement plan.

Taken together, these observations defined the local problem: a fall rate persistently above target, concentrated overnight and around toileting, on a unit whose risk-identification processes were not matched by a dependable routine of proactive care.

## 1.2 Available Knowledge in Brief

The literature on hospital fall prevention is extensive, and Chapter 2 reviews it in detail. Four points from that literature framed the design of this project.

First, risk assessment alone does not prevent falls. Scales such as the Morse Fall Scale and the Hendrich II Fall Risk Model were developed to help nurses identify fall-prone patients {{morse1989;hendrich2003}}, but their predictive accuracy varies between settings and declines when they are used repeatedly without local testing {{aranda2013}}. A risk score is useful only if it triggers an action.

Second, programs that combine several interventions appear more effective than single measures. Systematic reviews of inpatient fall prevention suggest that multicomponent programs can reduce falls by a meaningful margin, though the pooled estimates are imprecise and the results vary widely between hospitals {{hempel2013;miakelye2013;cameron2018}}. A cluster randomized trial of a fall-prevention toolkit that linked nurse-completed risk assessment to tailored interventions showed a reduction in falls but not in injurious falls {{dykes2010}}. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality toolkit emphasizes that how a program is implemented and sustained matters at least as much as which components it contains {{ahrq2013}}.

Third, the evidence for rounding specifically is encouraging but weak in important respects. Early multisite work suggested that structured rounds at one- or two-hour intervals reduced call-light use and falls and improved patient satisfaction {{meade2006}}, and a later systematic review judged the evidence that rounding reduces falls to be of moderate strength {{mitchell2014}}. Integrative reviews and single-site implementation projects have reported similar findings {{hicks2015;daniels2016}}. However, a large realist evaluation in English hospitals found that rounding, as widely adopted and adapted, was frequently delivered with low fidelity and produced little measurable benefit {{harris2019}}. The lesson drawn for this project was that rounding is likely to work only when it is purposeful, consistently delivered and monitored.

Fourth, any intervention that asks nurses to do more must take workload seriously. Nurse staffing and workload are associated with patient safety outcomes and with nurse burnout {{aiken2002}}, and a human factors perspective suggests that the design of work, not only the number of staff, determines how heavy that workload feels {{carayon2008}}. A rounding program that adds tasks without removing others risks becoming a documentation ritual rather than a change in care.

## 1.3 Rationale

The choice of purposeful hourly rounding followed from the local problem and from the evidence summarized above. The causal reasoning, which SQUIRE 2.0 encourages authors to set out explicitly {{ogrinc2016}}, can be stated simply. Many falls on 5 West occurred when patients, often at high risk and often at night, got up alone to meet a need, most commonly toileting. If staff anticipated those needs at predictable intervals, offering the toilet, relieving pain, repositioning and placing the call light and personal items within reach, patients would have fewer reasons to get up unassisted. Fewer unassisted mobilizations among high-risk patients should, in turn, produce fewer falls. A secondary effect, reported in the rounding literature {{meade2006;mitchell2014}}, is that anticipating needs reduces the number of times patients have to use the call light, which may partly offset the time that rounding takes.

Rounding also suited the unit's existing resources. It required no capital purchase, could be delivered by the RNs and NAs already on shift, and could be layered onto the Morse Fall Scale assessments, alarms and non-slip socks that the unit already used, turning risk identification into risk response. It was compatible with the multifactorial approach recommended in the literature {{oliver2010;ahrq2013}}, rather than competing with it.

Other options were considered and set aside. Extending bed and chair alarms to every patient would have added noise without addressing the underlying need to get up, and alarms already sounded for many high-risk patients who fell; an alarm signals that a fall may be imminent, but it does not prevent the patient from rising. Continuous observation by sitters was reserved by hospital policy for patients at risk of self-harm or severe agitation and was neither affordable nor appropriate as a unit-wide measure. Video monitoring was under discussion at hospital level but could not be introduced within the project's time frame. A program of patient education alone was judged unlikely to help the patients most at risk, many of whom were confused or drowsy at night. Rounding was the option that addressed the identified mechanism, toileting-related mobilization by high-risk patients, using resources the unit already had.

The Model for Improvement provided the method {{langley2009}}. Its three framing questions, concerning what the team is trying to accomplish, how it will know that a change is an improvement, and what changes might result in improvement, translated directly into the project's aim, its family of measures and its rounding protocol. Its Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles allowed the protocol to be tested on a small scale, adapted and then spread, an approach that suited a unit whose staff were skeptical of initiatives imposed without consultation. Because the project also required staff to change entrenched habits, Kotter's eight-stage model of organizational change {{kotter1996}} informed how urgency was established, how a guiding team of RNs, NAs and the nurse manager was formed, and how early gains were communicated. Both frameworks are discussed further in Chapter 2.

## 1.4 Specific Aim

The project's specific aim, written in SMART form, was:

> To reduce the 5 West fall rate by 30%, from 4.53 to 3.17 or fewer falls per 1,000 patient-days, within nine months of starting purposeful hourly rounding (July 2024 to March 2025), while keeping rounding documented in at least 85% of audited patient-hours.

The 30% target was chosen deliberately. It was ambitious enough to bring the unit close to the hospital's target of 3.0, yet it lay within the range of effect that systematic reviews of multicomponent programs suggest is achievable {{miakelye2013}}. The nine-month window allowed three PDSA cycles and enough monthly data points to apply run-chart rules with some confidence. The process threshold of 85% reflected the lesson from the rounding literature that low fidelity undermines benefit {{harris2019}}: an outcome change without reliable delivery of the intervention would be difficult to attribute to rounding at all.

Three guiding questions followed from the aim and organized the study of the intervention:

1. Was the introduction of purposeful hourly rounding followed by a non-random reduction in the monthly fall rate and in injurious falls on 5 West?
2. To what extent was rounding delivered and documented as intended across shifts and across the three PDSA cycles?
3. Did rounding produce unintended consequences, in particular an increase in perceived staff workload, a deterioration in patient-reported responsiveness or a rise in other nursing-sensitive harms such as pressure injuries?

These questions correspond to the outcome, process and balancing measures defined in Chapter 3, and each is answered in Chapter 4 and interpreted in Chapter 5.

## 1.5 Significance for Nursing Leadership

The project matters beyond 5 West for three reasons. First, it addresses a harm that patients and families experience directly and that hospitals are expected to prevent {{tjc2015}}. Second, it tests an intervention that is both popular and contested. Rounding programs have spread widely, yet the evidence suggests that their value depends on fidelity and context {{harris2019}}; a carefully documented local project, reported with its process data, adds to the practical knowledge of when rounding helps and when it becomes ritual. Third, it is an exercise in nurse-led improvement. The project was designed and led by a clinical nurse on the unit, with the nurse manager as sponsor, and its methods are ones that any unit-based nurse leader could reproduce. The leadership competencies it required, such as framing a problem with data, building a coalition, adapting a change in response to staff feedback and sustaining attention once early gains appear, are central to the master's-level role this degree prepares nurses to take on.

# Chapter 2: Review of the Evidence

This chapter examines the evidence that informed the design of the project. It is organized by argument rather than by source. Section 2.1 explains how the evidence was located and appraised. Section 2.2 establishes why inpatient falls matter, in terms of harm and cost. Section 2.3 considers the risk-assessment tools on which most fall-prevention programs depend, and their limits. Section 2.4 reviews multifactorial programs, the dominant model of hospital fall prevention. Section 2.5 examines the evidence for rounding specifically and argues that it is more mixed than its widespread adoption implies. Section 2.6 considers nurse workload as both a context for falls and a constraint on any intervention that asks more of nurses. Section 2.7 presents the two conceptual frameworks that guided the project, and Section 2.8 draws the strands together to show how the evidence shaped the intervention described in Chapter 3.

## 2.1 Approach to the Review

The review was undertaken as an integrative, rather than systematic, synthesis. Its purpose was to inform a local improvement project, not to produce a pooled estimate of effect, and it therefore drew on several kinds of evidence: systematic reviews and meta-analyses, randomized and quasi-experimental studies, implementation reports, realist evaluation, clinical guidance and methodological texts on improvement. I searched CINAHL, PubMed and the Cochrane Library for English-language publications using combinations of terms for inpatient falls, fall risk assessment, fall prevention programs, hourly or intentional rounding and nurse workload. Reference lists of key reviews were hand-searched, and national guidance on hospital fall prevention was obtained from the websites of the relevant agencies.

Sources were prioritized in a simple hierarchy. Where a systematic review addressed a question, it was used as the primary source, and individual studies were cited to illustrate or qualify its conclusions. Where no review existed, or where reviews disagreed, the strongest available primary studies were examined directly. For each source I considered the design, setting and population, how falls were defined and measured, whether the intervention was described in enough detail to be reproduced, and whether fidelity was reported. This last criterion proved especially important for the rounding literature, as Section 2.5 shows.

## 2.2 The Harm and Cost of Inpatient Falls

### 2.2.1 Frequency and distribution

Falls are among the most frequently reported adverse events in acute hospitals. The largest descriptive study available, drawn from a national nursing quality database in the United States, analyzed more than 88 million patient-days across over a thousand hospitals and reported an overall rate of 3.56 falls per 1,000 patient-days, with roughly a quarter of falls resulting in some injury {{bouldin2013}}. The same study found that rates differed by unit type, with medical units recording the highest rates and surgical units the lowest. A review of international data reached a broadly similar conclusion, reporting whole-hospital rates of between three and five falls per 1,000 bed-days {{oliver2010}}.

These figures are useful for orientation but should be interpreted with care. Falls are identified mainly through voluntary incident reporting, which depends on staff recognizing an event as a fall and taking the time to report it. Units with a strong reporting culture may therefore appear to have higher rates than units where falls go unrecorded, and comparisons across hospitals or countries are vulnerable to differences in definition {{currie2008}}. For a local improvement project, the more relevant comparison is a unit with itself over time, using a consistent definition and data source. This principle informed the decision, described in Chapter 3, to use the hospital's existing incident-reporting system and to keep its definition of a fall unchanged throughout the project.

The distribution of falls across unit types matters for this project because 5 West is a mixed medical-surgical unit with a predominantly older population. The evidence suggests that such units sit toward the higher end of the range {{bouldin2013}}, and that the patients who fall there share recognizable characteristics: advanced age, cognitive impairment, urinary urgency or frequency, impaired mobility and the use of medications that affect balance or alertness {{oliver2010;currie2008}}. Many of these are features of acute illness itself rather than stable traits, which helps to explain why fall risk changes during an admission and why a single assessment at admission is unlikely to be sufficient.

### 2.2.2 Consequences for patients

Most falls do not cause serious physical injury, but a meaningful minority do. A review of hospital falls reported that between 1% and 3% resulted in fracture {{oliver2010}}, and a sentinel-event alert from The Joint Commission emphasized that falls with injury, particularly head injury and hip fracture, can lead to death and that a substantial proportion of serious fall events reported to it had been fatal {{tjc2015}}. Older patients are especially vulnerable, both because they are more likely to fall and because they are less likely to recover fully from injury.

The harm of a fall is not limited to injury. Patients who fall may develop a fear of falling that leads them to restrict their own mobility, and staff may respond by restricting it further through bed rest or increased use of alarms, a combination that accelerates functional decline {{oliver2010}}. Falls can also prompt investigations, such as imaging for suspected fractures or head injury, that add discomfort and delay. For this reason, a project that reduces falls without injury still has value, a point made explicitly in the cost literature discussed below.

### 2.2.3 Consequences for hospitals

The economic burden of falls has been estimated in several ways. The Joint Commission alert cited an average cost of around $14,000 for a fall with injury in the United States {{tjc2015}}. A prospective cost study in Australian hospitals offers a more detailed picture: patients who fell had a mean increase in length of stay of eight days and mean additional hospital costs of AU$6,669 compared with patients who did not fall, after accounting for other factors {{morello2015}}. Notably, the authors found that much of this additional burden was associated with falls in general, not only with injurious falls, and concluded that prevention programs should target all falls rather than focusing solely on reducing injury {{morello2015}}.

This conclusion has a direct implication for how improvement projects define success. A program judged only on injurious falls, which are relatively rare on a single unit, would struggle to detect change within a reasonable time frame and would ignore much of the burden. This project therefore adopted the total fall rate as its primary outcome measure, with injurious falls reported as a secondary outcome, an approach consistent with the measurement guidance in the national toolkit {{ahrq2013}}.

## 2.3 Identifying Patients at Risk

### 2.3.1 The Morse Fall Scale and the Hendrich II model

Most hospital fall-prevention programs begin with a structured assessment of fall risk. The Morse Fall Scale, developed in the late 1980s, is among the most widely used of these tools in North America {{morse1989}}. It combines a small number of observable patient characteristics, scored at the bedside, into a total score that classifies patients by level of risk, and its brevity is a large part of its appeal on busy units. Its developer later set out a broader program for establishing fall prevention in institutions, in which the scale was one element alongside environmental measures, staff education and evaluation {{morse2009}}. That framing is worth emphasizing: the scale was never intended to stand alone.

The Hendrich II Fall Risk Model was developed later through a large case-control study in an acute tertiary hospital, comparing 355 patients who fell with 780 controls and examining a wide range of candidate risk factors {{hendrich2003}}. More than 600 candidate risk factors were assessed, and the resulting model retains eight assessment parameters, chosen for their predictive value and ease of use at the bedside {{hendrich2003}}. Its strength lies in its derivation from a large sample of acutely ill patients; its weakness, shared with every risk tool, is that its performance outside the setting in which it was developed must be established locally.

### 2.3.2 How well do the tools predict falls?

A systematic review and meta-analysis compared the diagnostic accuracy of the Morse Fall Scale, the STRATIFY tool and the Hendrich II model across studies in acute hospital settings {{aranda2013}}. It found that STRATIFY showed the greatest diagnostic validity of the three, and that the accuracy of the Morse Fall Scale fell when patients were reassessed during their admission. The authors advised that hospitals test any instrument in their own population before relying on it {{aranda2013}}.

This finding complicates the routine practice, followed on 5 West, of rescoring every patient each shift. Repeated assessment may produce scores that drift without corresponding changes in true risk, and staff who complete the same instrument many times may do so by habit rather than by fresh observation. More fundamentally, even a well-calibrated tool only identifies risk. A high score does not prevent a fall unless it reliably triggers actions that reduce the likelihood of one. The guidance literature makes the same point: risk assessment is valuable chiefly as a means of directing tailored interventions to the patients who need them {{ahrq2013;oliver2010}}.

For this project, the practical conclusion was not to replace the Morse Fall Scale, which was embedded in hospital policy and the electronic health record, but to change what followed from it. In the rounding protocol described in Chapter 3, a Morse score of 45 or above became a trigger for proactive toileting and for naming the patient in the charge nurse huddle. The scale was thus used as a prompt for a concrete action rather than as an end in itself. Whether the threshold of 45 was optimally calibrated for 5 West was not tested; it was adopted because it was already the hospital's threshold for high risk.

## 2.4 Multifactorial Fall-Prevention Programs

### 2.4.1 The case for multiple components

Because falls arise from the interaction of several patient and environmental factors, the dominant approach in hospitals has been to combine interventions. A review of hospital fall prevention concluded that the most appropriate approach involves multifactorial interventions with input from several professions, tailored to the individual patient's risk factors {{oliver2010}}. Systematic reviews have tested this proposition more formally. A review of inpatient fall-prevention programs reported that multicomponent programs could reduce the relative risk of falls by as much as 30% {{miakelye2013}}. A companion review of studies in US acute care hospitals found that most interventions, around four in five, included multiple components, among them care rounds, and that the pooled post-intervention incidence rate ratio across eight studies was 0.77 {{hempel2013}}. However, the 95% confidence interval for that estimate crossed one, which means the pooled result was compatible with no effect.

The most recent Cochrane review of falls prevention in care facilities and hospitals reached a similarly cautious conclusion for the hospital setting. It found that multifactorial interventions may reduce the rate of falls in hospitals, with a rate ratio of 0.80, but that the confidence interval again included the possibility of no effect, and subgroup analysis suggested the benefit might be concentrated in subacute rather than acute wards {{cameron2018}}. Earlier reviews had noted the scarcity of randomized trials of fall prevention in acute care {{currie2008}}, and although the evidence base has grown, the uncertainty has not disappeared.

### 2.4.2 Evidence from a randomized trial

One of the most rigorous studies in the acute setting is a cluster randomized trial conducted in four US hospitals {{dykes2010}}. The intervention was a fall-prevention toolkit supported by health information technology and built on a nurse-completed fall-risk assessment, so that the assessment led directly to prevention measures matched to the patient's identified risks. Site-adjusted fall rates were lower on intervention units than on control units, at 3.15 compared with 4.18 per 1,000 patient-days, a statistically significant difference, but there was no significant effect on fall-related injuries {{dykes2010}}.

Two features of this trial were influential in designing the present project. First, the effective element was not the risk assessment itself but its systematic linkage to visible, patient-specific actions. Second, the absence of an effect on injury, in a trial much larger than a single-unit project, suggested that a small local project should not expect to demonstrate a change in injurious falls with any confidence, and should treat that measure as descriptive.

### 2.4.3 Implementation as the active ingredient

Across these reviews, a recurring observation is that programs with similar components produce very different results in different hospitals. The review by Miake-Lye and colleagues identified the themes associated with successful implementation: leadership support, engagement of front-line staff, a multidisciplinary committee, pilot-testing of interventions and staff education {{miakelye2013}}. The review by Hempel and colleagues likewise examined adherence alongside components and effectiveness {{hempel2013}}, and the wide confidence interval around its pooled estimate is consistent with my own reading that programs with similar components may succeed or fail according to how completely they are delivered.

The national toolkit for hospital fall prevention translates this observation into practical guidance {{ahrq2013}}. It treats fall prevention as an organizational change rather than a clinical protocol, and organizes its advice around assessing readiness, managing change, choosing practices, implementing them, measuring falls and sustaining the program. The toolkit's emphasis on readiness and sustainability, and its explicit recommendation to measure both outcomes and the processes intended to produce them, shaped this project's family of measures. The Joint Commission's sentinel-event alert, written in response to reports of serious fall-related harm, similarly treats fall prevention as an organizational responsibility rather than a matter of individual vigilance {{tjc2015}}. That framing encouraged the project team to learn systematically from each fall that did occur, and it informed the post-fall huddle introduced in the third PDSA cycle.

The implication for this project was that the choice of intervention mattered less than the quality of its implementation. A rounding protocol introduced without pilot-testing, staff engagement and process measurement would be at risk of repeating the inconsistent results seen across the literature.

## 2.5 The Evidence for Purposeful Rounding

### 2.5.1 Origins and early evidence

Structured rounding as a deliberate intervention, rather than an informal habit, entered nursing practice largely through a multisite study published in the mid-2000s {{meade2006}}. In a six-week quasi-experimental study on 27 nursing units in 14 hospitals, units conducted rounds at either one-hour or two-hour intervals, using a protocol that specified actions to be completed at each visit. The authors concluded that rounds incorporating specific actions, whether hourly or every two hours, could reduce call-light use, increase patient satisfaction with nursing care and reduce falls {{meade2006}}.

The study was influential, and its protocol, which combined a set of scripted checks with attention to pain, position and toileting, became the template for many later programs, including the "4 Ps" model adapted in this project. It also had important limitations. The study period was short, the design was quasi-experimental rather than randomized, and the study was associated with a consultancy that went on to promote the rounding model it tested, which in my view warrants caution about potential conflicts of interest {{meade2006}}. Six weeks is also a brief window in which to observe a relatively infrequent event such as a fall on any single unit. The finding that two-hourly rounds produced benefits similar to hourly rounds was, however, relevant to this project's decision to round every two hours overnight in order to protect sleep.

### 2.5.2 Systematic and integrative reviews

Later reviews have broadly supported the early findings, while noting the weakness of the underlying studies. A systematic review using the GRADE approach found moderate-strength evidence that hourly rounding programs improve patients' perception of nursing responsiveness, and moderate-strength evidence that they reduce falls and call-light use {{mitchell2014}}. A rating of moderate rather than high is a meaningful qualification. In my reading of the studies such reviews draw on, many used before-and-after designs without concurrent controls, introduced rounding alongside other changes and described their rounding protocols in too little detail to be reproduced, all features that limit confidence in attributing change to rounding itself.

An integrative review focused specifically on falls in acute care summarized several studies in which rounding was followed by a decrease in falls per 1,000 patient-days {{hicks2015}}. Integrative reviews of this kind are useful for identifying patterns but are vulnerable to publication bias: improvement projects that observe no change are less likely to be written up and published, so the available literature may overstate the typical effect. A best-practice implementation project on a medical-surgical unit in the United States illustrates both the promise and the limitations of single-site evidence {{daniels2016}}. Using a structured implementation method, the project increased compliance with a rounding protocol from zero to 64% and reported a 50% decrease in patient falls {{daniels2016}}. The setting closely resembles 5 West, and the result is encouraging. Yet a single unit, an uncontrolled design and compliance well short of full delivery mean that the observed reduction cannot be confidently attributed to rounding alone.

### 2.5.3 A critical counterpoint: rounding at scale

The most substantial challenge to the rounding literature comes from a large realist evaluation of intentional rounding in English acute hospitals {{harris2019}}. Nearly all of the trusts surveyed (97%) had implemented intentional rounding in some way. The evaluation found, however, that fidelity to the original protocol was generally low, with the intervention widely adapted from the form in which it had first been described and tested {{harris2019}}. The authors concluded that the evidence for the effectiveness of intentional rounding, as it was being implemented and adapted in England, was very weak {{harris2019}}.

This evaluation does not show that rounding cannot work. A realist design asks how, for whom and in what circumstances an intervention produces its effects, and a finding of weak effectiveness under low fidelity is, at least in part, a finding about delivery. My own interpretation is that rounding works, when it does, through mechanisms such as anticipating needs, building patients' confidence that staff will return and creating regular opportunities for assessment, and that these mechanisms are easily lost when rounding becomes a documentation task. Its most important lesson for a project like this one is that rounding documented is not the same as rounding done. A high documentation rate may coexist with perfunctory visits, and a process measure based solely on documentation may overstate fidelity.

### 2.5.4 Implications for this project

Taken together, the rounding evidence supports cautious optimism rather than confidence. The direction of effect across reviews and implementation projects is consistent, and the proposed mechanism, anticipating patients' needs before they act on them unassisted, is plausible and matches the pattern of falls seen on 5 West. But the evidence is weakened by uncontrolled designs, small numbers, possible publication bias and, above all, inconsistent fidelity {{mitchell2014;harris2019}}.

Four design decisions followed. First, the rounding protocol was made purposeful: each visit had a defined content, the "4 Ps" plus an alarm and environment check, rather than being a simple observation. Second, the protocol preserved the relational element through a closing question offering further help and signaling that the staff member had time. Third, fidelity was measured directly through monthly audits of documentation, and this was supplemented in PDSA 3 by charge nurse huddles that brought high-risk patients and near-misses into open discussion, providing a check on whether rounds were substantive. Fourth, the project was designed from the outset as a series of small tests, so that the protocol could be adapted to the unit rather than imposed on it, in the hope of avoiding the drift from purpose to ritual that the English evaluation describes {{harris2019}}.

## 2.6 Nurse Workload and Patient Safety

Any intervention that adds a recurring task to nurses' work must be examined for its effect on workload, both because workload affects patient safety and because an intervention perceived as burdensome is unlikely to be sustained. A landmark study of hospital nurse staffing found that each additional patient per nurse was associated with a 7% increase in the likelihood of a patient dying within 30 days of admission, together with higher odds of nurse burnout and job dissatisfaction {{aiken2002}}. Although that study did not examine falls, it established that nurse workload is a patient-safety issue and not merely a matter of staff welfare.

A human factors perspective adds an important nuance {{carayon2008}}. Workload is shaped not only by staffing levels and patient acuity but by the design of the work system: the tools nurses use, the physical layout of the unit, the organization of tasks and the frequency of interruptions. On this view, an intervention can either add to workload or reduce it depending on how it fits into the existing pattern of work {{carayon2008}}. Rounding is an instructive case. It adds scheduled visits, but if those visits anticipate needs that would otherwise generate call-light activations, unplanned interruptions and the time-consuming aftermath of a fall, the net effect on workload may be neutral or even favorable. Early rounding studies reported a reduction in call-light use that is consistent with this argument {{meade2006;mitchell2014}}.

This reasoning shaped the project in two ways. First, rounding was shared between RNs and NAs, alternating hourly, so that the additional visits were distributed across the team and the NA role was strengthened rather than bypassed. Second, workload was treated as a balancing measure from the outset. The project measured call-light activations, the time a rounding visit took and staff perceptions of workload before and after implementation. The purpose was not to prove that rounding was cost-free, but to detect early whether it was creating a burden that would undermine either safety or sustainability.

## 2.7 Conceptual Frameworks

SQUIRE 2.0 asks improvement teams to report the formal and informal theory that underpins their work {{ogrinc2016}}. Two frameworks served this purpose: the Model for Improvement, which guided the method of testing and learning, and Kotter's model of leading change, which guided the human and organizational side of implementation.

### 2.7.1 The Model for Improvement

The Model for Improvement is a widely used framework for planning and testing changes in health care and other settings {{langley2009}}. It has two parts. The first consists of three questions that frame any improvement effort: what the team is trying to accomplish, how it will know that a change is an improvement, and what changes it can make that will result in improvement. The second is the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle, a structured method for testing a change on a small scale, studying the results against a prediction and deciding whether to adopt, adapt or abandon the change before testing it further {{langley2009}}.

In this project, the three questions mapped directly onto the project's structure. The first was answered by the SMART aim stated in Chapter 1. The second was answered by the family of outcome, process and balancing measures defined in Chapter 3, displayed over time on run charts, which allow a team to distinguish signals of change from random variation using simple probability-based rules {{perla2011;provost2011}}. The third was answered by the rounding protocol, informed by the evidence reviewed above. The PDSA cycles provided the means of adapting the protocol to 5 West: a pilot on one hallway, followed by unit-wide spread with modifications, followed by changes to documentation and team communication. Each cycle began with an explicit prediction and ended with a decision, a discipline intended to make learning visible rather than assumed.

The Model for Improvement suited this project for three reasons. It is designed for local, iterative work under real-world constraints rather than for controlled experiments. It privileges learning from frequent data over time, which suited a unit that generated monthly fall counts. And it gives front-line staff a structured way to shape a change, which addressed the risk, highlighted in the rounding literature, of a protocol being imposed and then quietly hollowed out {{harris2019}}. Its main limitation, discussed in Chapter 5, is that a sequence of PDSA cycles on a single unit cannot by itself exclude alternative explanations for any change observed.

### 2.7.2 Kotter's model of leading change

The Model for Improvement explains how to test a change; it says less about how to persuade busy people to adopt one. For this, the project drew on Kotter's eight-stage process for leading organizational change {{kotter1996}}. Kotter argues that change efforts commonly fail because leaders neglect early stages, such as establishing a sense of urgency and forming a guiding coalition, or declare victory before new practices are anchored in the culture {{kotter1996}}.

The eight stages were used as a planning checklist rather than a rigid sequence. Urgency was established by sharing the unit's baseline fall data, including the quarterly rates shown in Figure 1.1, at staff meetings and by discussing anonymized accounts of recent falls. A guiding coalition was formed comprising the nurse manager, two charge nurses, two NAs, a night-shift RN and myself. The vision, that no patient on 5 West should need to get up alone to meet a basic need, was developed with that group and communicated through huddles, a pocket card and a poster in the break room. Obstacles identified by staff during the PDSA cycles, such as the length of the original script and the paper log, were removed in response, which is the empowerment stage in practical form. Short-term wins, including the first months below the baseline median, were shared promptly. The later stages, consolidating gains and anchoring the change in the culture, correspond to the sustainability plans discussed in Chapter 6.

Kotter's model was developed for corporate organizations and has been criticized for its linear structure, which may not reflect the more iterative reality of change in clinical settings. Combining it with the Model for Improvement partly addressed that limitation: the PDSA cycles provided the iteration, while Kotter's stages ensured that the human and political work of change was not neglected. The national fall-prevention toolkit makes a similar pairing, treating the management of change as a distinct task alongside the selection of clinical practices {{ahrq2013}}.

## 2.8 Synthesis and Gaps

The evidence reviewed in this chapter supports five conclusions that together shaped the project. First, inpatient falls cause substantial harm and cost, and the burden attaches to all falls, not only injurious ones, which justifies the total fall rate as a primary outcome {{morello2015;bouldin2013}}. Second, risk assessment tools identify patients at elevated risk but with imperfect accuracy, and they prevent falls only when they trigger reliable actions {{aranda2013;hendrich2003}}. Third, multifactorial programs probably reduce falls in hospitals, but effects vary widely and depend heavily on implementation {{cameron2018;hempel2013;miakelye2013}}. Fourth, rounding has consistent but weak evidence of benefit, and its effectiveness appears to depend on fidelity to its purpose rather than on its mere presence {{mitchell2014;harris2019}}. Fifth, nurse workload is a patient-safety issue, and interventions must be designed and monitored so that they do not add a burden that undermines either care or sustainability {{aiken2002;carayon2008}}.

The gaps in the evidence are as instructive as its findings. Few rounding studies report fidelity data alongside outcomes, few describe how the protocol was adapted to local conditions, and few report balancing measures such as workload or effects on other nursing-sensitive harms. Many improvement reports also describe PDSA cycles only loosely. This project was designed to address those gaps at the local level: by reporting monthly process data alongside outcomes, by documenting each PDSA cycle with its prediction and learning, by including balancing measures and by reporting the whole according to SQUIRE 2.0 {{ogrinc2016}}. Chapter 3 describes how this was done.

# Chapter 6: Conclusion, Sustainability and Implications for Practice

This final chapter states the conclusions of the project, sets out how the unit intends to sustain the change after the formal project period, and considers what the work implies for nursing practice, nursing leadership and further improvement. It draws on the results in Chapter 4 and the interpretation and limitations in Chapter 5, and it is written with two readers in mind: the 5 West team, who will carry the work forward, and colleagues on similar units who may be weighing whether to try it.

## 6.1 Conclusions

The project set out to reduce falls on a 32-bed adult medical-surgical unit by introducing purposeful hourly rounding and refining it through Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles. Over nine months the unit's fall rate fell from 4.53 to 2.62 per 1,000 patient-days, a 42% relative reduction that met the SMART aim of no more than 3.17, and rounding documentation exceeded the 85% target from November 2024 onward. The run chart showed a shift of seven consecutive months below the baseline median, and the balancing measures gave no sign that the gains were bought at the expense of staff workload, patient experience or pressure-injury prevention.

Three conclusions follow, each stated with the caution that a single-unit, uncontrolled design requires. First, a structured rounding routine that targets toileting, positioning, pain and the reach of personal items is feasible on a busy medical-surgical unit, at a median cost of about two and a half minutes per visit. Second, the improvement on 5 West appears to have depended on reliability rather than on the routine's existence: the fall rate moved only once documentation passed about 80% and was lowest when rounding was embedded in the electronic record and reinforced by daily huddles. Third, linking the existing Morse Fall Scale score to a concrete action, proactive toileting for patients scoring 45 or above, converted a risk assessment the unit already performed into a change in care. None of these conclusions shows that rounding alone caused the reduction; together they describe a bundle that worked in this context and the conditions under which it worked.

## 6.2 Sustainability

Improvement projects often fade once the project lead steps back and the novelty wears off, and the toolkit published by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality treats sustainability as a phase to be planned rather than an afterthought {{ahrq2013}}. The 5 West plan was agreed with the nurse manager, Marcus Bell, in March 2025 and rests on four elements.

The first is ownership. Responsibility for rounding has moved from the project lead to the charge nurse role, which already leads the 10:00 and 22:00 huddles. Two RNs and one nursing assistant from each shift have been named as rounding champions, so that knowledge of the routine is not held by one person. The final stage of Kotter's eight-stage process is anchoring new approaches in the culture {{kotter1996}}, and I take from it that a change is not secure until it has become ordinary practice rather than a project. On 5 West that means rounding appearing in orientation for new staff and in the unit's shift handover structure, both of which were in place by the end of the project period.

The second is measurement at a lower intensity. The monthly audit will continue at 20 rather than 40 randomly selected hours, which preserves a signal while halving the audit burden, and falls per 1,000 patient-days will remain on the unit's quality board. The run chart will be converted to a Shewhart u-chart using the intervention period as a new baseline, so that the unit can distinguish ordinary variation around the new level from a genuine deterioration {{provost2011;benneyan2003}}. A pre-agreed trigger, two consecutive months of documentation below 80% or any point above the upper control limit for falls, will prompt a review by the nurse manager.

The third is the electronic flowsheet. Because rounding is now documented in the record rather than on a paper log, it is visible to anyone reviewing care and can be reported automatically. The unit has asked the hospital's informatics team whether the flowsheet can generate a monthly compliance report, which would reduce reliance on manual audit [source needed].

The fourth is a plan for high-acuity days, the weakness identified in PDSA 2. The draft approach, still to be tested, is that when the charge nurse judges acuity to be high, rounding priority shifts to patients with a Morse score of 45 or above and to all patients overnight, with the remaining patients rounded every two hours. Testing this as a fourth PDSA cycle would make the routine more resilient on the days when it is most likely to slip.

## 6.3 Implications for practice

For clinical nurses and nursing assistants, the main implication is that rounding is a clinical intervention, not a documentation task. The closing question used on 5 West, "Is there anything else I can do for you? I have time," was retained because it signals to patients that asking for help, particularly help to the bathroom, is welcome. Since the majority of falls on the unit were unassisted and related to toileting, anything that lowers a patient's reluctance to call for assistance is likely to matter as much as the schedule itself.

For units that already assess fall risk, the project suggests looking hard at what the score changes. Many units score every patient each shift; fewer can say what care a high score triggers. The 5 West experience is that a simple rule, here proactive toileting at a Morse score of 45 or above, gave the score practical meaning. Units should, however, check how well their chosen tool performs locally rather than assume its validity, as the evidence on fall-risk instruments advises {{aranda2013}}.

For units considering adoption, the results argue for adopting the bundle rather than a script. The rounding huddle, post-fall huddle and shared RN and nursing-assistant roles were part of what was tested, and the evidence reviewed in Chapter 2 suggests that rounding reduced to a checkbox delivers little {{harris2019}}. A pilot on a single hallway, as in PDSA 1, is a low-risk way to find a unit's own obstacles before they undermine a unit-wide launch.

## 6.4 Implications for nursing leadership

The project has implications for nurse managers and quality leaders beyond the unit. Fall-prevention programs succeed more often where leaders support them visibly and front-line staff help design them {{miakelye2013}}, and that was the experience here: the nurse manager protected time for huddles and for the audit, while the changes in each cycle came from staff feedback. Leaders should also treat rounding as a workload question. Rounding redistributes work rather than adding to it only when the work system is redesigned around it {{carayon2008}}, and adequate staffing remains a precondition for safe care {{aiken2002}}. A unit that is chronically short of staff is unlikely to sustain hourly rounding, and asking it to do so risks producing documentation without substance.

For quality departments, the project illustrates the value of reporting improvement work in a structured way. Following SQUIRE 2.0 made it necessary to describe the context, the theory behind the change and the study of the intervention, not only the outcome {{ogrinc2016}}, and those descriptions are what another unit would need to judge whether the approach fits its own circumstances.

## 6.5 Recommendations for further work

Three extensions would strengthen what is known. The first is spread with comparison: introducing the bundle on a second medical-surgical unit in a staggered way, while a third unit continues usual care, would supply the comparison that this project lacked. The second is patient-level process data, recording whether toileting was offered and accepted for high-risk patients, which would allow the toileting mechanism proposed in Chapter 5 to be tested directly. The third is a longer follow-up of falls with injury; the counts in this project were too small for conclusions, and injury is the outcome that matters most to patients and families.

## 6.6 Concluding statement

Purposeful rounding is neither a new idea nor a guaranteed one. On 5 West, it became effective when it was tested in small steps, linked to the unit's existing knowledge of who was at risk and why, made visible through huddles and the electronic record, and shared between registered nurses and nursing assistants. The fall rate fell, and stayed down for seven months. Whether it stays down now depends less on the evidence reported here than on whether the unit continues to treat rounding as care rather than as a task to be recorded.

# Chapter 5: Discussion

This chapter interprets the results reported in Chapter 4. It opens with a short summary of the main findings and then considers them against the evidence reviewed in Chapter 2, asking in turn what the outcome means, which mechanisms most plausibly explain it, how fidelity to the intervention shaped what happened, and what the balancing measures say about cost to staff and patients. The second half of the chapter sets out the limitations of the project with the specificity that a single-unit, uncontrolled design demands. In keeping with the SQUIRE 2.0 guidance, the discussion gives weight to context and to the study of the intervention itself rather than to the outcome alone {{ogrinc2016}}.

## 5.1 Summary of key findings

The SMART aim of this project was to reduce the 5 West fall rate by 30%, from 4.53 to no more than 3.17 falls per 1,000 patient-days, within nine months of introducing purposeful hourly rounding, while keeping rounding documented in at least 85% of audited hours. Both parts of the aim were met. Across the nine intervention months (July 2024 to March 2025) the unit recorded 20 falls over 7,634 patient-days, a rate of 2.62 per 1,000 patient-days and a 42% relative reduction from the baseline year, in which 46 falls occurred over 10,159 patient-days. Falls with injury fell from nine in the baseline year (0.89 per 1,000 patient-days) to three in the intervention period (0.39 per 1,000 patient-days), as shown in Table 4.1.

The run chart in Figure 4.1 shows seven consecutive monthly points below the baseline median of 4.71 from September 2024 to March 2025. Under the run-chart rules this project adopted, that pattern is a shift, a signal of non-random change, and it was not preceded by any trend in the baseline year, where the number of runs fell within expected limits {{perla2011;provost2011}}. The largest and most consistent gains coincided with PDSA 3, when the electronic flowsheet, the twice-daily rounding huddle and the post-fall huddle were added; across those six months the unit recorded 10 falls in 5,099 patient-days, or approximately 1.96 per 1,000 patient-days.

The process measure rose from 58% of audited hours documented in July 2024 to 90% in March 2025 (Figure 4.2), with a mean of 87.3% over October 2024 to March 2025. The balancing measures in Table 4.2 moved in a favorable or neutral direction: call-light activations fell from 6.8 to 5.1 per patient-day, the median rounding visit took 2.5 minutes, nurses' agreement that rounding added to their workload fell from a mean of 3.6 to 3.1, their confidence in knowing which patients were at high risk rose from 3.4 to 4.3, the share of patients giving a top-box response for prompt staff response rose from 71% to 79%, and pressure injuries did not increase.

## 5.2 Interpretation

### 5.2.1 The outcome in light of the evidence on rounding

The direction of change on 5 West is consistent with the more favorable strand of the rounding literature. The early multisite study by {{meade2006|n}} concluded that rounds incorporating specific actions, whether hourly or every two hours, could reduce call-light use and falls and improve satisfaction with nursing care, and the systematic review by {{mitchell2014|n}} graded the evidence that hourly rounding reduces falls and call-light use as moderate in strength. {{hicks2015|n}} likewise summarized several studies reporting lower fall rates after rounding was introduced, and the best-practice implementation project described by {{daniels2016|n}} reported a halving of falls on a medical-surgical unit not unlike 5 West. Read alone, these sources would make a 42% reduction look unremarkable.

As Chapter 2 argued, however, the evidence is neither uniform nor strong. The realist evaluation of intentional rounding in English hospitals found that, as commonly implemented and adapted, its effectiveness was very weak, and it traced much of the shortfall to low fidelity to the original protocol {{harris2019}}. Systematic reviews of multicomponent hospital fall programs are similarly cautious: {{hempel2013|n}} reported a pooled postintervention rate ratio whose confidence interval included no effect, and the Cochrane review judged that multifactorial interventions in hospitals may reduce the rate of falls, with the benefit possibly concentrated in subacute settings {{cameron2018}}. The more optimistic estimate that well-implemented multicomponent programs can reduce relative risk by as much as 30% {{miakelye2013}} sits comfortably with this project's aim but should not be taken as a benchmark that the 5 West result has exceeded in any generalizable sense.

The fairest reading is that 5 West achieved an improvement at the upper end of what the literature suggests is possible, under conditions that the literature identifies as favorable. Those conditions, discussed below, matter more for other units considering the change than the size of the reduction itself. It also bears noting that the 42% figure is derived from 66 falls in total; with counts of one to five a month, a difference of two or three falls in either period would have moved the headline percentage considerably. The shift on the run chart is a more robust indicator of change than the percentage, because it depends on the pattern of points rather than on their exact values.

### 5.2.2 Plausible mechanisms: toileting and the night

Two features of the fall data help explain why rounding may have worked on this unit. First, 61% of all 66 falls were unassisted and related to toileting, and 74% of the patients who fell had a Morse Fall Scale score of 45 or above. The Morse scale was developed to identify the fall-prone patient {{morse1989}}, and its author's account of fall-prevention programs stresses that a risk score is useful only if it changes what staff do for the patient {{morse2009}}. Before the project, the unit scored risk on admission and each shift but did not link that score to a predictable care routine. PDSA 2 made that link explicit by offering toileting proactively to every patient with a Morse score of 45 or above, and PDSA 3 named those patients aloud at each rounding huddle. The "potty" element of the 4 Ps addresses directly the scenario that generated most falls: an older patient who needs the bathroom, does not want to wait or to bother the nurse, and gets up alone.

Second, roughly 70% of the 46 baseline falls occurred between 22:00 and 06:00, compared with 45% (9 of 20) during the intervention period. Overnight rounding every two hours was a deliberate compromise, adopted in PDSA 2, between surveillance and sleep. The reduction in the proportion of night-time falls suggests that the compromise did not leave the night unprotected, and the PDSA 2 observation that compliance was better at night than during the day may be part of the reason. Night shifts on 5 West carry fewer admissions, procedures and family visits, so a predictable two-hourly cycle may simply be easier to keep. The point is not that nights became safe but that the distribution of risk moved, which is what one would expect if scheduled toileting and alarm checks were intercepting the unassisted night-time trip to the bathroom.

These mechanisms are offered as the most plausible explanation, not a demonstrated one. The project did not collect patient-level data on toileting offered and accepted, and the fall-characteristics data describe the people who fell, not the much larger group who did not. A stronger test would compare toileting-related falls among high-risk patients before and after, with denominators of high-risk patient-days, which the current data system could not supply.

A further caveat concerns the risk tool. The meta-analysis by {{aranda2013|n}} found that the Morse scale had lower diagnostic validity than STRATIFY and that its performance declined on reassessment, and it advised testing instruments locally before relying on them. The Hendrich II model offers another validated alternative built from a large case-control study {{hendrich2003}}. In this project the Morse threshold functioned less as a precise predictor than as a practical prompt that focused attention on a manageable subset of patients. That role may tolerate imperfect discrimination, but it would be unwise to read the 74% figure as evidence that the scale is well calibrated on 5 West.

### 5.2.3 Fidelity: the process measure and the PDSA sequence

The process data support the view that fidelity was central to the result. The fall rate did not move out of its baseline pattern in July 2024 (4.70), when documentation stood at 58% and only one hallway on one shift was rounding. The run of low points began in September 2024, as documentation reached 80%, and the lowest rates occurred in PDSA 3, when documentation stayed between 84% and 90%. This sequence is consistent with a dose-response relationship between the reliability of rounding and the fall rate, although with nine process points and no patient-level linkage it cannot establish one.

The contrast with the English evaluation is instructive. {{harris2019|n}} found that rounding had often become a documentation exercise, completed on charts without the purposeful interaction the protocol intended. Two design choices on 5 West were aimed at that risk. The closing question, "Is there anything else I can do for you? I have time," was retained through every cycle because staff reported that it changed the tone of the visit, and the rounding huddles kept the purpose of rounding visible by tying it to named patients and recent near-misses. Whether these choices protected the substance of rounding cannot be shown from documentation audits alone, a limitation addressed in Section 5.3.5, but the falling call-light rate and the improved patient-experience item are at least consistent with patients experiencing more proactive contact rather than more paperwork.

The iterative structure of the project also deserves comment. The review by {{taylor2014|n}} found that few published PDSA projects documented a full sequence of cycles or used frequent quantitative data to guide them. This project used monthly data to decide what to change between cycles, and each cycle answered a problem that the previous one exposed (Table 3.1). The Model for Improvement frames this as learning by testing on a small scale before spreading {{langley2009}}, and the pilot on an eight-bed hallway in PDSA 1 did exactly what a pilot should: it found that the script was too long, that nights were not covered and that nursing assistants were unsure when to call the RN, all before those weaknesses could discredit the change unit-wide. Pilot-testing, front-line engagement and leadership support are among the themes that {{miakelye2013|n}} associated with successful fall-prevention programs, and each was present here.

### 5.2.4 Balancing measures, workload and safety

A persistent objection to hourly rounding is that it adds a fixed task to an already stretched workload. The concern is serious: nurse staffing and workload are linked to patient outcomes and to burnout {{aiken2002}}, and a change that consumes time without returning it could harm the very patients it is meant to protect. The 5 West data offer a partial answer. A median visit of 2.5 minutes (IQR 1.5 to 4.0) is not trivial when multiplied across a 32-bed unit, but the fall in call-light activations suggests that some of that time was recovered from reactive work, a pattern consistent with {{meade2006|n}}. Nurses' agreement that rounding added to their workload fell modestly between the two surveys.

The human factors perspective set out by {{carayon2008|n}} offers a way to understand this. Workload depends not only on staffing and acuity but on how work is designed. Rounding redesigned part of the work system: it converted unpredictable interruptions into scheduled visits, shared those visits between RNs and nursing assistants, and moved documentation from a paper log at the door into a flowsheet already used for other care. The dip in compliance on high-acuity days observed in PDSA 2 is a reminder that redesign does not remove the underlying constraint. When the unit is short of staff or caring for several unstable patients, rounding is likely to be the first task that slips, and the project did not test a contingency plan for those days.

The survey results require care. The pre and post samples (41 and 38 RNs) were not paired, the item scale is short, and staff who had invested in the project may have answered more favorably. The improvement in nurses' confidence about which patients were at high risk (3.4 to 4.3) is the most interpretable change, because it maps directly onto the rounding huddle, which named those patients twice a day. The stability of pressure injuries (two in the baseline year, one in the intervention period) indicates that repositioning was not displaced by the new focus on falls, although counts this small cannot detect anything but a large adverse effect.

### 5.2.5 Injuries and the meaning of the outcome

The reduction in injurious falls from nine to three is welcome, given the harms and costs associated with falls with injury {{tjc2015;oliver2010}}. It should not, however, be presented as a separate finding. Three events cannot support a claim that rounding reduces injury, and the trial by {{dykes2010|n}} found an effect on falls without a significant effect on fall-related injuries. A more defensible position is that of {{morello2015|n}}, who argued that programs should aim to prevent all falls rather than only injurious ones, because even non-injurious falls extend length of stay and consume resources. On that view, the overall fall rate was the right primary outcome for this project, and injuries are best monitored as a secondary indicator over a longer period.

## 5.3 Limitations

### 5.3.1 Single unit and no control unit

The project was conducted on one 32-bed unit in one community teaching hospital, with a patient population whose mean age was 67, and it had no concurrent control unit. Any change on 5 West between the baseline and intervention periods could therefore have arisen from factors other than rounding, and the result cannot be generalized to units with different patients, staffing or leadership. The run chart guards against mistaking random variation for change, but it cannot attribute a non-random change to a particular cause. A comparison with the fall rates of other medical-surgical units in the hospital over the same months would have strengthened the inference considerably, and the absence of that comparison is the single most important limitation of this report.

### 5.3.2 Secular trend and co-interventions

Fall rates can drift for reasons unrelated to any local project: changes in admission patterns, in staffing, in hospital-wide safety campaigns or in reporting culture. Three features of the data make a secular trend a less likely explanation for the 5 West result, though they do not exclude it. The baseline year showed no trend, so the improvement was not the continuation of a decline already under way. Monthly patient-days remained stable throughout (828 to 861), suggesting no large change in throughput. And the winter months, which were among the highest in the baseline year (December 2023 at 4.80 and January 2024 at 5.81), were among the lowest in the intervention period (December 2024 at 1.17 and January 2025 at 2.36), which argues against a seasonal artifact.

Co-intervention is a subtler problem within the project itself. The rounding bundle was not a single change: by PDSA 3 it combined the 4 Ps script, proactive toileting for high-risk patients, alarm checks, the rounding huddle, the post-fall huddle and a new documentation format. The design cannot separate their contributions, and it is possible that the huddles, by raising general vigilance, accounted for much of the effect. This is a familiar feature of multicomponent fall programs {{hempel2013}} and is not a flaw in itself, but it means that other units should adopt the bundle as a whole, or test components separately, rather than assume that rounding alone produced the result.

### 5.3.3 Hawthorne effect

Staff knew that rounding was being audited and that fall data were being displayed and discussed, and the project lead was a clinical nurse on the unit. Some of the improvement may therefore reflect heightened attention to falls rather than the rounding routine as such. Two observations temper this concern. The effect persisted for seven months, longer than a novelty effect would usually be expected to last, and attention was deliberately built into the intervention through the huddles, so that vigilance is part of the mechanism rather than merely a bias. Whether the gains outlast the end of the formal project is the real test, and it is taken up in Chapter 6.

### 5.3.4 Regression to the mean

The baseline year contained two conspicuously high months, September 2023 at 6.04 and January 2024 at 5.81 falls per 1,000 patient-days, and concern about such months was part of what brought the problem to the unit's attention. When a project is launched because recent results look poor, later results will tend to look better even without intervention, simply because extreme values are usually followed by values closer to the average. The project reduced this risk by using the full twelve-month baseline rather than the worst months as its comparator and by judging change against the median rather than against a peak. More importantly, regression to the mean would predict a return toward the baseline centre of about 4.5 to 4.7, not a sustained run of values between 1.17 and 3.58. Regression may account for some month-to-month movement but is an implausible explanation for the shift as a whole.

### 5.3.5 Measurement

Several measurement limitations apply. Falls were counted from the hospital incident-reporting system, so the outcome depends on reporting completeness. The post-fall huddle, introduced in PDSA 3, may have increased the reporting of falls that would previously have gone unrecorded, which would bias the results against improvement; the opposite, less likely, possibility is that staff invested in the project under-reported. The process measure audited documentation, not the quality of the rounding visit, so a documented round is evidence that a round was recorded, not that pain was addressed or toileting offered. Forty randomly selected hours a month is a reasonable audit burden but yields an estimate with appreciable sampling error, so the difference between 84% and 90% should not be over-read. The time study covered only two weeks in October 2024, and the patient-experience item came from an internal survey rather than a validated instrument. Finally, the run-chart analysis was appropriate for detecting change {{perla2011}}, but a Shewhart u-chart would have allowed the unit to judge whether the new level of performance was stable and to set control limits for ongoing monitoring {{benneyan2003;provost2011}}.

## 5.4 Chapter summary

The 5 West project met its aim, and the pattern of the data, a non-random shift that grew stronger as documentation became more reliable and coincided with fewer toileting-related and night-time falls, supports a cautious inference that purposeful rounding, embedded in a broader bundle and refined through three PDSA cycles, contributed to the improvement. The evidence base for rounding is mixed, and this report adds a well-described local case rather than settling the question. The limitations, chiefly the absence of a control unit and the inseparability of the bundle's components, mean that the result is best understood as a promising and plausible improvement on one unit. Chapter 6 draws the project's conclusions and turns to the practical questions that follow: how to sustain the gains on 5 West, what other units might take from the work and what further study it warrants.

# Chapter 4: Results

This chapter reports what happened when purposeful hourly rounding was introduced on 5 West, using the measures and analytic methods set out in Chapter 3. It begins with the course of the intervention, describing how the three PDSA cycles unfolded and what was adapted along the way. It then presents the primary outcome, the fall rate, first as a run chart and then by phase, before turning to injurious falls, the characteristics of the falls that occurred, the process measure and the balancing measures. Interpretation is kept to a minimum here; Chapter 5 considers what the results mean and how far they can be attributed to the intervention.

## 4.1 Course of the intervention

The project ran as planned from July 2024 to March 2025, and all three PDSA cycles were completed in the order and over the dates set out in Table 3.1. No cycle was abandoned, and no change was introduced outside the PDSA structure. Hospital fall prevention policy, the Morse Fall Scale assessment schedule, the use of alarms and non-slip socks, and the unit's staffing model were unchanged throughout. Monthly patient-days during the intervention ranged from 836 to 860, closely matching the baseline range of 828 to 861, so the unit's workload as measured by occupancy was comparable across the two periods.

During PDSA 1, the pilot on the east hallway confirmed that hourly rounding could be delivered on day shift, but only after staff informally trimmed the script. Debrief notes from the second and third weeks recorded the same observation from several staff members: the script read well on paper but took too long to recite in a room where the patient wanted to talk, and the environmental checks were being skipped first. The team also noted that the pilot left the hours of highest baseline risk untouched, because it did not run at night.

PDSA 2 brought the largest change in scope. The shortened pocket-card script was adopted quickly, and the NA escalation triggers on its reverse addressed the uncertainty that NAs had reported in the pilot. The PDSA log recorded two patterns that shaped the next cycle. First, champions reported that rounding was the task most often deferred on days with several patients requiring one-to-one observation, rapid response calls or a cluster of admissions. Second, night staff described the two-hourly schedule as easier to keep than the day-shift hourly schedule, and the night champion reported fewer missed rounds overnight than during the day.

PDSA 3 introduced the EHR flowsheet, the twice-daily rounding huddles and the post-fall huddle. The parallel run of paper and electronic documentation in the first week of October produced no disagreement that the champions judged material, and the paper log was withdrawn at the end of that week. The 10:00 and 22:00 huddles became part of the charge nurse's routine within the first month. Post-fall huddle records were completed for all ten falls that occurred in PDSA 3, and these are drawn on in Section 4.4.

Two adaptations were made within PDSA 3 without changing its design. Charge nurses began to read the names of high-risk patients from a list generated by the flowsheet rather than from memory, after one early huddle omitted a patient whose Morse score had risen during the shift. And the 22:00 huddle was moved to the start of the night handover on two evenings a week when the charge nurse was also covering admissions, so that it was not lost to competing work. Both were recorded in the PDSA log as small tests that were adopted. Staff comments in the log during this cycle shifted in tone, from concerns about the time rounding took to remarks about specific patients whose needs had been met before they called.

The baseline series varied between 3.48 and 6.04 falls per 1,000 patient-days, with the monthly rate alternating above and below the median in an irregular pattern. The highest baseline value, 6.04 in September 2023, followed a month of 3.48 and preceded a month of 4.68. It stood out on visual inspection but was not judged an astronomical point, since January 2024 (5.81) was of similar magnitude and both lay within the spread that the baseline showed as a whole. The run chart rules were applied first to the baseline alone. No point fell exactly on the median, so all twelve were useful observations. The baseline contained nine runs, which lies within the expected range for twelve useful observations {{perla2011}}. The longest sequence of consecutive increases or decreases was three points, short of the five required for a trend, and the longest run on one side of the median was two points, short of the six required for a shift. The baseline was therefore consistent with a stable process showing only common-cause variation, which made it an appropriate reference for judging later change.

In the intervention period, every monthly value lay below the baseline median. July 2024, the pilot month, recorded 4.70, almost exactly on the center line, as would be expected of a month in which rounding reached only eight beds on a single shift. August and September 2024 recorded 3.58 and 3.55, values within the range seen in the lower half of the baseline. From October 2024 onward the rate fell to between 1.17 and 2.39, lower than any baseline month.

Applying the analysis plan in Section 3.7.2, which counted any shift attributable to the intervention from September 2024, there were seven consecutive points below the baseline median from September 2024 to March 2025. This exceeds the six-point threshold and meets the shift rule, a signal of non-random change in the process. Had the two implementation months been included, as a less conservative reading would allow, the run below the median would extend to nine points; the conclusion is the same either way. No trend rule was met in the intervention period: the rate fell sharply between September and October 2024 and then fluctuated between about 1.2 and 2.4 rather than declining steadily. The pattern on the chart is therefore one of a step change coinciding with the start of PDSA 3, rather than of gradual improvement.

Across the full baseline year, 46 falls occurred over 10,159 patient-days, a rate of 4.53 per 1,000 patient-days. The rate declined across successive cycles: 4.70 in the pilot month, 3.56 in PDSA 2 and 1.96 in PDSA 3. The ten falls in the six months of PDSA 3 compare with 46 in the twelve months of baseline, equivalent on a monthly basis to fewer than half as many.

### 4.2.3 Attainment of the SMART aim

The SMART aim set two criteria, judged as described in Section 3.7.3. The first was a fall rate of 3.17 or less within nine months of starting rounding. The intervention-period rate of 2.62 met this criterion, exceeding the targeted 30% reduction. Every month from October 2024 onward was also below 3.17 individually, and all were below the hospital's internal target of 3.0. The months of PDSA 1 and PDSA 2 were not; the aim was achieved for the period as a whole because of the sustained low rates in PDSA 3.

The second criterion, rounding documented in at least 85% of audited hours, was met from November 2024 onward and is reported in Section 4.5. Taken together, the aim was met on both criteria by the end of the project, with the qualification that the process criterion was not reached during the first four months, when the intervention was still being built.

## 4.3 Injurious falls

Nine falls in the baseline year resulted in injury, a rate of 0.89 per 1,000 patient-days. Three injurious falls occurred during the intervention period, in July 2024, August 2024 and November 2024, a rate of 0.39 per 1,000 patient-days (Table 4.1). Only one injurious fall occurred during the six months of PDSA 3. The proportion of falls resulting in injury was 20% in the baseline (9 of 46) and 15% in the intervention period (3 of 20). Because injurious falls were few in both periods, with no more than one in any month, the monthly series was too sparse for meaningful run chart analysis, and these figures are reported descriptively. The direction of change was the same as for all falls, but the numbers are too small to separate a real reduction in injury from chance.

## 4.4 Characteristics of falls

All 66 falls across the two periods were classified by the three characteristics defined in Section 3.6.1. Of the 66, 40 (61%) occurred while the patient was trying to reach or use the toilet without assistance, and 49 (74%) occurred in patients whose most recent Morse Fall Scale score was 45 or above. These two characteristics confirm the picture that informed the theory of change in Section 3.3.2: falls on 5 West were concentrated among patients already identified as high risk and around the unmet need for toileting.

The time of day at which falls occurred changed between periods. In the baseline year, 32 of the 46 falls (70%) occurred between 22:00 and 06:00. In the intervention period, 9 of the 20 falls (45%) occurred during those hours. The absolute number of overnight falls therefore fell from 32 in twelve months to 9 in nine months, while daytime falls fell from 14 to 11. Overnight hours, which had accounted for the majority of baseline falls, accounted for fewer than half of falls once two-hourly overnight rounding and proactive toileting were in place.

The post-fall huddle records for the ten falls in PDSA 3 added detail that the incident reports did not capture. The huddles most often identified one of three circumstances: a patient who had declined a toileting offer at the previous round and later got up alone; a patient who had become newly confused since the last Morse assessment; and a fall in the interval between rounds shortly after a change of shift. In several huddles staff noted that the patient's call light and possessions were within reach, suggesting that the elements of the script that depended on the environment were being completed more reliably than the elements that depended on the patient's agreement.

*Note.* Forty randomly selected hours were audited each month; the unit of analysis is the patient-hour. July 2024 audits covered the pilot hallway on day shift only. Documentation was on paper from July to September 2024 and in the EHR flowsheet from October 2024.

Documentation rose in each of the first four months, from 58% in the pilot month to 71% in August, 80% in September and 84% in October. It reached the 85% target in November 2024 (86%) and remained above it for the rest of the project, at 88% in December, 87% in January, 89% in February and 90% in March. The mean across PDSA 3 was 87.3%. The largest single increase, thirteen percentage points, occurred between July and August, when the shortened script and pocket card were introduced across the unit. PDSA 2's prediction that documentation would exceed 75% of audited hours was met in September but not in August.

The PDSA log suggests that the dip on high-acuity days observed during PDSA 2 was reduced but not eliminated in PDSA 3. Champions continued to note occasional missed rounds on days with multiple one-to-one patients, but these appeared in the audit as scattered undocumented hours rather than as whole shifts without rounding. Observations of rounding visits during PDSA 3 found that the 4 Ps were usually completed when a visit was documented, while the closing question was the element most often omitted on busy shifts. As noted in Section 3.5, these observations came from a convenience sample and are reported qualitatively.

Read alongside Figure 4.1, the timing of the two measures is closely aligned. The fall rate first dropped below 3.0 in October 2024, the month in which documentation reached 84% and the EHR flowsheet and huddles began. The months in which documentation was lowest (July and August) were also the months in which the fall rate was highest.

### 4.6.1 Responsiveness and patient experience

Call-light activations fell from a mean of 6.8 per patient-day in the baseline year to 5.1 in March 2025. This is the direction predicted by the theory of change and reported in earlier work on rounding {{meade2006}}: if patients' needs are anticipated, fewer patients need to call. The comparison rests on a single post-intervention month, however, and was not tracked monthly. Patients' rating of prompt staff response, measured by the top-box percentage on the internal survey, rose from 71% in the baseline year to 79% in the first quarter of 2025. Neither measure suggests that rounding came at the expense of responsiveness; both moved in the favorable direction.

### 4.6.2 Staff time and perceptions of workload

In the October 2024 time study, a rounding visit took a median of 2.5 minutes, with an interquartile range of 1.5 to 4.0 minutes. Short visits typically involved a patient who was comfortable and needed nothing; the longest visits involved toileting assistance or repositioning, which staff pointed out they would otherwise have provided later in response to a call light. The time study did not measure the time saved by fewer call-light responses, so the net effect of rounding on staff time cannot be estimated from these data; what can be said is that the cost of an individual visit was modest and that most visits were brief.

Forty-one of the 48 RNs (85%) completed the survey in June 2024, and 38 (79%) in March 2025. Mean agreement with the statement "Rounding adds to my workload" fell from 3.6 to 3.1 on the five-point scale, meaning that RNs agreed less strongly after nine months of structured rounding than they had before it began, when rounding was informal. Mean agreement with "I know which patients are at high risk of falling" rose from 3.4 to 4.3, the largest change on either item. Because responses could not be linked and the respondents differed in part between administrations, these differences describe the two groups of respondents and should not be read as change within individuals.

### 4.6.3 Other care

Two hospital-acquired pressure injuries were recorded in the baseline year and one in the intervention period. The count is small in both periods and the difference is not meaningful, but it gives no indication that the shortened script displaced attention to skin. No other unintended consequences were identified in team meetings, huddle records or staff comments. Night staff raised the concern that rounding might disturb patients' sleep during PDSA 2; no formal measure of sleep was collected, and this remains a gap discussed in Chapter 5.

## 4.7 Summary of results

The results can be stated briefly. The baseline fall rate on 5 West was stable at a median of 4.71 falls per 1,000 patient-days. After purposeful hourly rounding was introduced, the rate fell to 2.62 over the nine-month intervention period, a 42% relative reduction that met the SMART aim, and to 1.96 during PDSA 3. The run chart showed seven consecutive points below the baseline median from September 2024 to March 2025, meeting the shift rule. Injurious falls fell from 0.89 to 0.39 per 1,000 patient-days, though the numbers were too small for firm conclusions. The share of falls occurring overnight fell from 70% to 45%. Documented rounding rose from 58% to 90% and met the 85% target from November 2024, with a mean of 87.3% across PDSA 3. The balancing measures moved in favorable directions: call-light use fell, patients' rating of prompt response rose, RNs reported less workload from rounding and greater awareness of high-risk patients, and pressure injuries did not increase. Chapter 5 interprets these results against the evidence reviewed in Chapter 2 and considers the alternative explanations that a single-unit, uncontrolled design leaves open.

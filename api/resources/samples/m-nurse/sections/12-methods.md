# Chapter 3: Methods

This chapter describes how the project was designed, carried out and studied. It begins with the reporting framework and improvement model, then sets out the context of 5 West in enough detail for a reader to judge how far the findings might travel. The intervention, purposeful hourly rounding, is described next, with the theory of change that linked it to the aim stated in Chapter 1. The chapter then explains the three Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles, the study of the intervention, the family of measures and their operational definitions, and the analysis, which relied on run charts. It closes with the ethical considerations that applied to a quality improvement project rather than a research study.

## 3.1 Approach and reporting framework

The project was a single-site quality improvement initiative with a time-series design: a twelve-month baseline followed by a nine-month intervention period. It was not a controlled study and did not seek generalizable knowledge in the way a trial would. Its purpose was local: to reduce falls on one unit and to learn, cycle by cycle, which version of rounding could be sustained there.

The report follows the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence, version 2.0 {{ogrinc2016}}. SQUIRE 2.0 was chosen because it asks authors to make explicit three things easily lost in reports of local improvement: the formal and informal theory behind the change, the context in which the work took place, and the study of the intervention as distinct from the study of its outcomes {{ogrinc2016}}. Each has its own section below, and Appendix C maps every SQUIRE item to the relevant section of this report.

The work was organized around the Model for Improvement {{langley2009}}, which poses three questions (what we are trying to accomplish, how we will know that a change is an improvement, and what changes will result in improvement) and pairs them with iterative PDSA cycles for testing changes on a small scale before spreading them. Here the first question was answered by the SMART aim in Chapter 1, the second by the family of measures in Section 3.6, and the third by the rounding intervention and its refinements in Sections 3.3 and 3.4. An iterative design was preferred to a fixed protocol because Chapter 2 suggested that rounding succeeds or fails largely on how well it is adapted to a unit's routines.

## 3.2 Context

### 3.2.1 The unit and its patients

5 West is a 32-bed adult medical-surgical unit at Kestrel Bay General Hospital, a 410-bed community teaching hospital. It admits general medical patients and a smaller number of post-operative surgical patients. The population is predominantly older; the mean age of patients admitted during the baseline year was 67. Many patients were receiving diuretics, analgesics or sedating medicines, had intravenous lines or urinary catheters, or were disoriented in unfamiliar surroundings, all of which the evidence reviewed in Chapter 2 associates with risk of falling.

The unit has two hallways meeting at a central nurses' station. The east hallway contains eight single rooms and is the part of the unit farthest from the station, which made it the natural choice for the pilot. Monthly patient-days during the project ranged from 828 to 861, so occupancy was high and stable throughout, a point that matters for the interpretation of rates in Chapter 4.

### 3.2.2 Staffing and existing fall prevention practice

The unit employed 48 registered nurses (RNs) and 22 nursing assistants (NAs), with a charge nurse on every shift. The nurse manager, Marcus Bell, MSN, RN, served as site mentor. I was a clinical nurse on the unit throughout, which gave me detailed knowledge of its routines but also created an insider position whose implications are considered in Section 3.8 and Chapter 5.

Existing practice already contained several elements of the multifactorial programs reviewed in Chapter 2. Every patient was assessed with the Morse Fall Scale on admission and on every shift {{morse1989;morse2009}}. Patients scoring 45 or above were classed as high risk and received a bed or chair alarm, yellow non-slip socks and a door sign. These practices were set by hospital policy and remained unchanged during the project, so they formed the background against which the intervention was tested rather than part of it.

What the unit lacked was a reliable, proactive way of attending to the needs that most often precede a fall. Rounding was informal: staff looked in when passing, when medicines were due or when a call light sounded, and visits varied by nurse, shift and workload. Nothing ensured that a high-risk patient who needed the toilet at three in the morning would be asked before getting up alone. This was the gap the project set out to close.

### 3.2.3 Improvement team

The improvement team comprised myself as project lead; the nurse manager; two day-shift RNs and one night-shift RN who volunteered as rounding champions; one NA from each of the day and night rotations; and the unit's clinical nurse educator. The faculty advisor, Dr. Patricia Lindgren, DNP, met with me every two weeks and reviewed each PDSA plan before it began, and the hospital's patient safety officer provided access to incident data.

Front-line engagement and leadership support are among the themes associated with successful implementation of fall prevention programs {{miakelye2013}}, and the federal toolkit on preventing falls in hospitals gives whole sections to readiness and managing change {{ahrq2013}}. In the spirit of a guiding coalition {{kotter1996}}, the team therefore included NAs, who carry out much of the bedside work, and night-shift staff, whose routines differ most from the day's.

## 3.3 The intervention

### 3.3.1 Purposeful hourly rounding

The intervention was purposeful hourly rounding: a scheduled, proactive visit to every patient, carried out with a fixed script and documented at the time. Rounding took place hourly between 06:00 and 22:00 and every two hours between 22:00 and 06:00. RNs and NAs alternated, so that an RN saw each patient at least every two hours during the day and the NA covered the visits in between.

Each visit followed the "4 Ps" script. The staff member asked about and addressed pain; helped the patient change position if needed; offered toileting (potty); and placed possessions, including the call light, telephone, water and glasses, within reach. The visit also included a check that any bed or chair alarm was on and working and a brief scan of the environment for hazards. It ended with the same closing question: "Is there anything else I can do for you? I have time." The closing words were deliberate. They signaled that patients need not wait until a need became urgent before asking for help, and so aimed to reduce the number who got up alone rather than "bother" a busy nurse. Rounding was documented on a paper log at each patient's door during PDSA 1 and PDSA 2, and in an electronic health record (EHR) flowsheet from PDSA 3. The script and log fields are reproduced in Appendix A.

The intervention drew on rounding protocols in which specific actions were built into rounds at one- or two-hourly intervals {{meade2006}}, and on a best-practice implementation project that used a structured rounding protocol on a medical-surgical unit {{daniels2016}}. It differed from those models in two locally decided respects: the RN–NA alternation, which reflected 5 West's staffing mix, and the reduced overnight frequency, introduced in PDSA 2 to protect sleep.

### 3.3.2 Theory of change

SQUIRE 2.0 asks authors to state why an intervention was expected to work {{ogrinc2016}}. The project's informal theory, built from the evidence in Chapter 2 and the review of baseline falls, can be stated as a chain of four links. First, many falls on the unit occurred when a patient tried to meet a basic need, most often toileting, alone. Second, if staff anticipated those needs on a predictable schedule, fewer patients would get up unassisted. Third, a predictable schedule would lower patients' need to use the call light, freeing staff time and making responses to the calls that did occur quicker. Fourth, a short scripted visit would help staff notice changes in risk, such as a disabled alarm or new confusion, before they led to harm.

The theory implied four primary drivers: reliable anticipation of toileting needs, especially overnight and for patients with a Morse score of 45 or above; consistent placement of the call light and personal items within reach; working alarms for high-risk patients; and shared awareness among staff of which patients were at highest risk. Each PDSA cycle targeted one or more of these drivers, and the measures in Section 3.6 were chosen so that each link in the chain could be observed, at least indirectly.

The theory also carried a counter-hypothesis that the team took seriously from the start. A large realist evaluation found that fidelity to the original rounding protocol was generally low and judged the effectiveness of rounding as commonly implemented to be very weak {{harris2019}}. If rounding on 5 West became a documentation exercise, the theory predicted that documentation might rise with no change in falls. Distinguishing documented rounding from purposeful rounding was therefore central to the study of the intervention (Section 3.5).

## 3.4 Plan-Do-Study-Act design

Changes were tested in three sequential PDSA cycles. A systematic review of the PDSA method in healthcare found that few published reports fully documented a sequence of iterative cycles, and that fewer still used quantitative data collected at least monthly to decide how one cycle should lead to the next {{taylor2014}}. The design responded to both weaknesses. Each cycle had a written plan with an explicit prediction, agreed with the faculty advisor in advance; process data were reviewed at least monthly, and weekly during PDSA 1; and the decision at the end of each cycle (adopt, adapt or abandon) was recorded with its reasons. Table 3.1 summarizes the cycles.

**Table 3.1** PDSA cycles: dates, change tested, prediction, what was learned

| Cycle | Dates | Change tested | Prediction | What was learned | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PDSA 1 | July 2024 (weeks 1–4) | Hourly rounding with the full 4 Ps script on the 8-bed east hallway, day shift only; paper log at the door | Staff would complete and document rounds in most audited hours and find the visits acceptable | The script was too long; nights were not covered; NAs were unsure when to call the RN | Adapt and spread |
| PDSA 2 | August–September 2024 | Whole unit, all shifts; shortened script on a laminated pocket card; proactive toileting for patients with Morse ≥45; two-hourly rounding overnight | Documentation would exceed 75% of audited hours; overnight falls would begin to decline | Compliance dipped on high-acuity days; nights performed better than days | Adapt |
| PDSA 3 | October 2024–March 2025 | EHR flowsheet replaced the paper log; charge nurse rounding huddle at 10:00 and 22:00; post-fall huddle within one hour of any fall | Documentation would reach and hold at ≥85%; the fall rate would move below the baseline median and stay there | Reported in Chapter 4 | Adopt |

*Note.* Morse = Morse Fall Scale score; 45 or above was the hospital's high-risk threshold.

### 3.4.1 PDSA 1: small-scale test

The first cycle tested whether rounding could be delivered at all within the existing workload. Testing on a small scale followed the Model for Improvement {{langley2009}} and the finding that pilot-testing is a feature of successfully implemented fall prevention programs {{miakelye2013}}. The cycle was too small to affect the unit fall rate; its value lay in what staff said at weekly debriefs. Staff shortened the over-long script themselves, the absence of night coverage was conspicuous given where baseline falls clustered, and NAs asked repeatedly which findings they should escalate.

### 3.4.2 PDSA 2: spread to the whole unit

The second cycle spread rounding to all 32 beds and all shifts. The pocket card carried escalation triggers for NAs on the reverse. Overnight rounding was set at two-hourly intervals, consistent with evidence that rounds at one- or two-hour intervals have both been associated with benefit {{meade2006}}. Every RN and NA completed a twenty-minute in-service session in the first week of August. Documentation fell on days with several one-to-one patients, rapid response calls or many admissions, suggesting that rounding was among the first tasks dropped under pressure. Unexpectedly, rounding was more consistent at night, which the night champion attributed to the quieter environment and the predictability of the two-hourly schedule.

### 3.4.3 PDSA 3: embedding in routine work

The third cycle aimed to make rounding part of the unit's systems rather than an addition to them. The EHR flowsheet row, built with the nursing informatics team, recorded the time, the staff member and each script element. Post-fall huddles asked what had happened, why, and what could change for this patient and others. The rounding huddles were intended to protect rounding on high-acuity days by making high-risk patients visible to the whole shift; the flowsheet was intended to remove the duplicated effort of writing on a paper log and then charting in the EHR. Both changes reflected the view that nursing workload depends on the design of the work system as well as on staffing and patient acuity {{carayon2008}}: the aim was to make rounding cost less effort, not to ask staff to try harder.

## 3.5 Study of the intervention

SQUIRE 2.0 distinguishes the study of the intervention, which asks whether and how the change was carried out, from the measurement of its outcomes {{ogrinc2016}}. The distinction matters for rounding because it can be documented without being purposeful, and done without being documented. Four sources were used. The first was the monthly documentation audit (Section 3.6.2). The second was direct observation: in each month of PDSA 2 and PDSA 3, a champion observed a convenience sample of rounding visits and noted whether each script element was completed and the closing question asked. Because the sample was not random, these observations were used for coaching and to check that documentation corresponded to real visits, not to estimate fidelity as a single figure. The third was a two-week time study in October 2024, in which champions timed visits from entry to departure. The fourth was the PDSA log, which recorded team meetings, debriefs, post-fall huddles and staff comments throughout the project. Findings from these sources are reported in Chapter 4 alongside the outcome measures.

## 3.6 Measures

A family of measures was defined before the intervention began, pairing outcome measures with process and balancing measures so that the team could see both whether the change was working and whether it was causing harm elsewhere {{langley2009;provost2011}}. Table 3.2 lists the measures; full operational definitions are given in Appendix B.

**Table 3.2** Family of measures

| Measure | Type | Operational definition | Data source | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fall rate | Outcome (primary) | Falls on 5 West per 1,000 patient-days | Incident reporting system; midnight census | Monthly |
| Injurious fall rate | Outcome | Falls with any injury per 1,000 patient-days | Incident reporting system; midnight census | Monthly |
| Fall characteristics | Outcome (descriptive) | Proportion of falls related to unassisted toileting, occurring 22:00–06:00, and in patients with Morse ≥45 | Incident reports; post-fall huddle records | Each fall |
| Rounding documented | Process | Percentage of audited patient-hours with rounding documented | Paper log (Jul–Sep 2024); EHR flowsheet (Oct 2024–Mar 2025) | Monthly, 40 random hours |
| Call-light activations | Balancing | Activations per patient-day | Nurse call system | Baseline; March 2025 |
| Staff time per round | Balancing | Minutes per rounding visit | Time study | October 2024 |
| Staff perceptions | Balancing | Agreement with workload and risk-awareness items (1–5) | Anonymous RN survey | June 2024; March 2025 |
| Prompt response | Balancing | Percentage of top-box responses to "staff responded promptly" | Internal patient experience survey | Baseline year; January–March 2025 |

*Note.* EHR = electronic health record. Morse = Morse Fall Scale score.

### 3.6.1 Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the monthly fall rate. Following hospital policy, a fall was defined as an unplanned descent to the floor or another lower surface, with or without injury and whether or not witnessed; assisted falls, in which staff lowered a patient to the floor, were included. Falls were counted from the incident reporting system and checked against handover records to identify unreported events. Patient-days were the sum of the midnight census for each day of the month. The rate was calculated as shown in Equation (3.1).

$$
\text{rate} = \frac{\text{falls}}{\text{patient-days}} \times 1000 \tag{?}
$$

A rate adjusts for variation in occupancy and is expressed in the same units as the hospital's target. Phase-level rates were calculated by summing falls and patient-days across the months of a phase and applying Equation (3.1), rather than by averaging monthly rates.

The secondary outcome was the injurious fall rate: falls resulting in any injury, from minor (a bruise or skin tear) to major (a fracture), as recorded after medical review, per 1,000 patient-days. Each fall was also classified by three characteristics that the theory of change predicted would shift if rounding worked: whether the patient was trying to reach or use the toilet without assistance; whether the fall occurred between 22:00 and 06:00; and whether the patient's most recent Morse score was 45 or above.

### 3.6.2 Process measure

The process measure was the percentage of audited patient-hours in which rounding was documented. Each month, 40 rounding hours were selected with a random number generator, overnight hours being sampled according to the two-hourly schedule. For each selected hour, the record for every occupied bed in the rounding area was checked, so the unit of analysis was the patient-hour. A patient-hour counted as documented if an entry within the scheduled interval recorded at least the 4 Ps. During PDSA 1, audits were confined to the pilot hallway and day shift. The 85% target was set in advance as the level at which rounding could be regarded as normal practice while allowing for legitimate exceptions, such as a patient off the unit for a procedure.

### 3.6.3 Balancing measures

Balancing measures addressed three plausible unintended effects. The first was workload. Given the evidence linking nurse staffing to burnout and job dissatisfaction {{aiken2002}}, it was important not to add a task that staff experienced as a burden. A two-week time study measured visit duration, and an anonymous RN survey, administered in June 2024 and March 2025, asked staff to rate agreement on a five-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) with "Rounding adds to my workload" and "I know which patients are at high risk of falling," the second testing the driver of shared risk awareness.

The second was responsiveness. Call-light activations per patient-day were compared between the baseline year and March 2025; earlier work suggested that effective rounding should reduce rather than increase call-light use {{meade2006;mitchell2014}}. Patients' perception was measured by the top-box percentage on a prompt-response item in the hospital's internal patient experience survey. The third concern was displacement of other care. Hospital-acquired pressure injuries, which share preventive actions such as repositioning and toileting, were counted in each phase as a check that the shortened script had not weakened attention to skin.

### 3.6.4 Data quality

Fall counts were reconciled monthly between the incident system and handover records, and patient-days were taken directly from census reports. Because I was both project lead and a staff nurse, documentation audits were carried out by two champions in rotation, and I reviewed a sample of their work each month. The switch from paper to electronic documentation risked changing the process measure for reasons unrelated to practice, so the audit definition was kept identical across both media and the two methods were run in parallel for the first week of October 2024.

## 3.7 Analysis

### 3.7.1 Run charts

The primary analysis used run charts, which display a measure over time around a median center line and allow non-random patterns to be detected with simple rules rather than statistical modeling {{perla2011}}. Run charts were chosen over control charts for three reasons: twenty-one monthly points were adequate for run chart rules but modest for stable control limits; run charts make no assumption about the distribution of the data; and the method is accessible to the front-line staff who reviewed the charts at team meetings, which was itself part of the improvement strategy. Statistical process control offers more powerful tools when longer series are available {{benneyan2003;provost2011}}, and a control chart would become the more appropriate choice as the series lengthens.

The center line was the median of the twelve baseline monthly rates, 4.71 falls per 1,000 patient-days. It was frozen at the end of the baseline so that intervention points were judged against the baseline rather than against a median that included them.

### 3.7.2 Run chart rules

Four rules were applied, following {{perla2011|n}} and {{provost2011|n}}. Points falling exactly on the median were neither counted nor treated as breaking a run.

1. **Shift:** six or more consecutive points all above or all below the median.
2. **Trend:** five or more consecutive points all increasing or all decreasing.
3. **Runs:** too few or too many runs for the number of useful observations, judged against published tables of expected limits.
4. **Astronomical data point:** a point obviously different from the rest to anyone viewing the chart.

The rules were applied first to the baseline series, to confirm that it showed only random variation, since a baseline already in transition would make any later signal hard to interpret, and then to the full series.

Before the intervention began, the team agreed how the early months would be treated. July 2024 involved eight beds on one shift, and August 2024 was the month in which the whole unit was trained and the pocket card introduced. Both were annotated as implementation months, and the plan specified that any shift attributed to the intervention would be counted from September 2024, the first month of steady whole-unit operation. The choice is conservative: it can only shorten a run, and it avoids claiming credit for months in which the change had barely reached most patients.

### 3.7.3 Other analyses

The relative reduction in the fall rate was calculated as the difference between the baseline and intervention rates divided by the baseline rate. Attainment of the SMART aim was judged against two criteria set in advance: an intervention-period fall rate of 3.17 or less, and rounding documented in at least 85% of audited hours. Fall characteristics were summarized as counts and percentages by phase. Survey items were summarized as means; because responses could not be linked between administrations, differences are reported descriptively. Visit durations were summarized as a median and interquartile range because occasional long visits were expected to skew them. The PDSA log and huddle records were read for recurring issues that helped explain changes in the measures. No inferential tests were applied to the outcomes: run chart rules are themselves set so that a signal is unlikely to arise by chance in a stable process {{perla2011}}, and they were the agreed basis for judging improvement.

## 3.8 Ethical considerations

The project was submitted to the hospital's quality improvement review before it began. The review determined that it was quality improvement rather than human-subjects research, because it sought to improve care on one unit using an intervention consistent with existing standards, did not assign patients to different conditions and did not aim at generalizable knowledge. Institutional Review Board review was therefore not required, and the School of Nursing at Kestrel Bay University accepted this determination for the scholarly project.

All outcome and process data were de-identified, unit-level counts; the patient safety officer supplied fall counts and characteristics in aggregated form, and no patient identifiers were extracted. The staff survey was anonymous and voluntary, collected no demographic information that could identify individual nurses, and stated that non-participation carried no consequences. Time-study and observation records did not include staff names.

Two further issues received attention. Frequent visits can disturb rest and toileting offers can feel intrusive, so staff were taught not to wake patients who were asleep and comfortable, and patients could decline any element of a visit. My dual role also meant colleagues might feel obliged to take part because the request came from a peer. To limit this, the clinical nurse educator distributed and collected the survey, champions rather than I conducted the audits, and the nurse manager presented rounding as a unit practice change rather than a personal project.

> **Sample note.** The data reported in this thesis are synthetic, created to illustrate how such a study is reported. No participants were recruited.

## 3.9 Chapter summary

This chapter has described a single-unit quality improvement project, reported according to SQUIRE 2.0 and organized by the Model for Improvement. Purposeful hourly rounding, built around the 4 Ps and a closing offer of time, was tested and refined across three PDSA cycles that moved from an eight-bed pilot to a whole-unit practice embedded in the EHR and supported by twice-daily huddles. A family of outcome, process and balancing measures was defined in advance, and the primary outcome was analyzed with run charts against a frozen baseline median of 4.71 falls per 1,000 patient-days. Chapter 4 reports what these measures showed.

# Chapter 4: Methodology

## 4.1 Introduction

This chapter sets out how the study was designed and carried out, and why. Chapter 1 framed the problem: districts buy learning analytics dashboards for teachers, teachers often report that they intend to use them, and yet observed use stays shallow and uneven. Chapter 2 argued that the acceptance literature explains intention reasonably well but has little to say about the space between intention and use, and that the role of trust in dashboard data has been asserted more often than it has been measured. Chapter 3 then built a trust-extended acceptance model and stated six hypotheses. The task of this chapter is to show how the study was set up to test that model and, just as importantly, to explain what the model could not.

The three research questions drive every design decision reported below. RQ1 asks to what extent performance expectancy, effort expectancy, facilitating conditions and trust in dashboard data predict teachers' reported use of learning analytics dashboards. RQ2 asks how teachers explain the ways they use, adapt or avoid dashboards in their practice. RQ3 asks how the qualitative accounts explain the quantitative relationships, particularly the gap between intention and use. The first question calls for measurement and modeling; the second calls for teachers' own accounts in their own terms; the third calls for a principled way of bringing the two together. A single-method design could have answered one of these questions well but not all three.

The chapter proceeds as follows. Section 4.2 sets out the pragmatist stance that underpins the study. Section 4.3 describes the explanatory sequential design and the decisions about priority, timing and mixing that it required. Section 4.4 describes the research setting. Section 4.5 reports Phase 1, the survey, including sampling, instruments, log data, piloting, data screening and the analytic strategy. Section 4.6 explains the connecting point between the phases, and Section 4.7 reports Phase 2, the interviews. Section 4.8 sets out how the two strands were integrated, Section 4.9 addresses quality and inference, Section 4.10 addresses ethics and my own position, and Section 4.11 states the methodological limitations that the reader should carry into Chapters 5 to 7. Section 4.12 summarizes.

> **Sample note.** The data reported in this thesis are synthetic, created to illustrate how such a study is reported. No participants were recruited.

## 4.2 Philosophical Stance: Pragmatism

### 4.2.1 Why a paradigm statement matters here

Mixed methods studies are sometimes criticized for treating the combination of numbers and words as a technical convenience while leaving the underlying assumptions unexamined. That criticism has force in a study like this one, where the quantitative strand borrows heavily from a postpositivist tradition in information systems research, in which constructs such as performance expectancy are treated as stable latent properties of individuals, while the qualitative strand draws on an interpretive tradition in which teachers' meanings are constructed in particular schools, weeks and relationships. If I did not say how these traditions sit together, the reader would have no grounds for trusting the meta-inferences reported in Chapter 7.

### 4.2.2 Pragmatism as the organizing stance

I adopted pragmatism as the organizing stance of the study, following the argument by {{morgan2007|n}} that the paradigm debates of the late twentieth century can be set aside in favor of an approach that attends to the connections between research questions, methods and the consequences of knowledge. In that account, three moves distinguish a pragmatic approach from its alternatives. The first is abduction: rather than reasoning only deductively from theory to data or only inductively from data to theory, the researcher moves back and forth, using the results of one form of inquiry to frame the next. The second is intersubjectivity: rather than choosing between an objective world and a purely subjective one, the researcher works with the shared understandings that make communication about a phenomenon possible. The third is transferability: rather than claiming either universal generalization or radical context-specificity, the researcher asks how far what was learned in one setting can inform action in another.

Each of these moves maps onto a concrete feature of the design. Abduction describes the logic of the explanatory sequential design itself: the survey tested hypotheses derived from theory, and its results, including the results that did not fit, determined whom I interviewed and what I asked them. Intersubjectivity describes how I treated the survey constructs. I did not assume that "trust in dashboard data" was a natural kind that teachers carried around in their heads, but I did assume that it was a meaningful enough idea that teachers could respond to items about it and then talk about it, and that the two forms of response could be compared. Transferability describes the claims I make in Chapter 8: not that the coefficients estimated in the Northern Lakes region hold everywhere, but that the mechanisms identified are likely to matter wherever districts adopt dashboards with similar features and similar governance.

### 4.2.3 What pragmatism does not license

Pragmatism is sometimes read as permission to do whatever works. I did not read it that way. {{teddlie2009|n}} emphasize that mixed methods research still has to answer to standards of inference quality within each strand and across them, and that the integrative claims at the end of a study are only as good as the design quality and interpretive rigor that produced them. Accordingly, the survey strand in this study is held to the conventional standards of measurement and structural modeling, and the interview strand is held to the conventional standards of qualitative trustworthiness. The pragmatist stance governs how the strands relate to one another; it does not relax the standards within them.

## 4.3 Research Design

### 4.3.1 Choice of an explanatory sequential design

The study used an explanatory sequential design, in which a quantitative phase is followed by a qualitative phase designed to explain the quantitative results {{creswell2018}}. In the notation used by {{creswell2018|n}}, the design is QUAN → qual: the quantitative strand comes first in time and, in this case, carries the greater weight in the overall study, while the qualitative strand is built on its results.

I considered and rejected two alternatives. A convergent design, in which survey and interviews would have been conducted at the same time and compared afterwards, would have been faster. It would not, however, have allowed me to select interview participants on the basis of survey results, and the central puzzle the study was designed to address, why some teachers who intend to use dashboards do not, could only be investigated well if I could find those teachers first. An exploratory sequential design, beginning with interviews and building an instrument from them, would have been appropriate if no theory of technology acceptance existed. But the acceptance literature reviewed in Chapter 2 offers well-validated constructs {{davis1989;venkatesh2003}}, and the gap in that literature is not the absence of constructs but the absence of an explanation for why intention does not always become use. That gap is best addressed by testing the existing model, extending it with trust, and then examining where and why it falls short.

### 4.3.2 Priority, implementation and connecting decisions

{{ivankova2006|n}} identify a set of practical decisions that any explanatory sequential study must make: the priority given to each strand, the sequence of implementation, the point at which the strands connect, and the stage at which their results are integrated. I report each decision here so that the reader can judge it.

*Priority.* The quantitative strand was given priority in the sense that the research problem was framed by the acceptance model, the hypotheses were stated in advance, and the qualitative strand was designed to explain quantitative findings rather than to stand alone. This does not mean the interviews were treated as ornamental. As Chapter 6 shows, the interviews produced findings, notably about workarounds, that the survey could not have anticipated, and Chapter 7 treats these as integral to the study's contribution. Priority here describes the logic of the design, not the relative value of the two kinds of evidence.

*Implementation.* The survey was administered during one spring semester; the analysis of Phase 1 was completed over the following summer; and the interviews were conducted during the subsequent fall semester. The interval allowed the latent profile analysis to be completed before interview participants were selected, which was essential for the connecting point.

*Connecting.* The strands were connected at two points. First, the interview sample was drawn purposively from the profiles identified by latent profile analysis of the survey data (Section 4.6). Second, the interview protocol was built around specific quantitative results, including the moderation effect and the relatively weak role of facilitating conditions, so that the questions asked in Phase 2 were tied to the puzzles left by Phase 1.

*Integration.* Integration took place at the design, methods and interpretation levels {{fetters2013}}, and is described fully in Section 4.8.

## 4.4 Research Setting

### 4.4.1 The Northern Lakes region

The study was conducted in four public school districts in a US state that I refer to as the Northern Lakes region. The four districts are given the pseudonyms Harrow Lake, Pine Ridge, Westfield and Calder Valley. Between them they operate 31 secondary schools, comprising middle schools, high schools and a small number of combined grade 6 to 12 schools. The districts differ in size and setting: Westfield is suburban and the largest of the four; Harrow Lake and Pine Ridge each serve a small city and its surrounding area; and Calder Valley is predominantly rural, with several schools at considerable distances from the district office. This variation was useful for the study because it meant that the conditions under which teachers encountered the dashboard, including the amount of training offered and the expectations set by school leaders, were not uniform.

### 4.4.2 The district dashboard

All four districts used the same commercial learning analytics product, which I refer to throughout as "the district dashboard." I do not name the vendor, both to protect the confidentiality of the districts and because the study is not an evaluation of a product. The dashboard drew data from the districts' learning management system, their assessment platform and, with a delay, their student information system. For teachers, it offered a class overview showing assignment completion and recent assessment scores, a student-level view showing an individual's trajectory across assessments, an "at-risk" indicator that flagged students on the basis of a vendor algorithm combining attendance, missing work and assessment performance, and a set of export and reporting functions. Teachers could also view item-level results for common assessments.

In terms of the categories proposed by {{verbert2013|n}}, the dashboard was designed primarily to support awareness, and to a lesser extent reflection; it offered limited support for sensemaking and almost none for acting on what had been seen. This is consistent with the broader pattern reported in the systematic review by {{schwendimann2017|n}}, in which most dashboards present indicators for monitoring rather than for guiding instructional decisions. The point is relevant to the methodology because it shaped what "use" could reasonably mean. A teacher could log in and look; it was much harder to do anything within the dashboard beyond looking.

### 4.4.3 Why this setting

The setting was selected for three reasons. First, the four districts had adopted the same dashboard within a two-year window and had made it available to all secondary teachers, which meant that differences in use could not be attributed to differences in product. Second, the districts were willing to share platform log data for teachers who consented, which allowed the self-reported use measure to be checked against a behavioral indicator. Third, the districts had no prior relationship with me, which reduced, though it did not remove, the risk that teachers would see the study as an extension of district monitoring. I return to that risk in Section 4.10.

## 4.5 Phase 1: Quantitative Strand

### 4.5.1 Population and sampling

The target population was all classroom teachers of grades 6 to 12 employed in the 31 secondary schools of the four districts during the spring semester in which the survey was administered. District human resources offices provided lists of teachers with active dashboard accounts, from which I removed staff without a regular teaching assignment, such as instructional coaches and administrators. This produced a sampling frame of 1,180 teachers.

Because the frame was of manageable size and the study's aims included describing the distribution of acceptance profiles in the population, I invited every teacher in the frame rather than drawing a probability sample. This census approach avoided sampling error in the conventional sense but did not avoid nonresponse error, which is discussed below and in Section 4.11.

A priori power and sample size considerations for structural equation modeling are not reducible to a single rule. {{kline2016|n}} cautions against simple ratios of cases to parameters while noting that models with latent interactions and several latent variables generally require samples in the hundreds rather than the tens. {{hair2019|n}} similarly relate the required sample to model complexity, the number of indicators per construct and the communalities expected. Given six latent constructs with between three and six indicators each, and expected loadings in the range typical of established acceptance scales, I set a minimum target of 300 usable responses, with a preferred target of 400 to support the latent interaction and the latent profile analysis. The achieved sample of 412 met the preferred target.

### 4.5.2 Survey procedure

The survey was administered online over six weeks. Each district sent an initial notice from its office of teaching and learning, and I then sent a personalized invitation from a Kestrel Bay University address, followed by up to three reminders at roughly ten-day intervals. Teachers who completed the survey could enter a draw for one of twenty gift cards of modest value; the incentive was approved by the institutional review board and was not tied to the answers given.

The invitation emphasized three points: that the study was independent of the districts, that individual responses would never be shared with district or school staff, and that teachers who did not use the dashboard at all were as important to the study as those who used it heavily. The third point was included because pilot participants had suggested that infrequent users might assume the study was "not for them," which would have biased the sample toward engaged users.

Of the 1,180 teachers invited, 412 returned usable responses, a response rate of 34.9%. A response was classed as usable if the respondent had consented, was a classroom teacher in the frame, and had answered at least the acceptance and trust sections. Partial responses that ended before the acceptance section were excluded. The demographic profile of the sample is reported in Chapter 5; in summary, 61% of respondents identified as female, 38% as male and 1% as another identity, mean teaching experience was 12.4 years (SD 8.1), and the main subject areas were mathematics (22%), English (21%), science (19%), social studies (16%) and other subjects (22%).

### 4.5.3 Instruments

The survey contained six multi-item measures, a set of background questions and an optional final question asking whether the respondent was willing to be contacted about a follow-up interview. The full instrument appears in Appendix A. Table 4.1 summarizes the measures.

**Table 4.1** Survey measures, number of items, response format and sources

| Construct | Items | Response format | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Performance expectancy (PE) | 4 | 1–5 agreement | Adapted from UTAUT {{venkatesh2003}} |
| Effort expectancy (EE) | 4 | 1–5 agreement | Adapted from UTAUT {{venkatesh2003}} |
| Facilitating conditions (FC) | 4 | 1–5 agreement | Adapted from UTAUT {{venkatesh2003}} |
| Trust in dashboard data (TR) | 5 | 1–5 agreement | Developed for this study from {{mayer1995}} and {{lee2004}} |
| Behavioral intention (BI) | 3 | 1–5 agreement | Adapted from UTAUT {{venkatesh2003}} |
| Use (USE) | 6 | 0–5 frequency, averaged | Developed for this study from dashboard functions |

*Note.* Agreement items were scored 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. Use items asked how often in the past month the respondent had carried out each of six dashboard actions (0 = never to 5 = daily or almost daily). Reliability and validity evidence is reported in Chapter 5.

*Performance expectancy, effort expectancy and facilitating conditions.* These three constructs were adapted from the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology {{venkatesh2003}}, whose performance expectancy and effort expectancy constructs descend from the perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use constructs of the Technology Acceptance Model {{davis1989}}. Adaptation involved replacing references to "the system" with "the district dashboard," replacing references to job performance with references to teaching, and rewording one facilitating conditions item that referred to a specific help desk function not present in the districts. I deliberately kept the facilitating conditions items close to the original, which focus on resources, knowledge, compatibility with other systems and the availability of assistance. As Chapter 7 discusses, this decision had a consequence I did not fully anticipate: the facilitating conditions scale captured hardware, training and support, but not time. I report it here because it is a feature of the instrument, not a finding that emerged from nowhere.

*Social influence.* The UTAUT construct of social influence was not included. Chapter 3 explained the theoretical grounds: in districts where dashboard access is mandated and use is discussed in professional learning community meetings, social influence was expected to overlap substantially with organizational expectation, and the study's interest lay in trust rather than in normative pressure. The omission also kept the survey to a length that pilot participants judged tolerable. I recognize that the omission means some normative effects may be absorbed into other paths, and the interviews were designed partly to surface them.

*Trust in dashboard data.* No established scale for teachers' trust in learning analytics data was available that fitted the study's purposes, so I developed a five-item measure. The definition of trust followed {{mayer1995|n}}, who treat trust as a willingness to be vulnerable to another party on the basis of positive expectations about that party's conduct, and who distinguish the perceived ability, benevolence and integrity of the trustee. Applied to a data system, the "party" is the system together with the people and processes behind it. I drew also on {{lee2004|n}}, whose account of trust in automation emphasizes that trust should be calibrated to the actual reliability of a system, and that trust which is too low leads to disuse while trust which is too high leads to misuse. The five items therefore addressed the perceived accuracy of the data, its completeness, its currency, the reasonableness of the at-risk indicator, and the respondent's willingness to base instructional decisions on the data without checking it elsewhere. The last item operationalizes the "willingness to be vulnerable" element of the Mayer definition. The items were reviewed by two learning analytics researchers and two district data coordinators before piloting.

*Behavioral intention.* Three items asked about intention to use the dashboard in the coming month, plan to use it to inform instruction, and expectation of using it regularly during the rest of the school year.

*Use.* Self-reported use was measured by asking respondents how often in the past month they had carried out each of six actions: viewing the class overview, viewing an individual student's page, reviewing at-risk flags, viewing item-level results on a common assessment, using dashboard information to form student groups or plan reteaching, and discussing dashboard information with a colleague or in a meeting. Responses were scored from 0 (never) to 5 (daily or almost daily), and the six responses were averaged to give a score between 0 and 5. I chose a frequency format rather than an agreement format because use is a behavior, and asking teachers whether they "agreed" that they used the dashboard would have conflated behavior with attitude. The six actions were chosen to span a range from passive viewing to instructional action, following the distinction between awareness and action discussed in Chapter 2. They were identified from the dashboard's functions and checked with district coordinators to ensure that every action was available to every teacher.

*Background items.* Background questions asked about gender identity, years of teaching experience, main subject area, grade levels taught, district, and the amount of dashboard training received. School names were not collected, to reduce identifiability.

### 4.5.4 Platform log data

The final section of the survey asked teachers whether they consented to the research team receiving a count of their monthly logins to the district dashboard for the month before the survey. Of the 412 respondents, 288 consented. For these teachers, the districts' data coordinators provided login counts linked to a study identifier through a procedure that prevented the districts from learning which teachers had responded (Section 4.10.3). No information about which pages a teacher viewed, or for how long, was requested or received.

The log data served two purposes. The first was to provide a behavioral check on the self-report measure of use; Chapter 5 reports the correlation between the two. The second, which became more important than I had anticipated, was to allow the interviews to probe discrepancies between what teachers said they did and what the platform recorded. I treated logins as a partial indicator rather than a gold standard. A login records an entry to the system, not what the teacher did there, and, as Chapter 6 shows, it records nothing about the use teachers made of data after exporting it.

### 4.5.5 Pilot testing

The instrument was piloted in two stages. In the first stage, I conducted cognitive interviews with six secondary teachers from a district outside the study, who completed the survey while thinking aloud and then discussed items they found ambiguous. This led to three changes: a trust item that referred to "the algorithm" was reworded to refer to "the at-risk flag," because several teachers were unsure whether the dashboard used an algorithm at all; the time frame for the use items was fixed at "the past month" rather than "recently"; and an example was added to the item on item-level assessment results. In the second stage, the revised survey was completed by 38 teachers from the same outside district. The pilot data were used to check completion time (median about 14 minutes), item distributions and preliminary internal consistency. No item showed a floor or ceiling effect severe enough to justify removal. Pilot responses were not included in the main analysis.

### 4.5.6 Data screening and missing data

Data were screened for out-of-range values, straight-lining across the agreement items, implausibly short completion times, and univariate and multivariate outliers. Eleven responses with completion times under four minutes and identical responses across all agreement items were excluded before the usable sample of 412 was defined; these exclusions are included in the nonresponse figure rather than counted as responses.

Across the analytic variables, 3.1% of item responses were missing. Missingness was concentrated in the use items, where some respondents left actions blank rather than selecting "never," and in the trust item about the at-risk flag, which a small number of respondents appeared to skip because they had never opened that view. I examined patterns of missingness and found no evidence that missingness on any construct was strongly related to observed background variables. Missing data were handled by full information maximum likelihood (FIML) within the structural models, which uses all available information from each case under the assumption that data are missing at random {{kline2016}}. I did not use listwise deletion, which would have discarded information and could have biased estimates if missingness depended on observed variables.

Because several items, particularly the use items, were skewed, all latent variable models were estimated with robust maximum likelihood (MLR), which produces standard errors and a scaled chi-square statistic that are robust to non-normality {{kline2016}}. MLR is compatible with FIML, so the two decisions together allowed every usable case to contribute to estimation without assuming multivariate normality.

### 4.5.7 Analytic strategy

The quantitative analysis proceeded in five stages, each tied to a part of RQ1 or to the connecting point. The analyses were conducted in Mplus, with descriptive analyses and data management in R.

*Stage 1: Descriptive statistics and correlations.* I computed means, standard deviations, skewness and kurtosis for all items and composite scores, and the bivariate correlations among composites and between self-reported use and logged logins.

*Stage 2: Measurement model.* I estimated a confirmatory factor analysis in which each item loaded only on its intended construct and all latent constructs were allowed to correlate. Model fit was judged against the combination of criteria proposed by {{hu1999|n}}: a comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) close to .95 or above, a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) close to .06 or below, and a standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) close to .08 or below. I treated these as guidelines rather than strict thresholds, in line with the caution in {{kline2016|n}} that cutoff values were derived under specific simulation conditions and should not be applied mechanically. Convergent validity was assessed by standardized loadings (with .50 as a minimum and .70 as desirable) and average variance extracted (AVE) above .50 {{hair2019}}. Discriminant validity was assessed using the criterion of {{fornell1981|n}}, under which the square root of each construct's AVE should exceed its correlations with other constructs. Internal consistency was assessed with Cronbach's alpha, with .70 as the conventional minimum {{hair2019}}.

*Stage 3: Common method variance.* Because predictors and the main outcome were collected by self-report on the same instrument at the same time, common method variance was a plausible threat. Following {{podsakoff2003|n}}, I used both procedural and statistical remedies. Procedurally, the survey used different response formats for the predictors (agreement) and the use outcome (frequency), separated the predictor and outcome sections with the background items, assured respondents of anonymity, and avoided item wording that implied a desirable answer. Statistically, I conducted Harman's single-factor test as a coarse diagnostic and, more usefully, compared the self-report use measure with logged logins, which are not subject to the same method. I report the single-factor result in Chapter 5 with the caveat, which {{podsakoff2003|n}} make clearly, that the test is insensitive and cannot establish the absence of method bias.

*Stage 4: Structural model and moderation.* The hypothesized model specified paths from performance expectancy, effort expectancy and trust to behavioral intention (H1, H2, H3), and from behavioral intention and facilitating conditions to use (H4, H5). The moderation hypothesis (H6) stated that trust would moderate the path from intention to use, such that the path would be stronger when trust was high. Table 4.2 restates the hypotheses and the tests used.

**Table 4.2** Hypotheses and analytic tests

| Hypothesis | Path | Expected direction | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | PE → BI | Positive | Structural path coefficient |
| H2 | EE → BI | Positive | Structural path coefficient |
| H3 | TR → BI | Positive | Structural path coefficient |
| H4 | BI → USE | Positive | Structural path coefficient |
| H5 | FC → USE | Positive | Structural path coefficient |
| H6 | TR × BI → USE | Positive (stronger BI → USE at high trust) | Latent interaction (LMS); simple slopes at ±1 SD of trust |

*Note.* PE = performance expectancy; EE = effort expectancy; FC = facilitating conditions; TR = trust in dashboard data; BI = behavioral intention; USE = self-reported use. All models estimated with MLR and FIML.

The main-effects model was estimated first. The interaction was then tested with the latent moderated structural equations (LMS) approach, which models the product of two latent variables directly rather than forming products of observed composites, and so avoids attenuating the interaction through measurement error {{kline2016}}. Because LMS does not produce conventional global fit indices, I followed the common practice of assessing fit on the main-effects model and then comparing the main-effects and interaction models with a log-likelihood ratio test. To interpret the interaction, I estimated simple slopes of intention on use at one standard deviation above and below the mean of trust. Explained variance (R²) was reported for intention and use.

Two further specification decisions deserve mention. First, I did not include a direct path from trust to use in the hypothesized model, because Chapter 3 argued that trust would act on use chiefly by strengthening or weakening the translation of intention into action; I estimated it as a sensitivity check and report the result in Chapter 5. Second, I included years of teaching experience and district as covariates of use in a sensitivity model, to check that the hypothesized effects were not artifacts of differences between districts. District-level clustering was too coarse (four units) to model as a random effect, so district was entered as a set of fixed indicators.

*Stage 5: Latent profile analysis.* To identify subgroups of teachers with distinct configurations of acceptance, trust and use, and to provide the basis for interview sampling, I conducted a latent profile analysis on the factor scores for performance expectancy, behavioral intention, trust and use. Effort expectancy and facilitating conditions were excluded from the profile indicators because they were not central to the intention–use puzzle and including them produced profiles that were harder to interpret without improving fit; they were instead compared across profiles after the profiles were defined. I estimated solutions with two to five profiles and selected among them using the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better fit, entropy as an index of classification precision, the size of the smallest profile (with a minimum of 5% of the sample), and interpretability. The analysis was exploratory in the sense that I did not specify the profiles in advance. I did, however, expect that a profile combining relatively high intention with relatively low use would emerge if the intention–use gap was concentrated in an identifiable group rather than spread evenly across the sample. Chapter 5 reports the selected three-profile solution.

## 4.6 The Connecting Point

### 4.6.1 From profiles to participants

The connecting point is where an explanatory sequential design either earns its name or does not. {{ivankova2006|n}} note that the choice of which quantitative results to follow up, and of which participants to select, is the decision that most directly determines whether the qualitative phase can explain the quantitative one. In this study, the connection was made through the latent profile analysis. The three-profile solution described in Chapter 5 identified "Engaged users" (27% of the sample), who reported relatively high performance expectancy, intention, trust and use; a "Willing but stalled" group (41%), who reported high intention but low use and low trust; and "Sceptics" (32%), who reported low performance expectancy and low intention. The labels are mine and were assigned after inspecting profile means; they are shorthand for statistical configurations, not judgments of the teachers concerned.

I selected interview participants from all three profiles, for two reasons. The first was explanatory: RQ3 asks about the gap between intention and use, and the "Willing but stalled" teachers embody that gap, so they were the group whose accounts were most needed. The second was comparative: without Engaged users and Sceptics, I would have had no way to tell whether what Stalled teachers said about time, trust or guidance was distinctive to them or common to teachers across the board. I therefore sampled all three profiles but deliberately weighted the sample toward the Stalled group.

### 4.6.2 Selection procedure

Of the 412 survey respondents, those who had indicated willingness to be contacted for an interview were assigned to their most likely profile using the posterior probabilities from the latent profile analysis. To reduce the risk of interviewing teachers whose profile membership was ambiguous, I considered for selection only teachers whose posterior probability of membership in their assigned profile was at least .80. Within each profile, I then used maximum variation sampling on three characteristics: subject area, district and years of teaching experience. Where log data were available, I also included within the Stalled group several teachers whose logged logins were noticeably higher or lower than their self-reported use would suggest, because these discrepancies were one of the puzzles that Phase 1 handed to Phase 2.

Invitations were sent in waves. Thirty-four teachers were invited in total and 24 were interviewed: 7 Engaged users, 11 Willing but stalled, and 6 Sceptics. The others either did not reply, declined, or could not be scheduled before the end of the fall semester. Table 4.3 shows the composition of the interview sample by profile and subject area. Participant-level details, with pseudonyms, are given in Chapter 6.

### 4.6.3 Sample adequacy

Sample size in qualitative research is properly judged by the purpose of the analysis and the richness of the data rather than by a formula. Nonetheless, the question of how many interviews are enough has an empirical literature. In an analysis of their own interview data, {{guest2006|n}} found that most of the codes they eventually developed had appeared within the first twelve interviews, in a sample that was relatively homogeneous with respect to the topic. The Stalled subgroup of eleven teachers approaches that number and is broadly homogeneous in the sense relevant here, namely in sharing a statistical profile; the Engaged and Sceptic subgroups are smaller and were intended to serve as contrasts rather than to support independent thematic analyses. I monitored the development of codes during analysis and noted that the last four Stalled interviews added detail and illustration to existing codes but no new codes at the level of the eventual themes. I treat this as evidence of adequacy for the purposes of the study, not as proof of saturation in any absolute sense, and I return to the smaller subgroups as a limitation in Section 4.11.

## 4.7 Phase 2: Qualitative Strand

### 4.7.1 Interview protocol development

The interview protocol (Appendix B) was semi-structured. It had a fixed sequence of topics and core questions, followed by probes that I used selectively. The protocol was built from three sources. The first was the theoretical framework in Chapter 3, which pointed to the constructs that teachers might explain: expectations about usefulness and effort, the conditions that support use, and trust. The second, and most important, was the set of Phase 1 results that called for explanation. Three of these shaped the protocol directly: the moderation effect, which suggested that trust changed whether intention became use; the large Stalled profile, which suggested that the intention–use gap was concentrated in an identifiable group; and the moderate correlation between self-reported use and logged logins, which suggested that the two measures were capturing overlapping but distinct things. The third source was the data literacy framework of {{mandinach2016|n}}, which distinguishes between being able to read data and being able to transform information into instructional action. This framework informed a set of questions about what teachers did after looking at the dashboard.

The protocol opened with questions about the teacher's week and the place of the dashboard in it, moved to a "last time" question asking the teacher to describe in detail the most recent occasion on which they had used the dashboard, and then explored trust, time, action and the purposes the dashboard served. Toward the end of the interview, I presented a short, plain-language summary of one survey finding and asked the participant to comment on it. For example, Stalled teachers were told that many teachers who said they intended to use the dashboard reported using it rarely, and were asked what they thought explained that. This reflection on aggregate findings was a deliberate integration device: it allowed teachers to interpret the quantitative results directly, rather than leaving all interpretation to me. I did not tell participants which profile they had been assigned to, because doing so might have shaped their accounts.

The protocol was piloted with two teachers from the outside district used for survey piloting. The pilot led me to move the questions about surveillance and administrators later in the interview, because raising them early made one pilot participant noticeably guarded for the rest of the conversation.

### 4.7.2 Data collection

Interviews were conducted by video call during the fall semester following the survey, at times chosen by participants, most often after school. They lasted between 45 and 70 minutes. With participants' consent, interviews were audio-recorded; video was not recorded. Participants were invited, but not required, to share their screen and show me the dashboard views they used, and fifteen did so for at least part of the interview. Where they did, I took descriptive notes on what was shown rather than capturing images, to avoid recording student information.

Recordings were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service bound by a confidentiality agreement. I then checked each transcript against the recording, corrected errors, and removed or replaced identifying details, including student names, school names and colleagues' names. Participants were offered the opportunity to review their transcript; nine did so, and four made minor clarifications. I wrote a short reflective memo after each interview recording initial impressions, surprises and connections to the survey results.

### 4.7.3 Thematic analysis

Interview data were analyzed using thematic analysis as described by {{braun2006|n}}, who set out six phases: familiarization with the data, generation of initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report. I chose thematic analysis over alternatives such as grounded theory or interpretative phenomenological analysis because the study did not seek to generate a new theory from the ground up, nor to describe the lived experience of a small number of individuals in depth. It sought to identify patterned meaning across a set of accounts in relation to questions that had partly been set by a prior phase. Thematic analysis is flexible enough to be used with this kind of theoretically informed but open orientation.

The analysis combined deductive and inductive elements, and the balance needs stating precisely. I began with a small set of sensitizing categories drawn from the Phase 1 constructs (expectations, effort, conditions, trust, intention and use), but these were used to organize the initial reading rather than as a fixed coding frame. Most codes were generated inductively, at a semantic level in the first instance, and then refined toward a latent level as patterns became clearer. Coding was done in NVivo.

In practice the phases overlapped. During familiarization I read each transcript twice and listened again to the recordings of the first eight interviews. Initial coding produced 143 codes across the 24 transcripts. In searching for themes, I grouped codes into candidate themes using thematic maps, and at this stage I kept the three profiles separate so that I could see whether candidate themes were shared across profiles or concentrated in one. Reviewing themes involved two checks: whether the coded extracts within each candidate theme cohered, and whether the candidate themes together gave a faithful account of the data set as a whole. Several candidate themes were merged at this stage. For example, early candidates about "data lag" and "gradebook mismatch" were combined into a single theme about trust as timeliness and fit, because teachers consistently talked about the two together. One candidate theme about training was folded into the theme about moving from seeing to acting, because teachers rarely described training as a barrier in itself but frequently described it as failing to show them what to do with what they saw. Defining and naming themes produced the five themes reported in Chapter 6.

### 4.7.4 Trustworthiness

I addressed the trustworthiness of the qualitative strand in four ways. First, an audit trail of codebook versions, thematic maps and memos was kept throughout. Second, a doctoral colleague with experience in qualitative research independently coded four transcripts, drawn from all three profiles, at the midpoint of analysis; we met to discuss differences, which led to the clarification of several code definitions rather than to the calculation of an agreement coefficient, which I judged inappropriate for a reflexive approach. Third, I presented the candidate themes to my committee and, separately, to a group of three teachers from the outside pilot district, whose responses helped me check that the themes were recognizable to practitioners. Fourth, in reporting I sought to present negative cases, including teachers whose accounts ran against the dominant pattern of a theme, rather than only confirmatory extracts. Reflexivity is addressed in Section 4.10.4, where I describe my own position in relation to the study.

## 4.8 Integration Procedures

### 4.8.1 Integration at three levels

{{fetters2013|n}} distinguish integration at the level of design, at the level of methods and at the level of interpretation and reporting. I describe each in turn, because a common weakness of mixed methods dissertations is to claim integration in general terms while leaving it unclear where, concretely, the strands met.

At the design level, integration was built into the explanatory sequential structure itself. The qualitative phase existed in order to explain the quantitative phase, and its timing, sampling and focus were set accordingly.

At the methods level, integration occurred through connecting and building. Connecting took place when interview participants were sampled from the latent profiles (Section 4.6). Building took place when the interview protocol was constructed around Phase 1 results (Section 4.7.1), including the use of aggregate survey findings as prompts. The log data provided a further point of contact, because they allowed me to bring a specific discrepancy, between what teachers reported and what the platform recorded, into the interviews as a topic of inquiry.

At the interpretation level, integration was carried out through a joint display and through the technique of following a thread, both described below, and resulted in a set of meta-inferences reported in Chapter 7.

### 4.8.2 Following a thread

{{ocathain2010|n}} describe three techniques for integrating data in mixed methods studies: a triangulation protocol, following a thread, and a mixed methods matrix. I used the second as the main analytic technique at the interpretation stage. Following a thread involves selecting a question or finding from one strand and tracing it through the other strand, then back, so that the analysis of each strand is informed by the other. I identified four threads from Phase 1: (1) the moderating role of trust; (2) the Stalled profile and its combination of high intention and low use; (3) the effect of facilitating conditions on use, which was clear but left unexplained why teachers with adequate training and access still did not act on their intentions; and (4) the gap between self-reported use and logged logins. For each thread, I returned to the coded interview data, collated all relevant extracts across profiles, wrote an analytic memo, and then returned to the survey data to check whether patterns suggested by the interviews were visible in the quantitative results. For example, the interview finding that some teachers used the dashboard mainly by exporting data led me to re-examine whether teachers with high self-reported use but few logins differed on any survey item; this re-examination is reported in Chapter 7.

### 4.8.3 Joint display and meta-inferences

A joint display is a table or figure that brings quantitative and qualitative results together so that their relationship can be examined directly {{fetters2013}}. The joint display for this study (Table 7.1) is organized by research question and thread. For each row it shows the relevant quantitative result, the corresponding qualitative theme or themes with illustrative evidence, and the meta-inference drawn from the two.

Each meta-inference was classified according to the fit between the strands, using the categories set out by {{fetters2013|n}}. Confirmation means the two strands lead to the same conclusion. Expansion means they diverge in scope, with one strand adding to the other, for example by explaining what a construct consists of. Discordance means the strands appear to contradict each other. I committed in advance to reporting discordance rather than smoothing it over, because in an explanatory design apparent contradictions are often where the most useful explanations are found. Where discordance arose, I considered several explanations in turn: that one strand was wrong, that the strands measured different things, or that the measures in one strand were incomplete. Chapter 7 shows that the last two explanations account for most of the discordance observed.

## 4.9 Quality of Inferences

Quality in a mixed methods study has to be judged within each strand and across them. Within the quantitative strand, the relevant standards are measurement reliability and validity, model fit and the appropriateness of estimation methods, all of which were addressed in Section 4.5. Within the qualitative strand, the relevant standards are those of trustworthiness and reflexivity, addressed in Sections 4.7.4 and 4.10.4. Across the strands, {{teddlie2009|n}} propose that the quality of mixed methods inferences depends both on design quality, meaning whether the procedures were suitable and carried out well, and on interpretive rigor, meaning whether the conclusions follow from the findings, are consistent with one another and are defensible against plausible alternative explanations.

Three features of the design were intended to strengthen inference quality in that sense. The first was that the connecting point used an explicit, replicable procedure (posterior probabilities, a minimum probability for selection, and stated maximum variation criteria), so that the link between the strands was not left to convenience. The second was the use of the log data as a partially independent check on self-report, which allowed claims about use to be triangulated rather than resting on a single method. The third was the commitment to classify meta-inferences and to report discordance. Taken together, these choices are meant to ensure that the integrated conclusions in Chapter 7 are more than a juxtaposition of separate findings.

## 4.10 Ethical Considerations

### 4.10.1 Approval and consent

The study was approved by the Kestrel Bay University Institutional Review Board (protocol KBU-IRB-2024-117) and by the research offices of the four districts. Survey participants gave informed consent on the first screen of the survey; consent to the release of log data was sought separately at the end of the survey, so that teachers could take part in the survey without agreeing to it. Interview participants gave separate written consent, which covered audio recording, the use of anonymized quotations and the option of reviewing their transcript. All participants were told that they could withdraw at any point without consequence, and that withdrawal from the interview would not affect the use of their survey responses unless they asked for those to be removed as well.

### 4.10.2 Power, surveillance and the risk of the study itself

Learning analytics raises ethical questions about surveillance, consent and the uses to which data about people are put. {{slade2013|n}} discuss these questions primarily in relation to students, but similar concerns arise for teachers when data about their practice are visible to others, and the literature on data-driven leadership makes clear that how leaders frame data use shapes whether teachers experience it as support or as scrutiny {{datnow2014}}. This study was, in a sense, a study of a monitoring technology that itself relied on a form of monitoring, namely the platform logs. I took seriously the possibility that teachers would experience the study as another way of being watched.

Several measures followed from this. The districts did not receive any individual-level data from the study, only an aggregated summary after the dissertation was completed. Log data were limited to login counts for a single month and were requested only with explicit consent. Interview participants were not told their profile assignment, and profile labels were never used in communication with participants. Reports to districts were written so that no school-level or department-level result could be inferred, because some departments were small enough for individuals to be identified.

The study also touched indirectly on student data. Teachers sometimes referred to individual students during interviews or wished to show student-level dashboard views. I asked participants not to share student names, did not record video, and removed any student identifiers from transcripts.

### 4.10.3 Data linkage and security

Linking survey responses to log data required care. Each respondent who consented to log data release was assigned a random study identifier. I sent each district data coordinator a list of dashboard account identifiers for the consenting teachers in that district, without any survey data, together with the corresponding study identifiers. The coordinator returned login counts labeled only with study identifiers. Because coordinators received a list of consenting teachers, they could in principle learn that those teachers had taken part; this was explained to participants in the consent text, and coordinators signed agreements not to disclose or use this information. Coordinators did not receive the names of teachers who took part in the survey without consenting to log release, nor any survey responses.

All data were stored on encrypted university servers with access restricted to me and my committee chair. The key linking study identifiers to account identifiers was stored separately and destroyed once linkage was complete. Audio recordings will be destroyed three years after the completion of the study, in line with the approved protocol.

### 4.10.4 Researcher position

Before beginning doctoral study, I taught secondary science for eight years in a district outside the Northern Lakes region that used a different learning analytics product. I was, in the terms of the profiles reported in Chapter 5, probably a "Willing but stalled" user myself: I believed the dashboard could help, intended to use it, and rarely did. This history gave me useful insider knowledge, for example of how professional learning community meetings work and of the pressures of the school week, and it probably made it easier for teachers to talk to me frankly. It also carried risks. At the outset I was inclined to attribute low use to institutional failures rather than to teachers' own judgments, and I was alert to the danger of hearing my own story in other teachers' accounts. I managed these risks by writing reflective memos after each interview, by deliberately seeking out and reporting the accounts of Sceptics who had considered the dashboard and rejected it on reasoned grounds, and by asking my committee and peer coder to challenge the interpretations that seemed to me most obviously right. I mentioned my teaching background to participants when introducing myself, but did not describe my own experience of dashboards unless asked.

## 4.11 Methodological Limitations

The design has limitations that bear on how the findings in later chapters should be read. I state them here so that they are visible before the results, and I return to their consequences in Chapter 8.

First, the response rate of 34.9% leaves room for nonresponse bias. Teachers with strong views about the dashboard, whether positive or negative, may have been more likely to respond than those who were indifferent. I could compare respondents with the frame only on district and broad subject area, which limits the strength of any claim about representativeness.

Second, the survey was cross-sectional. The structural model specifies directional paths, but the data cannot establish temporal order between intention and use, and reverse influences, for instance from use to trust, are plausible. The interviews partly address this by asking teachers to narrate how their trust and use changed over time, but they cannot substitute for longitudinal data.

Third, use was measured primarily by self-report, with log data available for 288 of the 412 respondents. Logins are a narrow behavioral indicator, and both measures, as Chapter 6 makes clear, miss forms of use that take place outside the platform. This limitation became one of the study's findings, but it remains a limitation of the quantitative estimates.

Fourth, the facilitating conditions scale, adapted closely from UTAUT {{venkatesh2003}}, did not measure time. The interviews show that time was among the most important conditions for use. The quantitative estimate of the effect of facilitating conditions should therefore be read as an estimate of the effect of the conditions the scale measured, not of conditions in general.

Fifth, the trust measure was developed for this study. Although it was grounded in established theory {{mayer1995;lee2004}}, piloted, and showed good psychometric properties in this sample, it has not been validated in other samples.

Sixth, the interview subgroups for Engaged users and Sceptics were small. They served their intended purpose as contrasts, but claims about these groups rest on fewer accounts than claims about the Stalled group.

Seventh, all four districts used the same dashboard. This was a strength for internal comparison but limits transfer to settings where dashboards differ substantially in design, particularly those that offer instructional recommendations, which several teachers in this study said they wanted.

## 4.12 Summary

This chapter has described a pragmatist, explanatory sequential mixed methods design. Phase 1 surveyed 412 secondary teachers in four districts, with log data for 288, and tested a trust-extended acceptance model using confirmatory factor analysis, structural equation modeling with a latent interaction, and latent profile analysis. The connecting point used the latent profiles to select 24 teachers for interview, with an emphasis on the Willing but stalled group. Phase 2 analyzed those interviews thematically. Integration was planned at the design, methods and interpretation levels and is carried out in Chapter 7 through a joint display and by following threads from the quantitative results through the qualitative data. The chapter has also set out the ethical safeguards that a study of a monitoring technology required and the limitations that qualify its inferences. Chapter 5 now reports the results of Phase 1.

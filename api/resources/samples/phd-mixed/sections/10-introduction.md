# Chapter 1: Introduction

On a Tuesday afternoon in the second month of my fieldwork, a science teacher I will call Mr. Boateng shared his screen during our video interview and opened the district dashboard to show me what it could do. He clicked through a class overview, a list of students colored red, amber and green, and a chart of assessment results by standard. He knew where everything was. He had attended the training, he thought the tool was well designed, and he said, without prompting, that he fully intended to use it more. Then he closed the laptop and explained that he had last opened the dashboard three weeks earlier, for a department meeting, and that the students marked red were students he had already been worried about for a month.

That exchange captures the puzzle at the center of this dissertation. Mr. Boateng is not a reluctant adopter in the usual sense. He sees the value of the tool, finds it easy to use and intends to use it. By the standards of the models most often used to study technology adoption, he should be a user. Yet his actual use is occasional, shallow and driven by meeting schedules rather than by his teaching. This dissertation asks why teachers like him, who intend to use learning analytics dashboards, do not, and what role their trust in the dashboard's data plays in that gap.

This chapter introduces the study. Section 1.1 sets out the background: the rise of learning analytics dashboards in schools and the persistent finding that teachers' use of them is limited. Section 1.2 states the research problem. Section 1.3 states the purpose of the study, and Section 1.4 presents the research questions and hypotheses. Section 1.5 summarizes the research design and Section 1.6 describes the setting. Section 1.7 sets out the significance of the study and its four intended contributions. Section 1.8 defines key terms, Section 1.9 explains the scope, delimitations and my own position as researcher, and Section 1.10 outlines the organization of the dissertation.

## 1.1 Background

### 1.1.1 The promise of learning analytics

Over the past fifteen years, the idea that data generated in the course of teaching and learning could be collected, analyzed and returned to educators to improve their decisions has moved from the margins of educational technology to its center. Early statements of the field framed learning analytics as the measurement, collection, analysis and reporting of data about learners and their contexts, for the purposes of understanding and improving learning {{siemens2011}}. The appeal was easy to see. Schools were already producing large quantities of digital data through learning management systems, online assessments and student information systems, and much of it went unused. Analytics promised to turn those traces into timely, actionable information, allowing teachers to notice struggling students earlier, adjust instruction to the needs of a particular class and evaluate whether their changes made a difference.

From the start, however, the most thoughtful advocates of learning analytics warned that the value of analytics depended less on the sophistication of the algorithms than on whether the resulting information connected with how teaching and learning actually work. {{gasevic2015|n}} argued that analytics risk becoming an end in themselves when they are detached from learning theory and pedagogy, and that counting clicks or logins is not the same as understanding learning. That warning frames this study. The question is not whether dashboards can display data, but whether and how teachers take that data up in practice.

### 1.1.2 Dashboards as the teacher-facing form of analytics

For most classroom teachers, learning analytics arrive in the form of a dashboard: a visual display that aggregates indicators about students or classes into charts, tables and color-coded alerts. Dashboards are designed to support awareness, reflection, sensemaking and, ultimately, changes in teaching that affect learning {{verbert2013}}. In this framing, the dashboard is a step in a chain. It makes something visible; the teacher notices it, interprets it and decides what to do; and the decision changes something for students.

The research on dashboards has grown quickly, but it has tended to concentrate on the first links of this chain. A systematic review of learning dashboard research found that most studies reported on the design of dashboards and on users' perceptions of them, often in higher education and often in pilot settings, while relatively few evaluated whether dashboards changed learning in authentic settings {{schwendimann2017}}. Dashboards for secondary school teachers, used in the course of ordinary work over a full school year, are comparatively under-studied. Chapter 2 reviews this literature in detail.

### 1.1.3 Purchase without use

In the school districts where this study took place, as in many others, dashboards were not adopted by individual teachers but purchased by districts and made available to all staff. This pattern of adoption has consequences. When a district buys a tool, the decision to adopt is made by someone other than the eventual user, and the tool arrives with expectations attached: that teachers will consult it, that professional learning communities will discuss its data, and that its indicators will inform decisions about intervention. District purchase therefore guarantees access but not use.

What district and school leaders in the Northern Lakes region described to me in early conversations, and what Phase 1 of this study later quantified, was a familiar pattern. Nearly every teacher had an account. Most had been trained. Many said, when asked, that the dashboard was useful. Yet platform logs showed that a large share of teachers logged in rarely, and that logins clustered around the dates of scheduled data meetings. Use was shallow (a quick look at a class summary rather than sustained analysis) and uneven (concentrated among a minority of enthusiastic teachers). Leaders were puzzled by this, and some were frustrated. They had invested in a tool that teachers said they valued, and it was not being used in the way they had hoped.

This pattern is not unique to dashboards. Research on teachers' integration of technology has long distinguished between external, first-order barriers, such as lack of access, time, training and support, and internal, second-order barriers rooted in teachers' beliefs about teaching and about the technology itself {{ertmer1999}}. District purchase removes some first-order barriers, most obviously access, but it may leave others, such as time, untouched, and it does little to address second-order barriers. A dashboard that teachers can open but do not believe in, or do not have time to think about, is a dashboard that will not be used.

### 1.1.4 Acceptance models and their limits

The most widely used frameworks for explaining whether people use a technology are the Technology Acceptance Model {{davis1989}} and its successor, the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology {{venkatesh2003}}. In TAM, a person's intention to use a system is shaped by its perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, and intention in turn predicts use. UTAUT consolidated eight earlier models into a framework in which performance expectancy, effort expectancy and social influence predict behavioral intention, and intention and facilitating conditions predict use behavior. Both models have been tested extensively, and both consistently explain a substantial share of the variance in intention.

These models are a natural starting point for studying dashboard use, and this study builds on them. But they have two limitations that matter for the problem described above. First, their explanatory power is concentrated on intention. The path from intention to use is usually treated as straightforward, and less attention is given to the conditions under which people who intend to use a system fail to do so. In settings where use is voluntary and the person controls their own time, that assumption may be reasonable. In schools, where teachers' time is heavily structured and the decision to adopt was made by the district, it is much less so. Second, the models were developed largely for workplace information systems whose outputs users could take at face value. They have no construct for whether users believe the information the system gives them. For a dashboard whose entire function is to provide information about students, that omission is significant.

### 1.1.5 Trust in data

Trust has been studied extensively in organizational and human factors research. In the integrative model of organizational trust, trust is defined as a willingness to be vulnerable to another party based on positive expectations about that party's conduct, and the trustworthiness of the other party is assessed through perceptions of its ability, benevolence and integrity {{mayer1995}}. Research on trust in automation has adapted this idea to people's reliance on automated systems, arguing that trust guides reliance when systems are too complex to understand fully, and that the goal is calibrated trust: trust that matches the system's actual capabilities {{lee2004}}. Too much trust leads to misuse; too little leads to disuse.

A related line of research suggests that people's trust in algorithmic outputs may be particularly fragile. In a series of experiments, {{dietvorst2015|n}} found that people who saw an algorithm make an error were more likely to abandon it in favor of human judgment, even when the algorithm outperformed humans overall. If teachers respond to dashboard errors in a similar way, a single visible mistake, such as a student flagged as at risk who had in fact completed the work, might be enough to undermine a teacher's willingness to rely on the dashboard for months.

Despite this body of work, trust has rarely been incorporated into studies of teachers' acceptance of learning analytics. The ethical literature on learning analytics has raised concerns about surveillance, consent and power that bear directly on trust {{slade2013}}, and research on data-driven leadership has emphasized that the culture in which data are used, including whether data are used to support or to judge teachers, shapes how teachers engage with them {{datnow2014}}. But these strands have not been brought together with acceptance models in a way that allows their relative importance to be tested. Chapter 2 develops this argument, and Chapter 3 builds the theoretical framework that brings them together.

### 1.1.6 Data literacy and the step from seeing to acting

A final strand of background concerns what teachers need in order to turn dashboard information into action. {{mandinach2016|n}} described data literacy for teaching as an integration of data skills with knowledge of content, pedagogy and students, together with dispositions toward inquiry. Their framework makes clear that reading a chart is only the beginning. A teacher must also decide what the pattern means for a particular class and what to do differently on Monday morning. A dashboard that shows what is happening without helping a teacher decide what to do next leaves the most demanding part of the work to the teacher, who may not have the time or support to do it. Whether dashboards are used may therefore depend on whether they help teachers act, not only on whether they help teachers see.

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem

Districts are purchasing learning analytics dashboards for teachers, but teachers' use of them is shallow and uneven. The frameworks most often used to explain technology use, TAM and UTAUT, account well for teachers' intentions but are less able to explain why teachers who intend to use dashboards do not. In particular, they do not include a construct for teachers' trust in the data the dashboard presents, even though dashboards exist to provide data and even though research in organizational behavior and human factors suggests that trust shapes whether people rely on automated information. Research on learning dashboards has concentrated on design and perception, often in higher education, and has rarely examined sustained use by secondary teachers in the districts that bought the tools.

The problem therefore has three parts. Empirically, the gap between intention and use among teachers with access to dashboards is poorly documented and poorly explained. Theoretically, acceptance models lack a way of representing trust in data and of explaining the conditions under which intention translates into use. Methodologically, studies of dashboard use have relied either on self-report or on platform logs, and rarely on both together with teachers' own accounts, so it is not clear how well "use" itself is being measured.

A study that addressed only one of these parts would leave the others unresolved. A survey alone could test whether trust predicts intention and use, but it could not explain what trust means to teachers or why the intention–use gap appears. Interviews alone could describe teachers' experiences in depth, but could not establish how widespread those experiences are or test how the constructs relate. This is why the study uses an explanatory sequential mixed methods design, in which a survey identifies the relationships and the groups that need explaining and interviews then explain them.

## 1.3 Purpose of the Study

The purpose of this explanatory sequential mixed methods study was to explain secondary teachers' use of learning analytics dashboards in four public school districts. The study had two linked aims. The first was to test a trust-extended acceptance model on survey data from 412 teachers, examining whether performance expectancy, effort expectancy, facilitating conditions and trust in dashboard data predicted teachers' intention to use the dashboard and their reported use of it, and whether trust moderated the relationship between intention and use. The second was to explain the quantitative results through in-depth interviews with 24 teachers selected from profiles identified in the survey data, with particular attention to the large group of teachers who intended to use the dashboard but did not.

The two aims were connected by design. The quantitative phase had priority and established the relationships to be explained; the qualitative phase was designed in light of the quantitative results, and the two were integrated in interpretation to produce meta-inferences that neither phase could support on its own.

## 1.4 Research Questions and Hypotheses

The study addressed three research questions, one for each strand and one for their integration.

**RQ1 (quantitative).** To what extent do performance expectancy, effort expectancy, facilitating conditions and trust in dashboard data predict teachers' reported use of learning analytics dashboards?

**RQ2 (qualitative).** How do teachers explain how they use, adapt or avoid dashboards in their practice?

**RQ3 (mixed methods).** How do the qualitative accounts explain the quantitative relationships, particularly the gap between intention and use?

RQ1 was addressed through six hypotheses, derived in Chapter 3 from UTAUT, from the trust literature and from the argument that trust conditions whether intentions are carried through:

- **H1.** Performance expectancy is positively related to behavioral intention to use the dashboard.
- **H2.** Effort expectancy is positively related to behavioral intention to use the dashboard.
- **H3.** Trust in dashboard data is positively related to behavioral intention to use the dashboard.
- **H4.** Behavioral intention is positively related to dashboard use.
- **H5.** Facilitating conditions are positively related to dashboard use.
- **H6.** Trust in dashboard data moderates the relationship between behavioral intention and use, such that the relationship is stronger when trust is high.

H1, H2, H4 and H5 restate core UTAUT relationships in the dashboard context; they are included because the study needed to establish whether the baseline model held for this population before asking what trust added. H3 and H6 are the study's theoretical extensions. H3 treats trust as an additional antecedent of intention, in the way that trust has been added to acceptance models in other fields. H6 is the more distinctive proposition: it treats trust not only as a reason to intend but as a condition under which intentions are acted on. Social influence, the fourth UTAUT predictor, was not included in the structural model for reasons explained in Section 3.5.4.

RQ2 was deliberately open. Although the interview protocol was informed by the Phase 1 results, the question asked how teachers themselves explained their use, adaptation and avoidance of dashboards, so that explanations not anticipated by the model could emerge. RQ3 then asked how those explanations accounted for the quantitative relationships, and especially for the profile of teachers who reported high intention and low use.

## 1.5 Overview of the Research Design

The study used an explanatory sequential design, in which a quantitative phase is followed by a qualitative phase designed to explain its results {{creswell2018}}. In the notation conventionally used for mixed methods designs, it was a QUAN → qual design: the quantitative phase had priority, and the qualitative phase was smaller and dependent on it. The practical decisions involved in such designs (about priority, implementation, the point at which the phases connect and the stage at which they are integrated) are set out by {{ivankova2006|n}}, whose account guided the planning of this study.

The study was grounded in a pragmatist stance. Following {{morgan2007|n}}, I treat pragmatism not as a license to combine methods without reflection but as an approach that emphasizes abductive reasoning between theory and data, intersubjective agreement about meaning and the transferability of findings to other settings. This stance suited a study that began with a deductive test of a theoretical model and then moved inductively to teachers' accounts, before returning abductively to ask what those accounts implied for the model.

> **Figure 1.1** Overview of the explanatory sequential design. Phase 1: survey of secondary teachers (n = 412) with linked platform logs (n = 288) → confirmatory factor analysis, structural equation model, moderation and latent profile analysis → connecting point: purposive selection of interviewees from three latent profiles → Phase 2: semi-structured interviews (n = 24) → thematic analysis → integration: joint display and meta-inferences.

Phase 1 was a cross-sectional survey of secondary teachers in the four districts. Of 1,180 teachers invited, 412 provided usable responses, a response rate of 34.9%. The survey measured performance expectancy, effort expectancy and facilitating conditions using items adapted from UTAUT, trust in dashboard data using five items adapted from the organizational trust and automation trust literatures, behavioral intention and self-reported use. For 288 teachers who consented, platform log data on monthly logins were linked to survey responses, allowing self-reported use to be compared with a behavioral record. The measurement model was evaluated through confirmatory factor analysis, the hypotheses were tested through structural equation modeling with a latent interaction for H6, and latent profile analysis was used to identify groups of teachers with distinct patterns of intention, trust and use.

The connecting point between the phases was the selection of interview participants. Teachers who had agreed to be contacted were sampled purposively from the three latent profiles, with deliberate over-representation of the profile of teachers who intended to use the dashboard but did not. Phase 2 consisted of semi-structured interviews with 24 teachers, conducted by video call and lasting between 45 and 70 minutes, analyzed using thematic analysis {{braun2006}}.

Integration took place at three levels: at the design level through the explanatory sequential structure, at the methods level through connecting the samples, and at the interpretation level through a joint display and a process of "following a thread" from the quantitative results through the qualitative data {{fetters2013;ocathain2010}}. Chapter 4 describes and justifies each of these decisions in detail.

## 1.6 Setting

The study took place in the Northern Lakes region of a state in the upper Midwest of the United States. Four public school districts in the region took part, together serving 31 secondary schools: middle schools, junior high schools and high schools enrolling students in grades 6 through 12. The districts varied in size and character. The largest was a suburban district with the greatest number of secondary schools; the smallest was a rural consolidated district with only a handful. Two districts included a small city, and one included both suburban and rural attendance areas. This variation was useful, because it meant that the sample included teachers working in very different organizational conditions while using the same tool.

What the four districts had in common was the dashboard. Several years before the study, the districts had joined a regional purchasing consortium and adopted the same commercial learning analytics dashboard, referred to throughout this dissertation as "the district dashboard." The dashboard drew data from the districts' learning management system, their interim assessment platform and their student information system, and refreshed overnight for most data sources. Teachers could view class and student summaries, assessment results by standard, assignment completion, attendance and a set of color-coded "early warning" indicators that combined these sources. They could filter by student group, export tables and set up notifications. Administrators had access to the same data aggregated at the department, school and district levels, including views organized by teacher.

All four districts had provided initial training on the dashboard, and all four expected professional learning communities (PLCs), which in these districts were subject or grade-level teams that met weekly or fortnightly, to use dashboard data in at least some of their meetings. None of the districts had made dashboard use a formal part of teacher evaluation, but, as Chapter 6 shows, many teachers were uncertain about how administrators used the data, and that uncertainty became one of the most important themes in the study.

Holding the tool constant across districts was a deliberate design choice. Many studies of technology acceptance compare users of different systems or ask about technology in general. By studying teachers who all used the same dashboard, with the same features, data sources and update schedule, this study could attribute differences in trust and use to teachers and their working conditions rather than to differences between tools. The cost of this choice, discussed in Section 1.9 and again in Chapter 8, is that the findings may be shaped by features of this particular dashboard.

## 1.7 Significance of the Study

### 1.7.1 Why the intention–use gap matters

The gap between what teachers intend to do with dashboards and what they actually do is more than a curiosity for acceptance researchers. It has practical consequences for districts, for teachers and, potentially, for students. Districts invest money, training time and administrative attention in dashboards on the assumption that the information they provide will reach classroom decisions. If most teachers consult the dashboard only when a meeting requires it, that investment is largely spent on compliance rather than on teaching. For teachers, a tool they believe could help but cannot find time for can become one more source of professional guilt, and one more indicator by which they suspect they are being judged. For students, the value of an early warning depends on its being early enough, and acted on, to make a difference.

The gap also matters for how districts respond. If low use is interpreted as resistance, the natural response is more training, more encouragement or more accountability for use. If, as this study suggests, many low users are teachers who already value the dashboard and intend to use it, those responses are poorly targeted. More training does not create time, and more accountability may deepen the very concerns about surveillance that undermine trust. Understanding why intentions stall is therefore a precondition for responding to low use in a way that does not make it worse.

### 1.7.2 Intended contributions

The study makes four contributions, which are previewed here and developed in Chapters 7 and 8.

The first is theoretical: **a trust-extended acceptance model in which trust in dashboard data moderates the relationship between intention and use.** Acceptance models typically add new constructs as further antecedents of intention. This study tests that kind of addition (H3) but also proposes and tests a different role for trust, as a condition that determines how strongly intention carries through into behavior (H6). In the Phase 1 data, the intention–use relationship was substantially stronger among teachers with high trust (β = .49) than among those with low trust (β = .15, p = .07). If this pattern holds in other settings, it implies that acceptance models applied to information-providing systems should treat trust as part of the mechanism linking intention to use, not only as one more reason to intend.

The second contribution is conceptual: **a three-part account of trust in educational data as timeliness, accuracy and perceived purpose.** The survey measured trust with items adapted from established instruments, which emphasize the reliability and accuracy of information. Teachers' accounts in Phase 2 showed that, for them, trusting the dashboard also meant trusting that its data arrived in time to be useful and fitted what they knew of their students, and trusting that the data would be used for purposes they endorsed rather than to judge them. This expanded account connects the human factors view of trust in automation {{lee2004}} with the ethical and organizational concerns raised in the learning analytics and data use literatures {{slade2013;datnow2014}}.

The third contribution is methodological: **evidence that "use" of dashboards is under-measured when it is captured by logs or self-report alone.** Self-reported use and logged logins in this study were related (r = .58) but far from interchangeable, and interviews revealed forms of use, such as exporting data to spreadsheets and taking screenshots to share with parents, that neither measure captured well. The study therefore recommends that future research treat use as a broader construct, measured through multiple sources, and that districts be cautious about judging adoption from login counts.

The fourth contribution is practical: **recommendations for protected time, actionable dashboard views and transparent data governance.** The integrated findings suggest that the teachers most likely to respond to support are those who already intend to use the dashboard but lack time, guidance on what to do next and confidence about how the data will be used. These are conditions that districts and vendors can change.

### 1.7.3 Audiences

The study speaks to several audiences. For researchers in technology acceptance, it offers a test of a trust-extended model in a setting where adoption is mandated at the organizational level but use remains discretionary. For learning analytics researchers, it adds a study of sustained, authentic use by secondary teachers to a literature that has concentrated on design and higher education {{schwendimann2017}}. For mixed methods researchers, it offers a worked example of an explanatory sequential design in which latent profiles serve as the connecting point and in which discordance between strands is treated as a finding rather than a nuisance. For district leaders, professional development designers and dashboard developers, it offers evidence about which conditions most constrain use and which changes are most likely to matter.

## 1.8 Definitions of Key Terms

The following terms are used throughout the dissertation with the meanings given here. Several are developed more fully in Chapter 3.

**Learning analytics dashboard (LAD).** A software display that aggregates data about learners, generated by educational systems, into visual indicators intended to support awareness, reflection and decision-making {{verbert2013}}. In this study, the term refers specifically to the teacher-facing views of the district dashboard.

**The district dashboard.** The commercial dashboard used by all four participating districts. It is not named, because the study concerns teachers' use of a type of tool rather than the merits of a particular product.

**Performance expectancy (PE).** The degree to which a teacher believes that using the dashboard will help them improve their teaching or their students' learning, adapted from the UTAUT construct of the same name {{venkatesh2003}}.

**Effort expectancy (EE).** The degree of ease a teacher associates with using the dashboard {{venkatesh2003}}.

**Facilitating conditions (FC).** The degree to which a teacher believes that organizational and technical resources exist to support use of the dashboard {{venkatesh2003}}. As measured in this study, the construct concerned resources, knowledge, compatibility with other systems and the availability of assistance. As Chapter 7 discusses, it did not capture time, and that omission turned out to matter.

**Trust in dashboard data.** A teacher's willingness to rely on the information presented by the dashboard when making decisions about students, based on positive expectations about its accuracy and dependability. The definition draws on the organizational definition of trust as willingness to be vulnerable {{mayer1995}} and on the human factors account of trust as an attitude guiding reliance on automation {{lee2004}}. Chapter 3 explains why the object of trust is the data rather than the vendor or the district.

**Behavioral intention (BI).** A teacher's stated intention to use the dashboard in the near future and to draw on it in planning instruction, measured with three items.

**Use.** In the survey, the average self-reported frequency, over the previous month, of six dashboard actions, each rated on a 0–5 scale. In the log data, the number of logins per month. In the qualitative phase, use was defined by teachers themselves and included practices, such as exporting and screenshotting, that took place partly outside the dashboard. The tension between these definitions is one of the study's findings.

**Intention–use gap.** The difference between a teacher's stated intention to use the dashboard and their reported or recorded use, and, at the level of the model, the extent to which intention fails to predict use.

**Latent profile.** A subgroup of teachers identified statistically by a shared pattern of scores on the study's constructs. Phase 1 identified three profiles, labeled "Engaged users," "Willing but stalled" and "Sceptics." These labels summarize statistical patterns and are not meant to characterize individual teachers' professionalism.

**Professional learning community (PLC).** In the participating districts, a team of teachers organized by subject or grade level that met regularly to discuss instruction and student progress, often with reference to dashboard data.

**Data literacy for teaching.** The capacity to turn data into instructional knowledge and action, combining skills in collecting, analyzing and interpreting data with knowledge of content, pedagogy and students {{mandinach2016}}.

## 1.9 Scope, Delimitations and Researcher Position

### 1.9.1 Scope and delimitations

Several boundaries were set deliberately. The study concerned secondary teachers only, because secondary teachers typically teach many more students than elementary teachers and encounter dashboard data class by class, which shapes how they use it. Elementary teachers, administrators, counselors and students were not surveyed, although administrators' use of dashboard data became an important part of teachers' accounts. The study concerned a single dashboard used across four districts, for the reasons given in Section 1.6. It was cross-sectional: the survey and logs captured a single period of the school year, and the interviews followed in the next term. The study therefore cannot establish the direction of causal relationships among trust, intention and use, a limitation discussed in Chapters 5 and 8.

The study also did not examine the effects of dashboard use on student learning. It is concerned with whether and how teachers use dashboards, which is a precondition for any effect on students but not the same thing. Claims about the value of dashboards for learning lie outside its scope, and I have tried throughout not to assume that more use is always better. Indeed, one implication of the trust literature is that appropriate reliance, rather than maximum reliance, is the goal {{lee2004}}.

Finally, the study did not evaluate the dashboard's data quality directly. When teachers said the data were late or wrong, I could sometimes confirm that the update schedule worked as they described, but I did not audit the accuracy of indicators. Teachers' perceptions of accuracy are the object of study, and those perceptions matter whether or not they are correct.

### 1.9.2 Researcher position

Before beginning doctoral study, I taught secondary science for eight years in a district outside the Northern Lakes region that used a different learning analytics product. In my last years there I led my department's data meetings, and I came to this study as someone who had both championed data use and watched colleagues, and occasionally myself, fail to follow through on good intentions. That history gave me ready rapport with teachers and a working knowledge of the kind of dashboard the districts used, even though I had never used the district dashboard itself. It also brought risks. I was inclined to sympathize with teachers' complaints about time and with their suspicion of administrative uses of data, and I had to guard against reading those sympathies into the data. Chapter 4 describes how I attempted to manage this, including through a reflexive journal, peer debriefing and deliberate attention to disconfirming cases, particularly among the Engaged users who had found ways to use the dashboard under the same conditions that stalled their colleagues.

## 1.10 Organization of the Dissertation

The dissertation is organized in eight chapters.

Chapter 2 reviews the literature on learning analytics dashboards, teachers' data use, technology acceptance and trust. It argues that these literatures have developed largely in parallel and that their intersection, teachers' trust in the data dashboards provide and its role in turning intention into use, is where the most important unanswered questions lie.

Chapter 3 sets out the theoretical framework. It examines TAM and UTAUT, the organizational and automation literatures on trust, and frameworks for teachers' technology integration and data literacy, and it builds the trust-extended model tested in Phase 1, deriving the six hypotheses.

Chapter 4 describes the methodology: the pragmatist stance, the explanatory sequential design, the survey instrument and log data, the analytic strategy for Phase 1, the connecting point and sampling for Phase 2, the interview procedures and thematic analysis, the integration procedures, quality criteria and ethical considerations.

Chapter 5 reports the quantitative results: the response and missing data, descriptive statistics, the measurement model, the structural model, the moderation analysis and the latent profiles. It ends by identifying the puzzles the qualitative phase was designed to explain.

Chapter 6 reports the qualitative findings, organized around five themes: trust as timeliness and fit; time and the structure of the teaching week; the step from seeing to acting; surveillance and professional judgment; and workarounds as a form of use.

Chapter 7 integrates the two phases. It presents a joint display of quantitative and qualitative findings, draws meta-inferences of confirmation, expansion and discordance, and discusses each research question in relation to the literature.

Chapter 8 concludes the dissertation. It summarizes the findings by research question, sets out the four contributions, discusses implications for policy, practice and dashboard design, acknowledges the study's limitations and suggests directions for future research.

Before turning to the empirical work, the dissertation must first establish what is already known. Chapter 2 begins that task with a review of the research on learning analytics dashboards and their use by teachers.

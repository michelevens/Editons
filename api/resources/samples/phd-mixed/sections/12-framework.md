# Chapter 3: Theoretical Framework

Chapter 2 showed that research on learning analytics dashboards, research on teachers' data use and research on technology acceptance have developed largely in parallel, and that the role of teachers' trust in dashboard data has fallen between them. This chapter builds the theoretical framework that brings those strands together. It does two jobs. First, it develops the trust-extended acceptance model tested in Phase 1, deriving the six hypotheses stated in Chapter 1 and specifying the constructs precisely enough to be measured. Second, it sets out the wider set of ideas, about teachers' barriers to technology integration, about data literacy and about the organizational and ethical conditions of data use, that informed the design of Phase 2 and the interpretation of the integrated findings.

The chapter proceeds as follows. Section 3.1 explains the role a theoretical framework plays in an explanatory sequential study. Section 3.2 examines the technology acceptance tradition, from the Technology Acceptance Model to the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology, and identifies the limitations that motivate this study. Section 3.3 develops the concept of trust in dashboard data, drawing on organizational and human factors research. Section 3.4 introduces frameworks from educational research that explain why teachers' intentions may not carry through into practice. Section 3.5 assembles these elements into the research model and states the hypotheses with their rationale. Section 3.6 explains how the framework guided, and was deliberately held open by, the qualitative phase. Section 3.7 summarizes the chapter.

## 3.1 The Role of Theory in an Explanatory Sequential Study

In an explanatory sequential design, theory does different work in each phase {{creswell2018}}. In the quantitative phase, it supplies constructs and specifies expected relationships among them, which the survey then tests. In the qualitative phase, it serves more loosely as a sensitizing framework: it directs attention to particular aspects of participants' experience without prescribing what they will say. At the point of integration, theory provides the language through which results from the two phases are compared, and the integrated findings may in turn lead to revisions of the theory.

This dual role shapes the chapter. The research model in Section 3.5 is stated deductively and precisely, because Phase 1 needed testable hypotheses. The frameworks in Section 3.4 are presented more openly, because they were used to inform the interview protocol and to interpret teachers' accounts, not to constrain them. I adopt the pragmatist position that the movement between these modes is itself abductive: the study begins with a theory, confronts it with data, encounters results the theory does not fully explain, and seeks the best available explanation for them {{morgan2007}}. Chapter 7 returns to the model in that spirit.

## 3.2 The Technology Acceptance Tradition

### 3.2.1 The Technology Acceptance Model

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) was proposed to explain why people accept or reject information technology in the workplace {{davis1989}}. Its central claim is that two beliefs about a system, its perceived usefulness and its perceived ease of use, shape a person's attitude toward using it and their intention to use it, and that intention is the most immediate determinant of actual use. Perceived usefulness refers to the extent to which a person believes that using the system would improve their job performance; perceived ease of use refers to the extent to which they believe that using it would be free of effort. Ease of use was proposed to influence intention both directly and indirectly, through its effect on perceived usefulness: a system that is easier to use is, other things being equal, more useful, because less effort is needed to obtain its benefits.

TAM's appeal lies in its parsimony. It reduces the many reasons people might use or avoid a technology to two beliefs that can be measured with short, reliable scales, and it has been applied across a very wide range of technologies and populations. In Davis's original studies, perceived usefulness was the stronger predictor of use, and that pattern has generally been repeated: people will tolerate a system that is somewhat difficult to use if it is useful enough, but ease of use alone does not make a useless system attractive.

### 3.2.2 The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology

The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) was developed by integrating eight earlier models of acceptance, including TAM, into a single framework {{venkatesh2003}}. UTAUT proposes four core determinants. Performance expectancy, which subsumes perceived usefulness and related constructs, is the degree to which a person believes that using the system will help them attain gains in job performance. Effort expectancy, which subsumes perceived ease of use, is the degree of ease associated with using the system. Social influence is the degree to which a person perceives that important others believe they should use the system. Facilitating conditions are the degree to which a person believes that an organizational and technical infrastructure exists to support use. In the model, performance expectancy, effort expectancy and social influence predict behavioral intention, while behavioral intention and facilitating conditions predict use behavior. Gender, age, experience and voluntariness of use were proposed as moderators of several of these relationships.

Two features of UTAUT are important for this study. First, facilitating conditions are modeled as a direct predictor of use rather than of intention. The reasoning is that when performance and effort expectancy are taken into account, the availability of support does not make people more willing to use a system so much as it makes them more able to act on their willingness. This distinction between willingness and ability is central to the problem this dissertation addresses. Second, UTAUT treats voluntariness as a moderator, recognizing that the dynamics of acceptance differ when use is mandated. In the Northern Lakes districts, use of the dashboard occupied an ambiguous position. Access was universal and some use was expected in PLC meetings, but how often and how deeply a teacher used the dashboard in their own planning was left to them. In UTAUT's terms, dashboard use was formally voluntary but organizationally encouraged.

### 3.2.3 What acceptance models explain, and what they leave out

Acceptance models have been criticized on several grounds, and three of these criticisms bear directly on this study.

The first concerns the intention–use link. In both TAM and UTAUT, intention is the bridge between beliefs and behavior, and most of the explanatory work of the models is done on the belief side of the bridge. The models usually explain more variance in intention than in use, and the conditions under which intentions fail to become behavior receive relatively little theoretical attention. UTAUT's inclusion of facilitating conditions as a direct predictor of use is a partial acknowledgment of the problem, but it reduces the obstacles between intention and use to the availability of resources and support. In settings where the main obstacle is something else, such as the structure of the working week or the user's doubts about what the system tells them, that reduction may miss what matters.

The second criticism concerns the object of acceptance. Acceptance models treat the system as a tool whose outputs users can take at face value; what varies is how useful and easy to use the tool is. For many workplace systems this is reasonable. A word processor or a scheduling system does not present the user with claims about the world that the user must decide whether to believe. A learning analytics dashboard does. Its outputs are inferences, such as a student's risk status, mastery of a standard or likelihood of failing a course, drawn from data that may be incomplete, delayed or poorly aligned with the teacher's own knowledge. Whether a teacher uses the dashboard may depend not only on whether it is useful in principle and easy to operate but on whether its outputs are believed. Acceptance models have no construct for this.

The third criticism concerns context. Acceptance models are deliberately general, and their constructs are defined in ways that can apply to any technology. That generality is a strength for comparison but a weakness for explanation, because it abstracts away the features of a particular work setting that shape use. Teachers' work is organized by bells, timetables, marking cycles, meeting schedules and the presence of students, and the time available for a task like data analysis is not a matter of individual choice. Research on teachers' technology use, reviewed in Section 3.4, suggests that these features shape use in ways that general acceptance constructs only partly capture.

None of these criticisms is a reason to abandon acceptance models. They remain the best-tested frameworks available for explaining technology use, and the core UTAUT relationships provide a baseline against which any extension can be judged. Rather, the criticisms identify where an extension is needed. This study extends the model in two directions: by adding trust in dashboard data as a construct (Section 3.3), and by proposing that trust conditions the relationship between intention and use (Section 3.5).

## 3.3 Trust in Dashboard Data

### 3.3.1 Trust as willingness to be vulnerable

The most widely used definition of trust in organizational research defines it as the willingness of one party to be vulnerable to the actions of another, based on the expectation that the other will perform an action important to the trustor, irrespective of the trustor's ability to monitor or control that other party {{mayer1995}}. Three features of this definition are worth drawing out. First, trust is a willingness, not a behavior: it is a disposition that may or may not lead to action depending on the circumstances. Second, trust involves vulnerability: it matters only where the trustor has something at stake and could be harmed if the trust were misplaced. Third, trust is needed precisely where monitoring is incomplete: if the trustor could check everything, trust would be unnecessary.

{{mayer1995|n}} further proposed that trust depends on the trustor's perception of the other party's trustworthiness, which has three dimensions. Ability is the perceived competence of the trustee in the relevant domain. Benevolence is the perceived extent to which the trustee wants to do good for the trustor, apart from self-interest. Integrity is the perceived extent to which the trustee adheres to principles the trustor finds acceptable. The model also distinguishes trust from the trustor's general propensity to trust and from the risk-taking behavior that trust makes possible. Trust leads to risk-taking in the relationship, the outcomes of which feed back into perceptions of trustworthiness.

Each element of this account maps onto teachers' relationship with a dashboard. A teacher who relies on the dashboard's early warning indicators to decide which students to follow up with is making themselves vulnerable: if the indicators are wrong, a student who needs help may be missed, or time may be spent on a student who does not. The teacher cannot easily monitor how the indicators are produced. And the teacher's judgment of the dashboard's trustworthiness may well involve all three of Mayer and colleagues' dimensions: whether the data are competently produced (ability), whether the system is designed to help teachers rather than to serve some other interest (benevolence) and whether the data are handled according to principles the teacher accepts (integrity).

### 3.3.2 Trust in automation and appropriate reliance

The organizational model was developed to describe trust between people, or between people and organizations. Research in human factors has adapted the concept to people's relationships with automated systems. {{lee2004|n}} argued that trust is an attitude that guides reliance on automation, particularly when a system is too complex to be fully understood and when circumstances make complete analysis impractical. In such conditions, trust functions as a heuristic: rather than checking a system's every output, people rely on it to the extent they trust it.

The central normative idea in this literature is calibration. Trust is well calibrated when it matches the actual capability of the system. Overtrust, trust that exceeds capability, leads to misuse, in which people rely on a system in situations it cannot handle. Distrust, trust that falls short of capability, leads to disuse, in which people fail to rely on a system that would have helped them. The goal of design and training is therefore not to maximize trust but to promote appropriate reliance. {{lee2004|n}} also proposed that trust is shaped by information about a system's performance, its process and its purpose: what it does and how well, how it does it, and why it was designed. Where information about these is lacking or ambiguous, trust is likely to be poorly calibrated.

This framework helps clarify what is at stake in teachers' trust in dashboard data. Teachers who distrust the dashboard may disuse it, even when its indicators would have helped them notice a student they had missed. But teachers who overtrust it may rely on indicators that reflect stale or incomplete data, and may substitute the dashboard's categories for their own knowledge of students. The question for practice is not how to make teachers trust dashboards more, but how to make their trust track the dashboard's actual reliability. That reframing matters for how the results of this study are interpreted, and Chapter 8 returns to it.

The performance, process and purpose distinction also anticipated one of the findings of Phase 2. When teachers in this study talked about trust, they talked about the dashboard's accuracy (performance), about how and when its data were updated (process), and about who the data were really for (purpose). Chapter 7 develops this correspondence into the three-part account of trust that is the study's second contribution.

### 3.3.3 The fragility of trust after error

A further strand of research concerns how trust in algorithms responds to errors. In a set of experiments, {{dietvorst2015|n}} found that people who observed an algorithm make forecasting errors became less willing to use it than people who had not seen it perform, and more inclined to choose a human forecaster instead, even when the algorithm made fewer errors than the human. They called this pattern algorithm aversion. The implication is that confidence in an automated system may be asymmetrically fragile: a visible error weighs more heavily than an invisible record of success.

The relevance to dashboards is direct. A teacher who sees the dashboard flag a student as missing assignments that the teacher knows were submitted, or report an assessment score that does not match the gradebook, has seen the algorithm err. If teachers respond as the participants in these experiments did, a single such episode might shift them toward relying on their own judgment for a long time afterward, regardless of how often the dashboard was correct. Because dashboard data pass through several systems before reaching the teacher, and because update schedules can produce temporary mismatches, such visible errors may be common even when the underlying data are generally sound. This possibility was not built into the Phase 1 model, which measured trust at a single time point, but it sensitized the Phase 2 interviews to teachers' stories about the moment their trust changed.

### 3.3.4 Specifying the object of trust

Trust always has an object: one trusts someone or something to do something. In studies of technology, the object of trust can be specified in several ways: as the technology itself, as the vendor that produces it, as the organization that deploys it, or as the information it provides. These are different. A teacher might trust the vendor's competence but distrust the district's purposes, or trust the system's functionality while doubting the accuracy of particular data.

In this study, the object of trust was specified as the data presented by the dashboard. This choice was made for three reasons. First, the dashboard's function is to present information about students; whether teachers act on it depends most directly on whether they believe that information. Second, specifying the data rather than the system separates trust from the constructs already in the model. Belief that the dashboard is useful in principle is captured by performance expectancy, and belief that it is easy to operate is captured by effort expectancy; trust in the data captures something different, namely whether the particular outputs the teacher sees can be relied on. Third, the items available in the organizational and automation literatures could be adapted to the data as an object without great strain, focusing on dependability, accuracy and the teacher's willingness to base decisions on the information {{mayer1995;lee2004}}. Chapter 4 describes the adaptation of the five trust items.

This specification has a cost, which became evident in Phase 2. By focusing on the data, the survey trust measure foregrounded performance-related judgments, and gave less weight to teachers' judgments about the purposes for which the data would be used, which correspond more closely to the benevolence and integrity dimensions of the organizational model. Chapter 7 treats this as a point at which the qualitative findings expand the quantitative construct.

## 3.4 Teachers, Technology and Data: Frameworks from Educational Research

The acceptance and trust literatures come largely from outside education. To understand why teachers' intentions to use dashboards might not carry through, the framework also draws on educational research on technology integration, the diffusion of innovations, data literacy and the organizational and ethical conditions of data use. These frameworks were not tested as such in Phase 1, but they shaped the interview protocol and provided much of the vocabulary for interpreting Phase 2.

### 3.4.1 First- and second-order barriers

{{ertmer1999|n}} distinguished two kinds of barriers to teachers' integration of technology. First-order barriers are external to the teacher: lack of access to equipment, insufficient time, inadequate training and inadequate technical support. Second-order barriers are internal: teachers' beliefs about teaching and learning, their established classroom practices and their willingness to change. Ertmer argued that first-order barriers are more visible and easier to measure, and that removing them is necessary but not sufficient, because second-order barriers may persist after access and training are in place. Second-order barriers are harder to address because they are rooted in teachers' professional identities and are often not explicitly articulated.

This distinction is useful for this study in two ways. First, it highlights that the UTAUT construct of facilitating conditions corresponds closely to a subset of first-order barriers, namely access, training and support. Time, which Ertmer identified as a first-order barrier, is not usually included in measures of facilitating conditions, including the measure used in this study. If time is a significant constraint on dashboard use, the survey's facilitating conditions measure would not capture it. This was a measurement limitation I recognized only after Phase 2, and Chapter 7 discusses its implications for interpreting the effect of facilitating conditions on use.

Second, the concept of second-order barriers draws attention to teachers' beliefs about what kind of knowledge is relevant to teaching. A teacher who believes that knowledge of students comes from relationships and classroom observation may regard dashboard data as at best a supplement and at worst a distraction. Such beliefs are not the same as low trust in data, but they may interact with it: a teacher who already doubts the relevance of aggregated indicators may be quicker to lose trust when they encounter an error.

### 3.4.2 Diffusion of innovations

{{rogers2003|n}} described the diffusion of innovations as the process by which an innovation is communicated over time among members of a social system. Rogers identified five perceived attributes of innovations that influence their rate of adoption: relative advantage over existing practice, compatibility with existing values and needs, complexity, trialability and observability. He also described adopter categories, from innovators and early adopters through the early and late majority to laggards, distinguished by when individuals adopt relative to others in their system.

Two elements of diffusion theory are helpful here. First, the attributes of compatibility and relative advantage add nuance to performance expectancy. A dashboard may be useful in principle but incompatible with the rhythm of a teacher's week, or may offer little advantage over the knowledge teachers already have of their students. Teachers who say that the dashboard's red flags identify students they already knew about are, in Rogers's terms, questioning its relative advantage. Second, diffusion theory reminds us that when adoption decisions are made by organizations rather than individuals, the organizational adoption decision is only the beginning; individual members must still decide whether and how to implement it. District purchase of a dashboard is an authority decision in Rogers's sense, and the implementation that follows may involve considerable adaptation, which Rogers called reinvention. The workarounds that teachers in this study developed, such as exporting data to their own spreadsheets, can be understood as reinvention of this kind.

I do not use Rogers's adopter categories to classify teachers. The latent profiles in Phase 1 were derived empirically from teachers' patterns of beliefs, intention and use, not from the timing of adoption, and the labels attached to them were chosen to describe those patterns. But diffusion theory provides a useful caution against interpreting profiles as fixed types: in a diffusion framework, today's late majority may be tomorrow's users, if conditions change.

### 3.4.3 Data literacy for teaching

{{mandinach2016|n}} described data literacy for teaching as the ability to transform information into actionable instructional knowledge and practices, combining knowledge and skills relating to data with knowledge of content, pedagogy and students. Their framework identifies a sequence of components: identifying problems and framing questions, using data, transforming data into information, transforming information into a decision, and evaluating outcomes. It also emphasizes dispositions, such as a belief that all students can learn and a habit of inquiry, as part of data literacy.

The framework's significance for this study lies in its emphasis on the later steps in the sequence. Most dashboards are designed to support the earlier steps: they make data available and transform it into charts and indicators. The step from information to decision, which requires the teacher to connect what the dashboard shows with what they know about content and pedagogy, is left to the teacher. If a teacher lacks time, confidence or guidance to take that step, a dashboard that is useful in principle may still not lead to action. This analysis connects with the warning that analytics must be grounded in learning and pedagogy rather than in measurement for its own sake {{gasevic2015}}, and with the observation that dashboards are designed to support awareness and reflection, but that their effect on teaching depends on what users do with that awareness {{verbert2013}}.

### 3.4.4 Leadership, data culture and ethics

The final set of ideas concerns the organizational and ethical conditions in which teachers encounter data. Research on data-driven leadership has shown that school leaders shape how data are used through the routines they establish, the purposes they emphasize and the culture they model {{datnow2014}}. Where leaders frame data as a resource for teachers' inquiry and protect time for collaborative analysis, teachers are more likely to engage with data constructively. Where data are framed primarily as a tool for accountability, teachers may engage defensively, using data to demonstrate compliance rather than to improve teaching.

The ethical literature on learning analytics raises related concerns from a different direction. {{slade2013|n}} identified a set of ethical issues arising from the collection and analysis of educational data, including the location and interpretation of data, informed consent, privacy, the management and classification of data, and the power relationships that analytics may reinforce. Although their analysis focused mainly on students as the subjects of analytics, the same issues apply to teachers. Dashboards that make student data visible to teachers also make teachers' classes visible to administrators, and data organized by teacher can become an instrument of evaluation, whether or not that is the district's intention.

These conditions connect back to the organizational model of trust. A teacher's judgment of whether dashboard data can be relied on may depend not only on the data's accuracy, the ability dimension of trustworthiness, but on their beliefs about the purposes the data serve, which correspond to benevolence and integrity {{mayer1995}}. A teacher who suspects that the same data will be used to judge them may be reluctant to engage with it, even if they believe it to be accurate. The survey trust measure did not directly capture this, as Section 3.3.4 noted, but the framework made it a priority for the Phase 2 interviews.

## 3.5 The Research Model

### 3.5.1 Overview

The research model tested in Phase 1 combines the core UTAUT relationships with trust in dashboard data, which enters the model in two roles: as an antecedent of behavioral intention and as a moderator of the relationship between intention and use. Figure 3.1 summarizes the model.

> **Figure 3.1** The trust-extended acceptance model. Performance expectancy → behavioral intention (H1, +); effort expectancy → behavioral intention (H2, +); trust in dashboard data → behavioral intention (H3, +); behavioral intention → use (H4, +); facilitating conditions → use (H5, +). Trust in dashboard data also points to the behavioral intention → use path (H6), indicating that the strength of that path depends on the level of trust. Use is measured by self-report and, for a subsample, validated against platform logins.

The model makes a specific theoretical claim. Teachers form intentions to use the dashboard on the basis of what they expect it to do for them, how easy they expect it to be and how far they trust its data. Whether those intentions become use depends on the strength of the intentions, on the resources available to act on them, and on trust, which affects not only whether teachers intend to use the dashboard but whether, when the moment comes, they rely on what it shows them. The sections below state the hypotheses and their rationale.

### 3.5.2 Antecedents of intention (H1–H3)

**H1. Performance expectancy is positively related to behavioral intention.** The relationship between expected usefulness and intention is the most consistently supported finding in the acceptance literature {{davis1989;venkatesh2003}}. For teachers, performance expectancy concerns whether the dashboard will help them identify students who need support, understand how their classes are progressing and make better instructional decisions. Teachers who expect such benefits should be more inclined to use the dashboard. H1 is not a new claim, but establishing that it holds is necessary before the extensions can be interpreted, and a weak or absent relationship would suggest that the dashboard context differs from those in which the model was developed.

**H2. Effort expectancy is positively related to behavioral intention.** Both TAM and UTAUT propose that ease of use increases intention, though the relationship is typically weaker than that for usefulness and has been found to diminish as users gain experience {{venkatesh2003}}. In the Northern Lakes districts, the dashboard had been available for several years, all teachers had received training, and most had used it at least occasionally. I therefore expected a positive but modest relationship between effort expectancy and intention. Ease of use is, in this context, close to a precondition rather than a strong motivator.

**H3. Trust in dashboard data is positively related to behavioral intention.** Where a system's purpose is to provide information on which users will base decisions, the expectation that the information can be relied on should increase willingness to use it {{mayer1995;lee2004}}. A teacher who doubts the dashboard's data has less reason to plan to consult it, because they cannot expect it to improve their decisions. H3 might be thought to overlap with H1, since a teacher who distrusts the data may also expect it to be less useful. The two are conceptually distinct, however. Performance expectancy concerns whether using the dashboard would, in principle, help; trust concerns whether the particular data it presents are dependable. A teacher can believe that a well-functioning dashboard would be valuable while doubting that this one's data are reliable. The measurement model in Chapter 5 tests whether the constructs are empirically distinct.

### 3.5.3 Antecedents of use (H4–H6)

**H4. Behavioral intention is positively related to use.** In both TAM and UTAUT, intention is the proximal determinant of use {{davis1989;venkatesh2003}}. H4 restates this relationship. The interest of this study lies less in whether the relationship is present than in how strong it is and under what conditions it is stronger or weaker. A modest relationship between intention and use would itself be evidence of the gap the study seeks to explain.

**H5. Facilitating conditions are positively related to use.** UTAUT proposes that facilitating conditions affect use directly, because they determine whether people are able to act on their intentions {{venkatesh2003}}. In this study, facilitating conditions were measured as teachers' perceptions of the resources and knowledge they had for using the dashboard, its compatibility with other systems they used and the availability of assistance. These correspond broadly to the hardware, training and support elements of first-order barriers in Ertmer's terms {{ertmer1999}}. As noted in Section 3.4.1, time, which Ertmer also identified as a first-order barrier, was not part of the measure. H5 therefore tests the effect of a specific and partial set of conditions.

**H6. Trust in dashboard data moderates the relationship between intention and use, such that the relationship is stronger when trust is high.** This is the study's central theoretical proposition. Its rationale draws on the distinction, present in the organizational model of trust, between trust as a willingness and the risk-taking behavior it enables {{mayer1995}}, and on the argument from the automation literature that trust guides reliance at the moment a decision is made {{lee2004}}.

The reasoning is as follows. A teacher's intention to use the dashboard is formed in general terms, at a distance from any particular decision. Use, however, takes place in particular moments: a planning period, a meeting, a moment of concern about a student. In each such moment, the teacher must decide whether to open the dashboard and whether to act on what it shows. If the teacher trusts the data, consulting the dashboard is a reasonable investment of scarce time, and the general intention is likely to be carried through. If the teacher does not trust the data, consulting the dashboard carries the additional cost of checking what it shows against other sources, and the teacher may reasonably decide that it is quicker to rely on their own records or knowledge. Low trust thus weakens the link between intention and action, not by eliminating intention but by making each act of use more costly and less rewarding. The algorithm aversion research suggests that this effect may be especially strong after a teacher has seen the dashboard make a visible error {{dietvorst2015}}.

H6 also implies a particular reading of the intention–use gap. If trust moderates the intention–use relationship, then among teachers with low trust, even strong intentions may translate only weakly into use. Such teachers would appear, in survey data, as having high intention but low use: willing, but stalled. The model therefore predicts not only an interaction effect but the existence of a group of teachers whose intentions and behaviors diverge, and it locates part of the explanation for that divergence in trust. The latent profile analysis reported in Chapter 5 was designed to detect such a group if it existed.

H3 and H6 together give trust a dual role. Trust is expected both to raise intention and to strengthen the link between intention and use. These roles are not redundant. A model containing only H3 would imply that trust matters only through intention, so that two teachers with equal intention would be expected to use the dashboard equally regardless of their trust. H6 rejects that implication. It allows for teachers who intend to use the dashboard for reasons other than trust, such as its perceived usefulness or organizational expectation, but who do not follow through because, when the moment comes, they do not rely on what it shows them.

### 3.5.4 Constructs not included in the model

Three decisions about what to leave out of the model require explanation.

The first concerns social influence. UTAUT includes social influence as a predictor of intention, particularly in mandatory settings {{venkatesh2003}}. In the Northern Lakes districts, every teacher had been given access to the dashboard by the district, and use was discussed in PLC meetings. Under these conditions, I expected teachers' perceptions of what colleagues and leaders thought they should do to overlap substantially with the organizational expectation that applied to everyone, and therefore to vary relatively little across teachers in ways that would explain differences in intention. More importantly, the study's interest lay in trust rather than in normative pressure, and including a further construct would have lengthened the survey and increased the complexity of the latent interaction model without serving the research questions. The decision is a delimitation rather than a claim that social influence is irrelevant. The organizational context in which normative pressure operates, including the role of leaders and meetings, was explored in Phase 2, and Chapter 6 shows that it mattered, though in ways a social influence scale might not have captured.

The second concerns the UTAUT moderators of gender, age, experience and voluntariness. Gender and teaching experience were measured and are reported descriptively in Chapter 5, but they were not modeled as moderators of the structural paths. Voluntariness was not measured, because the formal conditions of use were the same across districts. Testing multiple moderators in addition to the latent interaction for H6 would have required a larger sample than was available and would have increased the risk of chance findings. The study therefore concentrated its analytic power on the one moderation hypothesis that was theoretically central.

The third concerns time. With hindsight, the most consequential omission from the model was any measure of the time available to teachers for data work. I did not include such a measure because the UTAUT facilitating conditions scale, which I adapted closely to preserve comparability, does not contain one, and because time did not emerge as a central concern in the pilot. Phase 2 showed that it was among the most important conditions shaping use. I do not revise the model retrospectively, because doing so would misrepresent what was tested. Instead, Chapter 7 treats the absence of time from the facilitating conditions measure as a point of discordance between the phases, and Chapter 8 recommends its inclusion in future research.

### 3.5.5 Measuring use

A final element of the framework concerns the outcome variable. Acceptance studies typically measure use through self-report, and self-reports of technology use are known to be imperfect indicators of behavior. Platform logs offer an alternative, but logs measure only what the platform records, typically logins, page views and clicks, and they cannot capture what users do with information after they leave the platform. Neither measure, on its own, captures use as teachers understand it.

The study responded to this problem in two ways. In Phase 1, use was measured through self-reported frequency of six specific dashboard actions, rather than a single global item, and self-reports were compared with logged logins for teachers who consented to linkage. In Phase 2, teachers were asked to describe how they used dashboard information, including uses that took place outside the dashboard. This combination reflects a stance, consistent with the pragmatist position of the study, that "use" is a construct whose meaning must be established empirically rather than assumed. The third contribution of the study, set out in Chapter 1, arises from this stance.

## 3.6 The Framework and the Qualitative Phase

The research model was designed for testing. Phase 2, by contrast, was designed to explain the results of that test, and the framework played a different role there. In keeping with the explanatory sequential logic, the interview protocol was developed after the Phase 1 results were known, and it focused on the relationships and groups that most needed explaining: the intention–use gap, the role of trust and the profile of willing but stalled teachers {{ivankova2006}}. The frameworks introduced in Section 3.4 provided sensitizing concepts for this work. Ertmer's barriers suggested that I ask about time and structure as well as about beliefs. Data literacy suggested that I ask what teachers did after looking at the dashboard. The trust literature suggested that I ask about moments when trust changed. The literature on leadership and ethics suggested that I ask who teachers thought the data were for.

At the same time, the framework was deliberately held open. The interview questions began with teachers' own descriptions of their practice before introducing the constructs of the model, and the thematic analysis was conducted inductively before themes were compared with the framework {{braun2006}}. This sequence was intended to allow teachers' accounts to challenge the model, not merely to illustrate it. Several of the most important findings, including the significance of workarounds as a form of use and the way teachers bound timeliness and fit into their judgments of trust, were not anticipated by the framework and emerged only because the analysis did not begin from it.

The relationship between the model and the qualitative findings is therefore abductive in Morgan's sense {{morgan2007}}. The quantitative results raised questions the model could not answer on its own; the qualitative findings offered explanations; and those explanations, in turn, suggest revisions to how the constructs of the model should be specified and measured. The integration procedures that formalize this movement, including the joint display and the technique of following a thread, are described in Chapter 4 {{fetters2013;ocathain2010}}.

## 3.7 Summary

This chapter has built the theoretical framework for the study. Starting from the Technology Acceptance Model and UTAUT, it identified three limitations of acceptance models for explaining teachers' use of dashboards: their thin account of the intention–use link, their lack of a construct for belief in a system's outputs, and their abstraction from the conditions of teachers' work. It drew on the organizational and human factors literatures to define trust in dashboard data as a willingness to rely on the information the dashboard presents, and argued that trust should be specified with the data as its object. It introduced frameworks from educational research, concerning barriers to technology integration, the diffusion of innovations, data literacy and the organizational and ethical conditions of data use, that explain why intentions might not carry through.

From these elements, the chapter assembled a trust-extended acceptance model with six hypotheses. Four restate core UTAUT relationships in the dashboard context. Two are extensions: trust as an antecedent of intention (H3), and trust as a moderator of the intention–use relationship (H6). The latter is the study's central theoretical proposition, and it predicts the existence of a group of teachers whose intentions are not carried through into use. The chapter also explained why social influence and the UTAUT moderators were not modeled, acknowledged that the facilitating conditions measure did not include time, and set out how the framework guided but did not constrain the qualitative phase.

Chapter 4 now describes how this framework was put to work: the methodology through which the model was tested on survey data from 412 teachers and through which 24 of those teachers were selected and interviewed to explain the results.

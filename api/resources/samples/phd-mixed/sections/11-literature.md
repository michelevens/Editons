# Chapter 2: Literature Review

## 2.1 Introduction and scope of the review

This chapter reviews the scholarship that bears on a deceptively simple question: why do secondary teachers who have access to a learning analytics dashboard, and who say they intend to use it, so often use it only occasionally, superficially, or not at all? Chapter 1 set out the problem in practical terms. Districts in the Northern Lakes region, like many others, have bought a commercial dashboard on the expectation that teachers will consult it routinely, interpret what they see, and adjust their teaching in response. Use in practice has been shallow and uneven. The purpose of this chapter is to show what existing research already explains about that pattern, where its explanations run out, and why the combination of a trust-extended acceptance model and teachers' own accounts is a reasonable way to close the remaining gaps.

The review is organized by argument rather than by author or by chronology. It makes five moves. Section 2.2 examines learning analytics as a field and dashboards as its most visible product for teachers, arguing that the field's founding promise rested on an assumption about use that has rarely been tested in authentic school settings. Section 2.3 turns to the older and larger literature on teachers' use of data, which shows that data use is a professional and organizational practice rather than a matter of access, and that it depends on skills, time, and leadership as much as on tools. Section 2.4 reviews the technology acceptance tradition, from the Technology Acceptance Model to the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology, and argues that these models explain intention well but explain the step from intention to behavior much less well. Section 2.5 develops the construct at the center of this study, trust, drawing on organizational and human factors research to argue that trust in the data a tool presents is distinct from beliefs about the tool's usefulness and may govern whether intention becomes action. Section 2.6 considers the ethical and political dimensions of analytics in schools, in particular the possibility that teachers read dashboards as instruments of surveillance. Two shorter sections then address how "use" has been measured (Section 2.7) and how the field has studied these questions methodologically (Section 2.8). Section 2.9 draws the threads together into a set of specific gaps, and Section 2.10 summarizes the chapter and hands on to Chapter 3, where the theoretical framework and hypotheses are specified formally.

Three boundaries of scope should be stated at the outset. First, the review concerns dashboards designed for teachers, not student-facing dashboards or institutional analytics used by administrators, although the latter matter where they shape how teachers perceive the tools they are given. Second, the review concentrates on secondary schooling but draws on higher education research where school-level evidence is thin, which is often. Where it does so, the transfer is flagged and its limits noted. Third, the review is selective. It is built around a fixed body of sources that speak directly to the study's constructs and design, and it does not attempt a systematic review of learning analytics, which others have undertaken {{schwendimann2017}}. Readers will notice that some claims are presented as my own argument rather than as established findings; where the literature is silent, I have tried to say so rather than to fill the silence.

## 2.2 Learning analytics and the promise of the dashboard

### 2.2.1 The emergence of learning analytics

Learning analytics emerged as a named field in the early 2010s, in the wake of rapid growth in the digital traces that students leave as they work in learning management systems, online assessment platforms, and other educational software. {{siemens2011|n}} offered one of the earliest and most widely circulated framings. They described analytics as a way to see through the "fog" of educational decision making, arguing that institutions had long made consequential choices about teaching and support with little systematic evidence, and that the data now being generated could change this. Importantly, they distinguished learning analytics, concerned with learners and the learning process and oriented toward teachers and students, from academic analytics, concerned with institutional efficiency and oriented toward administrators and policy makers. That distinction is directly relevant here, because a dashboard bought by a district and installed in classrooms can sit uncomfortably across it: it is presented to teachers as a learning tool while being procured, configured, and sometimes monitored as an institutional one.

The early framing was optimistic, and in hindsight it carried an implicit theory of change. Data would be collected, analyzed, and presented; the people presented with it would understand it; understanding would lead to better decisions; and better decisions would improve learning. Each link in that chain is an assumption. The first two links, collection and analysis, attracted most of the field's early technical energy. The later links, concerning what people do with what they are shown, received less attention, and it is in those later links that the problem addressed by this dissertation lies.

A corrective to the field's early emphasis came from within. {{gasevic2015|n}} argued that learning analytics had in some respects lost sight of learning. Analytics built without grounding in learning theory or in the instructional design of the course from which the data came, they suggested, risk producing indicators that are easy to compute but hard to interpret, and models that generalize poorly across contexts. Their argument was aimed largely at predictive models in higher education, but its implications for teacher dashboards are clear. A dashboard that displays login counts, time on task, or completion rates without reference to what a teacher is actually trying to teach invites precisely the question that recurs in this study's interviews: what is this number telling me that I can act on? If indicators are not anchored in pedagogy, the burden of making them meaningful falls on the teacher, and it is reasonable to expect that some teachers will decline that burden.

### 2.2.2 What dashboards are meant to do

Dashboards are the form in which most teachers meet learning analytics. A dashboard aggregates indicators about students, groups, or tasks and displays them visually, typically through charts, color-coded tables, and alerts. {{verbert2013|n}} proposed a process model that remains a useful way of thinking about what dashboards are intended to accomplish. In their account, a dashboard first supports awareness, making visible data that would otherwise go unseen. Awareness can prompt reflection, in which the user asks questions of the data. Reflection supports sensemaking, in which the user forms an interpretation and links it to what they know. Only after sensemaking does the model reach impact, in which the user changes behavior in some way that might, eventually, affect learning.

The value of this model for the present study is that it disaggregates "use." A teacher who opens the dashboard, glances at a summary, and closes it has reached awareness at most. A teacher who notices a cluster of students struggling with a particular assessment item, wonders why, checks the item, and reteaches the concept the following lesson has traveled the whole path. Survey items and platform logs that treat these two teachers as equivalent users, differing only in frequency, collapse a distinction that matters. Section 2.7 returns to this measurement problem. For now, the point is that the dashboard literature itself implies that use is staged and that most of the value lies in the later stages, which are also the stages least visible to the system.

The model also implies something about where use can break down. A dashboard can fail at the first stage if teachers do not open it, at the second if what they see does not provoke questions, at the third if they cannot connect what they see to their own knowledge of students, and at the fourth if they cannot see what to do differently. These are different failures with different remedies. The acceptance models reviewed in Section 2.4 are mostly concerned with whether people engage with a system at all; the dashboard literature points toward the quality and depth of engagement. One aim of this study is to bring the two together, using the quantitative model to account for engagement and the qualitative phase to examine where along the path from awareness to impact teachers stop.

### 2.2.3 The evidence on dashboards in practice

Given the investment that schools and universities have made in dashboards, one might expect a substantial body of evidence on whether they change teaching and learning. The evidence is thinner than the investment. {{schwendimann2017|n}} conducted a systematic review of learning dashboard research and found that much of it concerned the design and technical development of dashboards, that evaluations were frequently limited to perceived usefulness or usability, and that relatively few studies had examined effects on learning in authentic settings over meaningful periods. Their review also noted that dashboards drew on a fairly narrow range of data sources, often activity logs from a single platform, and that the indicators they displayed were not always justified in terms of what they meant for learning.

Three implications follow for the present study. First, the field's evidence base for the proposition that teachers will use dashboards productively, once given them, is weak; districts that purchase dashboards are in effect acting on the early promise rather than on demonstrated effects. Second, where evaluations rely on perceived usefulness, they are measuring something close to the acceptance constructs reviewed in Section 2.4. A teacher may find a dashboard useful in principle and still not use it, which means that positive evaluations of perceived usefulness may coexist with low actual use, exactly the pattern this study investigates. Third, the reliance on single-platform activity logs is mirrored in how use is often measured: by logins. If teachers' meaningful engagement with dashboard information happens partly outside the platform, as when they export data to a spreadsheet or print a summary for a meeting, then log-based measures will undercount use. I return to this possibility in Section 2.7, and it becomes one of the study's central findings in Chapters 6 and 7.

### 2.2.4 From higher education to the secondary classroom

Much of the early work in learning analytics, including the framing offered by {{siemens2011|n}} and the critique of {{gasevic2015|n}}, was rooted in higher education, where online learning environments generate dense activity data and where analytics were often aimed at student retention. The transfer of these ideas to secondary schools is not straightforward, and several differences deserve attention because they shape what a teacher dashboard can plausibly offer.

First, the data are different. In many secondary classrooms, a large share of learning activity happens offline, in discussion, practical work, and paper-based tasks, so the digital trace on which a dashboard draws is a partial record of what students do. A dashboard built on assessment uploads and platform activity therefore sees only the portion of learning that passes through the platform. Second, the relationship between teacher and student is different. Secondary teachers typically see the same students several times a week and form detailed impressions of them, whereas instructors in large university courses may have little direct knowledge of individual students. The informational gap that a dashboard is meant to fill is correspondingly smaller in a secondary classroom, which raises the bar for relative advantage in the sense described in Section 2.3.5. Third, the organizational setting is different. Secondary teachers work within departmental and school structures, with collaborative planning meetings and accountability systems that university instructors often do not share, so dashboard use is more likely to be shaped by meeting agendas and leadership expectations.

None of these differences means that dashboards cannot serve secondary teachers well. They do mean that findings and design assumptions from higher education should not be carried over uncritically, and that the conditions examined in the following sections, data literacy, leadership, time, and trust, may weigh more heavily in schools than the early literature anticipated. Where this chapter draws on higher education research, it does so with these differences in mind.

The dashboard literature, then, provides a vocabulary for what use is supposed to look like and a sobering assessment of how little is known about whether it happens. It does not, on its own, explain why teachers engage or fail to engage. For that, the review turns to research on teachers and data more broadly.

## 2.3 Teachers and data: practice, literacy and organization

### 2.3.1 Data use as professional practice

Long before learning analytics acquired its name, school systems had been encouraging teachers to use data to inform instruction. Assessment results, attendance records, and grades have been the raw material of "data-driven decision making" initiatives for decades, and the dashboards that districts now purchase are in many respects a new interface on an old aspiration. The research on data use in schools is therefore an essential context for understanding dashboard use, and it carries a lesson that the acceptance literature tends to underplay: using data is a practice, not an event. It involves selecting data, interpreting them in light of knowledge about students, curriculum, and pedagogy, deciding what to do, and doing it, often in collaboration with colleagues and under the direction or influence of school leaders.

Seen this way, a dashboard is one resource within a practice, not the practice itself. A teacher who is skilled at using data may make limited use of a particular dashboard because other sources, such as their own gradebook, classroom observations, or conversations with students, serve the same purpose better. Conversely, a teacher with little experience of systematic data use may log in frequently but derive little from the visit. This distinction matters for interpreting the quantitative results in Chapter 5, where self-reported use and logged use are related but far from identical, and for interpreting teachers' accounts in Chapter 6, where several describe the dashboard as an addition to practices they already had rather than a foundation for new ones.

### 2.3.2 Data literacy for teaching

If data use is a practice, then it requires competence, and the most developed account of that competence is the framework of data literacy for teaching set out by {{mandinach2016|n}}. They argued that data literacy for teachers is not the same as general statistical or assessment literacy. It is the ability to transform information into actionable instructional knowledge and practices, which requires integrating data skills with content knowledge, pedagogical content knowledge, and knowledge of students and context. Their framework distinguishes components such as identifying problems and framing questions, using data, transforming data into information, transforming information into a decision, and evaluating outcomes, and it treats dispositions, including beliefs about the value of data and an inquiry orientation, as part of literacy rather than separate from it.

Two features of this framework are particularly important for this study. The first is its emphasis on transformation into action. Mandinach and Gummer locate the hardest part of data use not in reading a chart but in moving from what the data show to what a teacher should do. This maps closely onto the final stages of the process model of {{verbert2013|n}}, and it anticipates a theme that teachers in this study raised repeatedly: dashboards show what is happening but not what to do about it. A dashboard that stops at display leaves the most demanding step to teachers. Where teachers' data literacy is strong, they may manage it; where it is not, or where time is short, the dashboard is likely to be consulted and then set aside.

The second feature is the framework's inclusion of contextual knowledge. Teachers know things about their students that are not in the data: who was absent with illness, who has a difficult situation at home, who rushes tests but understands the material. This knowledge allows teachers to interpret indicators, but it also allows them to dismiss them. When a dashboard flags as at risk a student the teacher already knows to be struggling, the flag is redundant; when it flags a student the teacher knows to be doing well, the flag is wrong in the teacher's eyes. Either experience can lower the perceived value of the tool, and the second can lower trust in it. The data literacy literature therefore points toward the construct this study adds to the acceptance model: the degree to which teachers believe the data are accurate and meaningful, which I treat in Section 2.5 under the heading of trust.

It is worth noting what the data literacy literature does not say. It does not predict that teachers with higher data literacy will use any particular tool more. A highly data-literate teacher might be the most critical user of a poorly designed dashboard. Data literacy is better understood as a condition that shapes the quality of use and the standards by which a tool is judged than as a simple predictor of frequency. In this study, data literacy was not measured directly in the survey, a limitation discussed in Chapter 8; it enters the analysis principally through teachers' accounts in Phase 2.

### 2.3.3 Leadership, collaboration and the organizational conditions of data use

Teachers do not use data in isolation. They work in schools whose leaders set expectations, allocate time, and model how data should be discussed. {{datnow2014|n}} examined how school and district leaders shape data use and argued that the culture surrounding data matters at least as much as the data themselves. In their account, leaders who build productive data use do several things: they establish a shared purpose for data use focused on improving instruction rather than on compliance or blame, they create structured time for teachers to examine data together, they build capacity through professional learning, and they cultivate trust among staff so that data can be discussed openly. Where these conditions are absent, data use tends to become a matter of ritual compliance, with data reviewed because a meeting agenda requires it rather than because teachers find it useful.

This literature is important for the present study in two ways. First, it identifies time as a structural condition rather than an individual attribute. Teachers in schools that set aside time for collaborative data inquiry have opportunities to use dashboards that teachers in other schools do not, regardless of their attitudes. The facilitating conditions construct in the acceptance models (Section 2.4) is supposed to capture this kind of structural support, but as I argue there, the way it is usually measured emphasizes hardware, training, and technical help rather than protected time. The organizational literature suggests that this emphasis may miss the condition that matters most.

Second, the leadership literature introduces the question of purpose. Data can be used to help teachers improve their teaching, but the same data can be used to evaluate, rank, or sanction teachers. {{datnow2014|n}} emphasized that when teachers perceive data use as evaluative, they become guarded and the collaborative examination of evidence becomes harder. This is a matter of organizational culture, but it is also, I argue in Section 2.5, a matter of trust. A teacher's willingness to engage with a dashboard may depend not only on whether the data are accurate but also on who else is looking at them and for what purpose. Section 2.6 takes up this concern in relation to the ethics of analytics.

### 2.3.4 Barriers to technology integration

A further strand of research, concerned with teachers' integration of technology generally rather than data specifically, offers a complementary lens. {{ertmer1999|n}} distinguished between first-order and second-order barriers to technology integration. First-order barriers are external to the teacher: lack of access to equipment, insufficient time, inadequate training, and limited technical support. Second-order barriers are internal: teachers' beliefs about teaching and learning, their established classroom practices, and their views about the role of technology. Ertmer argued that first-order barriers, while real, are in principle easier to identify and remove, whereas second-order barriers are more deeply rooted and require change in beliefs, and that removing first-order barriers does not by itself produce integration if second-order barriers remain.

The distinction is useful here because it cuts across the constructs in the acceptance models in an instructive way. Facilitating conditions, as typically measured, correspond to some first-order barriers, namely access, training, and support. Performance expectancy and trust are closer to second-order barriers, as they concern beliefs about the value and reliability of the tool. Yet time, which Ertmer classified as a first-order barrier, is seldom captured in acceptance instruments. The "Willing but stalled" teachers identified in Chapter 5 are, in Ertmer's terms, teachers whose second-order barriers appear largely overcome, since they believe the dashboard could help and intend to use it, but who remain blocked by a first-order barrier that the survey did not measure. This interpretation is developed in Chapter 7; its foundation is laid here.

Ertmer's framework also cautions against treating non-use as resistance. Much writing on teacher technology adoption, especially in policy contexts, frames teachers who do not use a tool as reluctant or resistant. The barrier perspective reframes non-use as the product of conditions, some external and some internal, and invites the researcher to ask what conditions would need to change. That stance informs the qualitative phase of this study, which asked teachers to explain their use and non-use rather than to justify it.

### 2.3.5 Diffusion, compatibility and reinvention

The diffusion of innovations tradition provides one further lens on teachers' engagement with dashboards. {{rogers2003|n}} identified a set of perceived attributes of innovations that influence their rate of adoption: relative advantage over existing practice, compatibility with existing values, experiences, and needs, complexity, trialability, and observability. Two of these are especially pertinent. Relative advantage asks not whether a dashboard is useful in the abstract but whether it is more useful than what the teacher already does. A teacher with a well-organized gradebook and good knowledge of students may see little relative advantage in a dashboard that reports the same information a week later. Compatibility asks whether the dashboard fits the teacher's existing ways of working and the rhythm of their week. A tool that requires a block of uninterrupted time in a working week that does not contain one is incompatible in a practical sense, whatever its merits.

Rogers also described reinvention, the degree to which an innovation is changed or modified by users in the process of adoption. Reinvention is often treated by system designers as deviation, but Rogers presented it as a common and often productive feature of adoption, which can increase the sustainability of an innovation by adapting it to local needs. This idea is important for the present study because teachers in Phase 2 described a range of adaptations, exporting dashboard data to spreadsheets, taking screenshots to share with parents, and combining dashboard views with their own records, that amount to reinvention. Such practices represent use, but they are use that platform logs do not register, a point that becomes central to the methodological contribution set out in Chapters 7 and 8. Rogers's adopter categories, which classify individuals by the relative timing of adoption, are less useful here, because the dashboard was introduced district-wide rather than adopted voluntarily; the latent profiles reported in Chapter 5 describe patterns of current engagement, not timing of adoption, and should not be read as adopter categories.

Taken together, the research on teachers' data use, data literacy, organizational conditions, technology barriers, and diffusion establishes that dashboard use is shaped by competence, time, culture, purpose, fit, and adaptation. It does not, however, provide a parsimonious model that can be tested across several hundred teachers to estimate the relative weight of these influences. For that, the review turns to the technology acceptance tradition.

## 2.4 Explaining acceptance: from TAM to UTAUT

### 2.4.1 The Technology Acceptance Model

The Technology Acceptance Model, introduced by {{davis1989|n}}, has been one of the most widely applied frameworks for explaining why people adopt or reject information technology. Davis proposed that two beliefs are central. Perceived usefulness is the degree to which a person believes that using a system would enhance their job performance. Perceived ease of use is the degree to which a person believes that using the system would be free of effort. Drawing on the theory of reasoned action, the model posits that these beliefs shape attitudes and intentions, and that intention in turn predicts actual use. In Davis's original studies, perceived usefulness was the stronger correlate of use, and ease of use appeared to operate partly through usefulness: a system that is easy to use but does nothing valuable is unlikely to be adopted, whereas users will tolerate some difficulty in a system they find genuinely useful.

The model's appeal lies in its parsimony. Two beliefs, measured with a handful of items each, account for a meaningful share of variation in intention across many technologies and settings. That parsimony made TAM attractive to researchers in education, where it has been applied to learning management systems, interactive whiteboards, and many other tools. It also makes the model a natural starting point for a study of dashboards, since a district that purchases a dashboard implicitly assumes that teachers will use it if they find it useful and not too difficult.

Parsimony, however, has costs. TAM says little about the conditions under which beliefs are formed or about what happens between intention and behavior. It treats the system as the object of belief and abstracts away from the organizational setting in which the system is used. And it was developed in workplaces where use was often voluntary and where the system's outputs were relatively well defined. Dashboards in schools differ on each count: their use is formally encouraged and sometimes expected, their outputs are interpretations of student data rather than completed transactions, and the setting in which they are used, a school week with very little discretionary time, constrains behavior in ways that beliefs may not overcome.

### 2.4.2 The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology

Over the decade following Davis's work, a number of competing models of technology acceptance were proposed. {{venkatesh2003|n}} reviewed eight of these, compared them empirically, and integrated their common elements into the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). UTAUT proposes four core determinants. Performance expectancy, the degree to which a person believes that using the system will help them attain gains in job performance, incorporates perceived usefulness and related constructs from other models. Effort expectancy, the degree of ease associated with using the system, incorporates perceived ease of use. Social influence is the degree to which a person perceives that important others believe they should use the system. Facilitating conditions refer to the degree to which a person believes that an organizational and technical infrastructure exists to support use. The model also specifies moderators, namely gender, age, experience, and voluntariness of use, that condition the strength of these relationships.

A structurally important feature of UTAUT is that its determinants do not all operate in the same place. Performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social influence are theorized as determinants of behavioral intention. Facilitating conditions, in contrast, are theorized as a direct determinant of use behavior, on the reasoning that once performance and effort expectancies are accounted for, the presence of support affects whether a person can act on an intention rather than whether they form one. This placement matters a great deal for the present study, because it builds into UTAUT an acknowledgment that intention is not sufficient for use. Some conditions external to the user's beliefs must be in place. UTAUT thus already contains the seed of an explanation for why intention does not always translate into use, although it locates that explanation entirely in infrastructure and support.

In this study, I retain performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and facilitating conditions from UTAUT and do not include social influence as a separate construct. The reasons are set out in Chapter 3 and concern both the mandated character of dashboard provision in the participating districts and the decision to model the purpose and governance of data use through teachers' accounts rather than through a normative-pressure construct. That decision has a cost, discussed in Chapter 8, since social influence may capture some of the leadership dynamics described in Section 2.3.3.

### 2.4.3 Acceptance models in education

The acceptance models were developed largely in corporate settings, and their transfer to education has been extensive but uneven. In education research, performance expectancy or perceived usefulness has generally emerged as a strong predictor of teachers' intentions to use new tools, consistent with the original findings. The role of effort expectancy has been more variable, a pattern that the original UTAUT work would anticipate, since effort expectancy was found to matter most in the early stages of experience with a system and to diminish as users became familiar with it {{venkatesh2003}}. Teachers in the Northern Lakes districts had had access to the district dashboard for some time before the survey, which suggests that effort expectancy might be a relatively weak predictor in this sample. This expectation is formalized in Chapter 3 as hypothesis H2, and the results reported in Chapter 5 are consistent with it: the path from effort expectancy to intention was positive but small.

Two features of educational settings complicate the application of acceptance models. The first is voluntariness. Teachers are rarely ordered to log into a dashboard a given number of times per week, but they are often expected to bring dashboard data to meetings or to use it in planning. Use is therefore neither fully voluntary nor fully mandated, and the line between the two varies by school and leader. The second is the nature of the output. In a business setting, the output of an information system is often a completed task: an order placed, a report generated. The output of a dashboard is information that the teacher must interpret and act upon. The "performance" in performance expectancy is therefore indirect, mediated by the teacher's own judgment and by the quality of the data. This indirection is the reason why, I argue, beliefs about the data themselves need to enter the model alongside beliefs about the tool.

### 2.4.4 The intention–behavior gap

The central limitation of acceptance models for the problem this study addresses is that they are much better at explaining intention than at explaining behavior. The models' own logic treats intention as the proximal cause of use, and in many studies intention does predict use. But the relationship is typically far from perfect, and the variance in use explained by intention and facilitating conditions is usually considerably smaller than the variance in intention explained by the belief constructs. Put plainly, acceptance models tell us a good deal about who means to use a system and rather less about who actually does.

There are several reasons why this gap might arise, and they are not mutually exclusive. Some concern measurement. Intention is typically measured at the same time as the belief constructs, using the same method, which inflates their association relative to the association between intention and a behavior measured differently or later, a point taken up in Section 2.7. Self-reported use may also be imperfectly related to actual use. Other reasons are substantive. Intentions may be formed in good faith but overridden by competing demands on time. They may be conditional, in the sense that a person intends to use a system if circumstances allow, and circumstances may rarely allow. Or the first experience of acting on an intention may change the beliefs that produced it. A teacher who intends to use a dashboard, opens it, and finds that the data are out of date or plainly wrong about a student they know well may revise both their belief in its usefulness and their willingness to rely on it.

This last possibility points directly to the role of trust. If the information a system presents is not believed, the intention to use the system can be sincere and still not produce sustained use. Acceptance models do not include a construct that captures the user's belief in the reliability of the system's outputs, because in the settings where they were developed that reliability could largely be assumed. In the case of dashboards, which present inferences about students derived from data of variable quality and timeliness, it cannot. The next section develops this argument.

Before doing so, it is worth acknowledging that the acceptance tradition has itself recognized trust as a potential extension, particularly in research on online commerce and automated systems, where users must rely on systems whose inner workings they cannot inspect [source needed]. What is less common is the treatment of trust as a moderator of the intention–use relationship rather than as an additional antecedent of intention. The distinction matters theoretically. If trust is simply another belief that shapes intention, then teachers with low trust should report low intention, and the "willing but stalled" pattern of high intention with low use would remain unexplained. If trust also conditions whether intention becomes action, then teachers can intend to use a dashboard and nonetheless hold back from acting on what it shows them because they do not trust the data enough to rely on it. The present study tests both roles: trust as an antecedent of intention (H3) and as a moderator of the intention–use link (H6). The rationale for that specification is developed in Chapter 3.

### 2.4.5 Facilitating conditions reconsidered

The placement of facilitating conditions as a direct determinant of use offers a partial answer to the intention–behavior gap, but the content of the construct limits that answer. In UTAUT and the instruments derived from it, facilitating conditions are usually measured through items about whether the user has the resources and knowledge necessary to use the system, whether the system is compatible with other systems they use, and whether a specific person or group is available for assistance. The items adapted for the present survey, described in Chapter 4 and reproduced in Appendix A, follow this pattern and ask about devices, training, and technical help.

The literature reviewed in Section 2.3 suggests that this content is incomplete for teachers. The organizational research of {{datnow2014|n}} identifies structured, protected time for data inquiry as a key condition; the barrier framework of {{ertmer1999|n}} lists time among the first-order barriers; and the compatibility attribute described by {{rogers2003|n}} implies that a tool's fit with the rhythm of the working week affects its adoption. None of this is captured by an item asking whether a teacher has the device and training needed to use the dashboard. It is possible, then, that the facilitating conditions construct as conventionally measured understates the structural constraints on teachers' use, and that some of the unexplained variance in use reflects the absence of time rather than the absence of will. This possibility cannot be tested directly with the survey data, which is one reason the study's design includes a qualitative phase capable of identifying conditions the instrument did not anticipate. Chapter 7 reports that this is precisely what occurred.

### 2.4.6 What the acceptance tradition contributes and what it leaves open

The acceptance models make three contributions to this study. They supply a well-tested set of constructs and measures that allow teachers' beliefs to be estimated reliably across a large sample. They provide a structural logic, beliefs to intention to use, against which deviations can be identified. And through UTAUT's placement of facilitating conditions, they acknowledge that intention alone does not determine behavior. What they leave open is why intention so often fails to produce behavior, how the reliability of a system's outputs bears on its use, and how organizational purpose and time shape whether teachers act. These questions motivate the extension of the model with trust and the use of a qualitative phase to explain the quantitative relationships, as set out in the study's research questions in Chapter 1.

## 2.5 Trust in data, systems and automation

### 2.5.1 Why trust, and trust in what?

The previous section argued that acceptance models lack a construct capturing whether users believe the outputs of a system. This section develops trust as that construct. Before reviewing the relevant literature, it is necessary to be precise about the object of trust, because "trust in the dashboard" can mean several different things. A teacher might trust the software, in the sense of expecting it to load and function. A teacher might trust the vendor or the district that procured it. A teacher might trust the data the dashboard displays, believing them to be accurate, current, and meaningful. And a teacher might trust the people who also have access to the dashboard, believing that they will use what they see fairly. These objects are related, but they are not the same, and conflating them risks a construct that measures everything and explains nothing.

In this study, the survey construct is trust in dashboard data: the extent to which teachers believe that the information the dashboard presents about their students is accurate, current, and dependable enough to act upon. This choice was made because the data are what teachers are being asked to rely on when they use a dashboard to inform instruction, and because the literature on data use (Section 2.3) consistently identifies data quality as a condition of use. The qualitative phase, however, was designed to allow teachers to describe trust in their own terms, and it is there that other objects of trust, notably the purposes for which data are used by others, emerge as important. Chapter 7 argues that the survey construct captured one part of a broader phenomenon.

### 2.5.2 Organizational trust: ability, benevolence and integrity

The most influential definition of trust in organizational research is that of {{mayer1995|n}}, who defined trust as the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party, based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party. Two features of this definition are central. First, trust involves vulnerability: it matters only when the trustor has something at stake and cannot fully protect against the other party's failure. Second, trust is distinguished from the risk-taking behavior that may follow from it. Trust is a willingness; acting on that willingness is a separate step, which occurs when trust exceeds the perceived risk of the situation.

Mayer and colleagues also proposed that trust rests on three perceived characteristics of the trustee. Ability is the set of skills and competencies that enable the trustee to perform in a specific domain. Benevolence is the extent to which the trustee is believed to want to do good to the trustor, apart from self-interested motives. Integrity is the trustor's perception that the trustee adheres to a set of principles the trustor finds acceptable. They further identified the trustor's general propensity to trust as an influence on trust, particularly when little is known about the trustee.

Although this model was developed for trust between people in organizations, it is illuminating when applied to dashboards and the systems around them. The ability dimension maps onto the accuracy and timeliness of the data: does the dashboard competently represent what is happening with students? Benevolence and integrity map less obviously onto software, but they map clearly onto the institution that deploys it. Teachers asking whether the district dashboard serves them or serves those who evaluate them are, in effect, asking about the benevolence and integrity of the organization behind it. The distinction between trust and risk-taking, meanwhile, is directly relevant to the intention–use gap. A teacher may hold a moderate level of trust in the data and still not act on them, if the perceived risk of acting on wrong information, such as intervening with a student who does not need it or overlooking one who does, is judged to exceed that trust. The model thus offers a theoretical reason to expect that trust conditions the step from intention to behavior, since behavior involves risk-taking in a way that intention does not.

The trust items in this study's survey were adapted in part from this tradition, as described in Chapter 4. They focus on the ability dimension, as applied to data: accuracy, currency, and dependability. That focus was deliberate, since the ability dimension is the one most directly tied to the dashboard as an information source. But it leaves the benevolence and integrity dimensions, which concern the purposes of the institution, to be explored qualitatively. Chapter 6 reports that teachers raised these dimensions without prompting.

### 2.5.3 Trust in automation and appropriate reliance

A second body of work on trust comes from human factors research on automation. {{lee2004|n}} reviewed this research and proposed an account of trust in automation that has shaped thinking about how people rely on algorithmic and automated systems. They treated trust as an attitude that guides reliance when complexity and unanticipated situations make a complete understanding of the automation impractical. Their central concern was not that people should trust automation more but that they should trust it appropriately. Trust that exceeds the system's actual capabilities leads to misuse, in which people rely on automation when they should not; trust that falls short of those capabilities leads to disuse, in which people reject automation that would have helped them. The goal of design, on this view, is calibrated trust: trust that matches the true reliability of the system, and that is specific enough to distinguish the conditions under which the system can and cannot be relied upon.

Two elements of this account are especially relevant. The first is calibration. The question for dashboards is not simply whether teachers trust the data more or less, but whether their trust corresponds to how reliable the data actually are. Teachers with low trust in a dashboard that is in fact reliable are disusing a helpful tool; teachers with high trust in a dashboard that contains errors may misuse it. This study measures teachers' trust but cannot independently measure the dashboard's reliability, so it cannot assess calibration directly, a limitation acknowledged in Chapter 8. However, teachers' accounts in Chapter 6 frequently refer to specific features of the data, such as lags between assessment and display or mismatches with the gradebook, which suggests that at least some low trust reflects real limitations of the data rather than generalized skepticism.

The second element is the bases of trust. {{lee2004|n}} drew on earlier work to identify three types of information on which trust in automation can be based: performance, meaning what the automation does and how reliably it does it; process, meaning how it operates and whether its mechanisms are appropriate; and purpose, meaning why it was developed and whether it is being used within the designer's intent. This tripartite structure resonates closely with the account of trust that emerged from teachers' interviews in this study, in which trust depended on whether the data were timely, whether they were accurate, and whether the purpose for which they were collected and viewed was one teachers could accept. The parallel was not anticipated in the design of the interview protocol, which asked open questions about trust, and I treat the correspondence in Chapter 7 as a point of convergence between the qualitative findings and an existing theoretical framework rather than as a confirmation of it. The study's second contribution, a three-part account of trust in educational data, builds on this convergence while adapting it to the specific conditions of school data.

### 2.5.4 How trust is lost: the asymmetry of error

If trust governs reliance, it is important to understand how trust changes. The automation literature suggests that trust is dynamic, developing through experience with a system and responding to its successes and failures. A particularly relevant finding concerns how people respond to errors made by algorithms. {{dietvorst2015|n}} conducted a series of experiments in which participants could choose whether to rely on a statistical algorithm or on human judgment, including their own, to make forecasts. Participants who had seen the algorithm perform were less likely to choose it than those who had not, even when they had also seen that it outperformed the human forecaster. Seeing the algorithm err reduced confidence in it more than seeing a human err reduced confidence in the human. The authors termed this pattern algorithm aversion.

The relevance of this work to dashboards is direct but should be stated carefully. A learning analytics dashboard is not always an algorithm in the forecasting sense; much of what it shows is aggregated data rather than predictions. Some dashboard features, however, such as risk flags or alerts that identify students as needing attention, are algorithmic inferences, and teachers encounter them in a situation similar to that studied by Dietvorst and colleagues: they can compare the system's judgment with their own. The algorithm aversion findings suggest that a single visible error, such as a flag on a student the teacher knows to be thriving, may reduce trust disproportionately and may lead teachers to fall back on their own judgment even where the dashboard could add value. Teachers in Phase 2 described exactly this experience, and several dated their disengagement from a specific incident. The experimental findings provide a plausible mechanism for that pattern, while the school setting adds a feature the experiments did not address: in schools, errors can be discussed among colleagues, so that one teacher's experience of an error may plausibly lower the trust of others who did not witness it. Whether this happens is a question the qualitative phase was able to explore.

This asymmetry has implications for design and implementation that are taken up in Chapter 8. If trust is lost quickly and regained slowly, then the accuracy and timeliness of data in the early period of a dashboard's deployment may matter more than its range of features, and transparency about known limitations may protect trust better than an appearance of completeness. These are arguments rather than findings, and I flag them as such.

### 2.5.5 Trust in educational data specifically

Applying these frameworks to educational data requires attention to features of school data that distinguish them from the data in most of the settings where trust has been studied. School data are often delayed: assessments must be administered, scored, and uploaded before they appear in a dashboard, and the delay may be days or weeks. They are partial: a dashboard typically draws on a subset of the information teachers hold, omitting classroom observations, informal assessments, and knowledge of students' circumstances. They are produced by multiple parties, including teachers themselves, whose data entry practices vary. And they are consequential not only for students but for teachers, since the same data may inform judgments about teaching quality.

These features suggest that trust in educational data has dimensions beyond accuracy in the narrow sense. Timeliness matters because data that are accurate but out of date may be of little use for planning next week's lesson. Fit matters because data that do not match the teacher's own records, even if each is accurate on its own terms, create uncertainty about which to believe. And purpose matters because teachers who suspect the data will be used to judge them may be reluctant to rely on, or to be seen relying on, information that could later be used against them. The data literacy framework of {{mandinach2016|n}} implies the importance of contextual fit, and the leadership research of {{datnow2014|n}} implies the importance of purpose, but neither treats trust as a construct to be measured and modeled. The present study attempts to do so, while acknowledging that its survey measure captures the accuracy and currency dimensions better than it captures purpose.

### 2.5.6 Trust as moderator: the theoretical case

Bringing these strands together, there are three theoretical reasons to expect trust to moderate the relationship between intention and use, in addition to any effect it has on intention itself. First, the organizational definition of trust distinguishes trust from risk-taking and holds that trust leads to risk-taking only when it outweighs perceived risk {{mayer1995}}. Using a dashboard to change instruction involves risk; intending to use it does not. Trust should therefore matter more for behavior than for intention, and in particular should govern whether intentions are carried through. Second, the automation literature describes trust as guiding reliance in situations of uncertainty {{lee2004}}. A teacher who intends to use a dashboard faces, at the moment of use, uncertainty about whether the data reflect reality; trust shapes whether they rely on the data or fall back on their own judgment. Third, the algorithm aversion research shows that experience with errors reduces reliance even among people who have reason to believe the system is useful {{dietvorst2015}}. A teacher's intention may reflect a general belief in the dashboard's value, while their actual use reflects their experience of its specific failures.

These arguments support hypothesis H6 in this study, that trust moderates the intention–use relationship such that the relationship is stronger when trust is high. Chapter 3 sets out this hypothesis formally alongside the others, and Chapter 5 reports that it was supported: the intention–use relationship was substantially stronger for teachers high in trust (simple slope β = .49) than for those low in trust (β = .15, p = .07). The qualitative phase then explains what lies behind low trust and what other conditions, notably time, interact with it.

### 2.5.7 Trust alongside time and capacity

The emphasis on trust in this section should not be read as a claim that trust is the only, or even the main, reason teachers' intentions go unrealized. The literature reviewed in Section 2.3 identified time, data literacy, and leadership as conditions of data use, and there is no theoretical reason to expect trust to displace them. A more plausible expectation, and the one this study carries into its qualitative phase, is that trust and these other conditions combine. A teacher who trusts the data but has no time to consult them will not use the dashboard much; a teacher with time but little trust will consult it and set it aside; a teacher who has both but cannot see what to do with what the dashboard shows will look without acting. On this reading, the intention–use gap is unlikely to have a single cause, and different teachers may be held back by different combinations of conditions.

This expectation has a methodological consequence. A survey model can estimate the average contribution of trust and facilitating conditions to use, and can test whether trust moderates the intention–use relationship, but it cannot easily reveal how conditions combine within particular teachers' working lives, nor identify conditions the instrument did not measure. The latent profile analysis reported in Chapter 5 offers a first step, grouping teachers by their patterns across constructs, and the qualitative phase then examines what lies behind each profile. Teachers in the profile labeled "Willing but stalled", whose intention is high but whose use and trust are low, are of particular interest, because their pattern is the one that the acceptance models are least equipped to explain. Chapter 6 examines how, in these teachers' accounts, low trust, the absence of protected time, and the difficulty of moving from seeing to acting bear on one another.

It is also worth noting a possible direction of influence that the cross-sectional model cannot resolve. Trust may shape use, as hypothesized, but use may also shape trust: teachers who use the dashboard regularly have more opportunities both to encounter errors and to develop calibrated confidence in its reliable features. The automation literature describes trust as evolving with experience {{lee2004}}, and the algorithm aversion findings imply that more exposure can lower trust when errors are visible {{dietvorst2015}}. The present study's design does not allow these directions to be separated statistically, a limitation discussed in Chapter 8, but teachers' accounts in Chapter 6 provide some evidence about the sequence in which trust and use developed for individual teachers.

## 2.6 Ethics, surveillance and professional judgment

### 2.6.1 The ethics of learning analytics

The ethical literature on learning analytics has concentrated mainly on students, and for good reason: students are the subjects of most educational data, and they typically have little say in how the data are collected or used. {{slade2013|n}} set out an influential framework for the ethical issues raised by learning analytics. They argued that analytics should be understood as a moral practice rather than a purely technical one, and they identified a set of concerns that recur across the field: the location and interpretation of data, informed consent and the possibility of opting out, privacy and the boundaries of acceptable surveillance, the classification and management of data, and the asymmetries of power between institutions and the people whose data they hold. They emphasized that students should be treated as agents in the analytics process, not merely as objects of it, and that institutions bear responsibility for transparency about what data are collected and for what purpose.

Although Slade and Prinsloo's attention was directed principally at students, their concerns apply with some modification to teachers. Teachers are both users of dashboard data and, indirectly, its subjects. Dashboards that display the performance of a teacher's students can be read, by anyone with access, as displaying the performance of the teacher. The questions they raise about transparency, purpose, and power therefore apply to teachers as well: who can see the data, what will they conclude, and what consequences might follow? These questions are rarely addressed explicitly when districts deploy dashboards, and the absence of clear answers may itself shape teachers' engagement.

### 2.6.2 Surveillance and the dual use of data

The possibility that teachers perceive dashboards as instruments of surveillance follows from the dual use of school data. The same assessment results that help a teacher plan instruction may be aggregated to compare classes, identify teachers whose students underperform, or inform evaluations. When the tool that presents data to teachers is also accessible to their supervisors, teachers may reasonably wonder whether their own interactions with it, including how often they log in and what they look at, are being monitored. Even in the absence of any actual monitoring, the perception of surveillance can affect behavior.

This concern connects to the leadership research reviewed in Section 2.3.3. {{datnow2014|n}} observed that productive data use depends on a culture in which data are used for improvement rather than blame, and that leaders play a central role in establishing that culture. It also connects to the organizational trust model of {{mayer1995|n}}, in which benevolence and integrity are perceived characteristics of the trustee. When teachers ask "who is this for?", they are asking whether the institution deploying the dashboard is benevolent toward them and whether it adheres to principles they accept. The ethical literature, the leadership literature, and the trust literature thus converge on the proposition that teachers' engagement with dashboards will depend in part on how they perceive the purposes of the institution behind them.

The available literature does not, however, offer much direct evidence on how teachers in schools experience dashboards in these terms. The ethical discussion has been largely conceptual, and the empirical research on teachers' data use has focused more on capacity and organization than on perceived surveillance. This study's qualitative phase was designed to allow such perceptions to surface without presupposing them, and Chapter 6 reports that they did, forming one of the five themes.

### 2.6.3 Professional judgment and the authority of data

A related concern is the relationship between dashboard data and teachers' professional judgment. Teachers develop detailed knowledge of their students through daily interaction, and they make many instructional decisions on the basis of that knowledge. Dashboards present an alternative source of knowledge, one that claims a kind of objectivity by virtue of being quantitative and systematic. When the two agree, the dashboard may seem redundant; when they disagree, the teacher must decide which to believe. The data literacy framework of {{mandinach2016|n}} treats the integration of data with contextual knowledge as a core competence, implying that neither should simply override the other. But in organizational settings where data carry institutional authority, teachers may feel pressure to defer to the dashboard, or, conversely, may resist it as an encroachment on their professional expertise.

{{gasevic2015|n}} cautioned against analytics that are detached from the instructional context in which data are generated, arguing that interpretation requires knowledge of the course design and the learning activities involved. In school settings, teachers hold much of that knowledge. A dashboard that does not accommodate it, or that presents its indicators as authoritative without acknowledging their limits, may provoke the kind of skepticism that undermines trust. This consideration supports the practical recommendation, developed in Chapter 8, that dashboards be designed to support rather than to substitute for teachers' judgment, for instance by making the sources and timing of data visible and by framing alerts as prompts for inquiry rather than as conclusions.

## 2.7 Measuring "use"

### 2.7.1 Self-report and its limits

In the acceptance literature, use has most commonly been measured through self-report: respondents are asked how often they use a system, or how much of their work involves it. Self-report has obvious advantages of convenience and scope, since it allows use to be measured across systems and settings where logs are unavailable. It also has well-known weaknesses. Respondents may misremember or misestimate their behavior, and they may present it in a socially desirable light. When self-reported use is collected in the same survey as the belief constructs that are hypothesized to predict it, the associations among them may be inflated by common method variance.

{{podsakoff2003|n}} reviewed the sources of common method bias in behavioral research and the procedural and statistical remedies available. Sources include common rater effects, such as consistency motifs and social desirability; item characteristics, such as common scale formats; item context effects, such as the ordering of items; and measurement context effects, such as collecting predictor and criterion at the same time with the same instrument. Procedural remedies include obtaining measures of predictor and criterion from different sources, separating their measurement in time or method, protecting respondent anonymity, and improving item wording. Statistical remedies include various approaches to modeling or controlling a method factor, though the authors noted the limitations of commonly used techniques such as Harman's single-factor test. The present study addresses this concern in two ways, described in Chapter 4: by collecting platform log data for teachers who consented to linkage, providing an independent behavioral measure for a subsample, and by assessing common method variance statistically while acknowledging the limits of the tests used.

### 2.7.2 Logs and their limits

Platform logs appear to offer an objective alternative. They record when users log in, which pages they view, and sometimes how long they stay. Learning analytics research has relied heavily on such data, and dashboard evaluations have frequently used log-based measures of engagement {{schwendimann2017}}. Logs are not, however, a neutral record of use. They capture interactions with the platform and nothing else. A teacher who logs in once a month, exports a class report to a spreadsheet, and works with that spreadsheet throughout the month will appear in the logs as a light user. A teacher who logs in daily and glances at the front page will appear as a heavy user. If the purpose of a dashboard is to inform instruction, the first teacher may be making more meaningful use of it than the second.

This point connects to Rogers's concept of reinvention, discussed in Section 2.3.5. When users adapt an innovation to their own purposes, their use may move outside the channels through which it was designed to be observed. It also connects to the process model of {{verbert2013|n}}: logs capture awareness, in the sense of exposure to the dashboard, but are largely silent on reflection, sensemaking, and impact. Measuring use only through logs therefore risks systematically undercounting the kinds of use that matter most.

### 2.7.3 Toward a broader construct of use

The literature reviewed here suggests that "use" of a dashboard is a multidimensional phenomenon that neither self-report nor logs capture fully. Self-report captures what respondents believe and are willing to say about their use, which may include off-platform activity but is subject to bias. Logs capture on-platform activity accurately but miss everything else. Neither captures the depth of use, in the sense of how far along the path from awareness to impact a teacher travels. In this study, use is measured through a self-report scale covering six specific dashboard actions, validated against logged logins in the subsample of 288 teachers for whom logs were available; Chapter 5 reports a correlation of r = .58 between the two, indicating substantial but incomplete overlap. Teachers' accounts in Phase 2 then explain part of the gap. The study's third contribution, set out in Chapters 7 and 8, is a recommendation for a broader construct of use that incorporates off-platform adaptations and depth of engagement.

## 2.8 How the problem has been studied

### 2.8.1 The dominance of single-method designs

The research reviewed in this chapter has, for the most part, been conducted within single methodological traditions. The acceptance literature is overwhelmingly quantitative, relying on surveys and structural equation modeling to estimate the relationships among beliefs, intentions, and behavior. These methods are well suited to testing whether hypothesized relationships hold across large samples and to estimating their relative strength, and they have well-established standards for measurement and model fit {{kline2016;hair2019}}. They are less well suited to explaining why relationships take the form they do, or to identifying influences that the researcher did not anticipate when designing the instrument.

The research on teachers' data use, by contrast, has relied substantially on qualitative case studies and interviews, which provide rich accounts of how data use unfolds in particular schools and how teachers and leaders understand it. These accounts are essential for understanding mechanisms but are harder to generalize, and they do not usually estimate the relative weight of different influences. The learning analytics literature has been dominated by design and evaluation studies, often in higher education and often focused on usability, with comparatively few studies of sustained use in authentic school settings {{schwendimann2017}}.

### 2.8.2 The case for an explanatory sequential design

The problem this study addresses calls for a design that can do both things: estimate the relationships among acceptance constructs, trust, and use across a large sample, and explain those relationships, especially the unexplained gap between intention and use, through the accounts of the teachers involved. Mixed methods research provides such a design. {{creswell2018|n}} describe the explanatory sequential design as one in which a quantitative phase is followed by a qualitative phase intended to explain the quantitative results, with the qualitative sample selected on the basis of the quantitative findings. {{ivankova2006|n}} discussed the practical decisions the design entails, including the relative priority of the two phases, the point at which they connect, and how the results are integrated.

The philosophical basis for combining methods in this way is developed in Chapter 4, which adopts a pragmatic stance {{morgan2007}}. It is sufficient here to note that the mixed methods literature offers both a rationale for integration and practical techniques for achieving it. {{fetters2013|n}} distinguished integration at the levels of design, methods, and interpretation, and described joint displays as a way of bringing quantitative and qualitative results together so that their relationship, whether confirmation, expansion, or discordance, can be examined systematically. {{ocathain2010|n}} described "following a thread," in which a finding from one component is pursued through the other. {{teddlie2009|n}} emphasized the quality of the inferences drawn from mixed methods studies, arguing that meta-inferences combining both strands should be judged on their own terms. Each of these is used in this study, as Chapters 4 and 7 explain.

Few studies of teachers' technology use, and fewer still of dashboard use, have taken this approach. The present study's design is not novel as a design, but its application to the intention–use gap in dashboard use is, and I argue in Chapter 7 that the integration of survey, log, and interview data revealed findings that none of the three could have produced alone.

### 2.8.3 Explaining quantitative results qualitatively

An explanatory sequential design places particular demands on its qualitative phase. The interviews are not an independent study but a means of explaining the patterns found in the survey, which means that the qualitative sample must be selected with reference to those patterns and that the analysis must remain open to explanations the survey did not anticipate. The literature offers guidance on both points. {{ivankova2006|n}} described how the connecting point between phases can be defined through the selection of participants on the basis of quantitative results, such as typical or extreme cases, or cases representing statistically identified groups. In this study, the latent profiles from Chapter 5 provide that basis, and the interview sample was weighted toward the "Willing but stalled" profile because its pattern was the least well explained by the model.

On the question of sample size, {{guest2006|n}} examined how many interviews were needed before new codes ceased to emerge in a relatively homogeneous sample and found that most themes were identified within the first dozen interviews. That finding cannot be transferred mechanically to a study whose participants differ in subject, school, and profile, but it gives some assurance that a sample of 24 teachers, with 11 in the profile of greatest interest, is sufficient to identify the main patterns within and across profiles. Chapter 4 discusses this judgment in more detail.

For the analysis itself, the reflexive and flexible approach to thematic analysis set out by {{braun2006|n}} is well suited to an explanatory phase, because it allows themes to be developed both deductively, in relation to the constructs of the quantitative model, and inductively, from patterns teachers raised that the model did not include. The phases of familiarization, coding, theme development, review, and definition that they describe provide a transparent procedure, and the inductive element is what allowed time and perceived purpose to emerge in this study as explanations the survey had not captured. The standards for the quantitative phase, including conventional criteria for model fit {{hu1999}} and for discriminant validity {{fornell1981}}, are addressed in Chapters 4 and 5.

Three gaps are central and correspond to the study's research questions. And the practical recommendations respond to the organizational and ethical literature's emphasis on time, actionable information, and transparent governance.

## 2.10 Chapter summary

This chapter has argued that the shallow and uneven use of learning analytics dashboards by secondary teachers cannot be adequately explained by any single body of literature. The learning analytics literature describes what dashboards are meant to do and reveals how little is known about whether they do it. The literature on teachers' data use shows that data use is a practice requiring literacy, time, and supportive leadership. The technology acceptance tradition provides robust models of intention but weaker accounts of behavior, and measures facilitating conditions in ways that may omit time. The trust literature offers a construct, and a theoretical rationale, for explaining why teachers who intend to use a dashboard may not act on what it shows them. The ethics literature raises the possibility that teachers' engagement depends on their perception of the purposes behind the data. And the measurement literature suggests that use, as conventionally measured, may be undercounted.

Chapter 3 builds on this review to specify the theoretical framework that guides the study. It defines the constructs, sets out the trust-extended acceptance model, and states hypotheses H1 to H6, including the moderating role of trust. Chapter 4 then describes the explanatory sequential design through which the model was tested and its results explained.

# Abstract

School districts increasingly purchase learning analytics dashboards for teachers, yet teachers' use of these tools is often shallow and uneven. Technology acceptance models explain why teachers intend to use a dashboard, but they say little about why teachers who intend to use one do not, or about how trust in the underlying data shapes what teachers actually do. This dissertation addresses that gap through an explanatory sequential mixed methods study of secondary teachers in four public school districts in the Northern Lakes region of the United States, all of which used the same commercial dashboard.

In Phase 1, 412 teachers in 31 secondary schools (a response rate of 34.9%) completed a survey measuring performance expectancy, effort expectancy, facilitating conditions, trust in dashboard data, behavioral intention and self-reported use; platform log data were available for 288 consenting teachers. A structural equation model fit the data well. Performance expectancy (β = .41) and trust (β = .29) predicted intention, and effort expectancy did so weakly (β = .12). Intention (β = .33) and facilitating conditions (β = .27) predicted use, explaining 31% of its variance. Trust moderated the intention–use relationship (β = .19): the path was markedly stronger for high-trust teachers (β = .49) as for low-trust teachers (β = .15, p = .07). Latent profile analysis identified three groups: Engaged users (27%), a large Willing but stalled group with high intention, low trust and low use (41%), and Sceptics (32%).

In Phase 2, interviews with 24 teachers selected from the three profiles were analyzed thematically. Teachers described trust as a matter of timeliness and fit as well as accuracy, lacked protected time for data work, wanted dashboards to suggest what to do next, worried that administrators would use the same data to judge them, and used dashboards through workarounds that platform logs did not record.

Integrating both phases, the study contributes a trust-extended acceptance model in which trust moderates the intention–use link; a three-part account of trust in educational data as timeliness, accuracy and perceived purpose; evidence that "use" is under-measured when it relies on logs or self-report alone; and practical recommendations for protected time, actionable dashboard views and transparent data governance.

**Keywords:** learning analytics dashboards; technology acceptance; trust; teacher data use; explanatory sequential design; mixed methods

# Chapter 7: Integration and Discussion

## 7.1 Introduction

Chapters 5 and 6 reported the two phases of this study on their own terms. Chapter 5 tested a trust-extended acceptance model on survey responses from 412 secondary teachers in the Northern Lakes region and found that performance expectancy, effort expectancy and trust in dashboard data together explained close to half of the variance in behavioral intention, that intention and facilitating conditions together explained just under a third of the variance in reported use, and that trust moderated the link between intention and use. It also identified three latent profiles of teachers, the largest of which, the "Willing but stalled" group, combined relatively high intention with low use and low trust. Chapter 6 then reported a thematic analysis of 24 interviews with teachers drawn from all three profiles and developed five themes about how teachers use, adapt or avoid the district dashboard in their weekly work.

This chapter brings the two strands together. It does what an explanatory sequential design is for: it uses the qualitative accounts to explain, qualify and, where necessary, contest the quantitative relationships {{creswell2018}} {{ivankova2006}}. The chapter has two jobs. The first is integration in the narrow technical sense, that is, the systematic comparison of the two data sets and the drawing of meta-inferences from them. The second is discussion in the conventional dissertation sense, that is, the placing of those meta-inferences against the literature reviewed in Chapter 2 and the theoretical framework set out in Chapter 3.

The chapter proceeds as follows. Section 7.2 describes how integration was carried out and presents the joint display (Table 7.1), which is the central analytic object of the chapter. Sections 7.3, 7.4 and 7.5 then take the three research questions in turn. RQ1 and RQ2 are discussed briefly in their own right, since each was answered within its phase, but they are discussed with an eye to what the other strand adds. RQ3, the mixed methods question about the gap between intention and use, receives the longest treatment, because it is where integration does the most work. Section 7.6 states the contributions of the study as they emerge from this integration. Section 7.7 reflects on the quality of the meta-inferences, and Section 7.8 summarizes the chapter and hands on to the conclusion.

## 7.2 Integration procedures and the joint display

### 7.2.1 Levels of integration

Integration in this study was planned at three levels, following the distinction drawn by {{fetters2013|n}} between integration at the design, methods and interpretation levels. At the design level, integration was built in through the explanatory sequential structure itself: the qualitative phase existed in order to explain the quantitative one, and its questions were shaped by the quantitative results rather than written in advance (Chapter 4, Section 4.2). At the methods level, integration took the form of connecting, in which the latent profiles from Phase 1 served as the sampling frame for Phase 2 and the interview protocol was built around specific quantitative puzzles (Chapter 4, Section 4.6; Chapter 6, Section 6.1). This chapter concerns the third level, integration at the level of interpretation and reporting.

Two techniques were used at this level. The first was a joint display, a table that places quantitative results and qualitative findings side by side so that the relation between them can be stated explicitly {{fetters2013}}. The second was "following a thread," in which a question or finding from one strand is picked up and pursued through the other until it either resolves or reveals something new {{ocathain2010}}. Following a thread was used informally from the moment the Phase 1 results were in, since several interview questions were themselves threads, and more formally during this final stage, when each quantitative result in the structural model was traced through the coded interview data and each theme was traced back to the survey and log data.

### 7.2.2 Assessing fit

For each pairing in the joint display, I made a judgement about what {{fetters2013|n}} call the "fit" of the integrated data, using their three categories. Confirmation means that the two strands lead to the same conclusion. Expansion means that they diverge in scope, so that one strand adds detail or dimensions that the other did not capture, without contradicting it. Discordance means that the strands are inconsistent or contradict each other. Discordance is not a failure of the design; in an explanatory study it is often the most informative result, because it shows where one method's construct or measure has missed something the other has caught. I adopted the pragmatist stance set out in Chapter 4, in which the aim of inquiry is warranted, useful inference rather than the reconciliation of rival worldviews {{morgan2007}}. On that stance, discordance prompts a further abductive move: what would have to be true for both sets of results to hold at once?

The judgements of fit were made in two passes. In the first pass I made them alone, working from the quantitative output and the coded interview data. In the second pass, my chair and one committee member each reviewed the draft joint display and the evidence for each cell, and we discussed the three cells on which one of them disagreed with my first classification. Two of those disagreements concerned whether a finding was best described as expansion or discordance, and are discussed below where they arise. This procedure is not a reliability check in the psychometric sense, and I do not report agreement statistics, but it was a deliberate attempt to expose the meta-inferences to critique before committing to them {{teddlie2009}}.

## 7.3 RQ1: What predicts teachers' reported use of dashboards?

RQ1 asked to what extent performance expectancy, effort expectancy, facilitating conditions and trust in dashboard data predict teachers' reported use of learning analytics dashboards. Chapter 5 answered this question statistically. The measurement model fitted the data well by conventional criteria (χ²(194) = 412.6, CFI = .956, TLI = .948, RMSEA = .052, SRMR = .046) {{hu1999}}, all constructs showed adequate reliability and convergent validity, and the structural model supported all six hypotheses, with H2 supported only weakly. Together the predictors explained 48% of the variance in intention and 31% of the variance in reported use. This section discusses what those results mean once the interview data are brought alongside them.

### 7.3.1 Performance expectancy: usefulness in principle

Performance expectancy was the strongest predictor of intention (β = .41), and its mean of 3.71 was the highest of any predictor. In this the study reproduces the most consistent finding of the acceptance literature, in which the belief that a technology will improve one's job performance is the dominant driver of intention to use it {{davis1989}} {{venkatesh2003}}. The interviews confirm the result and, importantly, locate it. Teachers in all three profiles, including several Sceptics, described the *idea* of a dashboard as useful. They wanted earlier warning of students falling behind, a quicker view of who had missed assignments and some way of seeing patterns across classes. Ms. Alvarez, an Engaged mathematics teacher, put the shared view simply:

> When it works, it's the fastest way I have of seeing the kid who is slipping before the kid knows they're slipping. (Ms. Alvarez, maths, Engaged)

The Sceptics were the exception, and their exception is instructive. Their low performance expectancy rested less on a rejection of data than on the judgment that this dashboard told them nothing new. Mr. Dawson's complaint that the red list named "six kids" he could have identified in September (Chapter 6, Section 6.2.3) is a claim about redundancy, not about the value of knowing which students are struggling. And the clearest disconfirming case in the sample, Mr. Patel, was classified as a Sceptic because he had set the district dashboard aside in favor of a tracking system he had built himself (Section 6.8). His low performance expectancy was a verdict on a tool, not on data use as such.

This pattern matters for how the gap between intention and use should be read. If low use were driven by doubts about usefulness, one would expect the Willing but stalled teachers, who make up 41% of the sample, to express such doubts. They did not. Their performance expectancy was high in the survey, and in interview they spoke of the dashboard as something they *should* use and *would* use if circumstances allowed. In terms of the diffusion literature, the dashboard's relative advantage was largely conceded; what was in question was its compatibility with the working week and the observability of its benefits {{rogers2003}}. The meta-inference is that, for this population, perceived usefulness is a precondition that has largely been met, and the policy and design levers lie elsewhere.

### 7.3.2 Effort expectancy: where the effort really is

Effort expectancy predicted intention only weakly (β = .12, p < .05). The acceptance literature offers a familiar explanation: ease of use tends to matter most early in adoption and to fade as users gain experience {{venkatesh2003}}. The district dashboard was not new in any of the four districts, and most teachers were familiar with its basic navigation. The interviews are consistent with that explanation but add something to it. When teachers described effort, they rarely described the interface. Logging in, filtering by class and opening a student record were treated as manageable once learned. What they described as effortful was the time it took to get from login to something usable: reconciling the dashboard with the gradebook, working out what the data meant and deciding what to do about it. Ms. Eriksen, a Stalled mathematics teacher, made the distinction directly:

> It's not hard. I can find stuff. It's that every time I go in, it's twenty minutes before I've got something I could use. And I don't have twenty minutes. If it was two minutes I'd be in there every day. (Ms. Eriksen, mathematics, Willing but stalled)

The EE items, adapted from UTAUT, asked about the ease of learning and operating the system. They did not ask about the ease of interpreting the data or translating it into instruction. The weak EE coefficient is therefore best read as a finding about *operational* effort, not about effort in general. The effort that matters for teachers is the effort of data literacy in the full sense developed by {{mandinach2016|n}}: identifying problems, interpreting data, and transforming information into instructional decisions. I return to this in Section 7.5, because it is one of the two mechanisms that the interviews identified as converting, or failing to convert, intention into use. Here the point is a narrower one about measurement: a construct borrowed from the general acceptance literature, where the effort of use is largely effort of operation, does not transfer cleanly to a domain where the effort of use is largely effort of judgement.

### 7.3.3 Trust: a strong predictor that teachers held at a low level

Trust in dashboard data was the second strongest predictor of intention (β = .29), and yet it had the lowest mean of any construct in the model (2.94, below the scale midpoint). That combination is the starting point for most of what follows. It means that trust mattered a good deal for intention and that, on average, teachers did not have much of it.

The decision to extend the acceptance model with trust was argued in Chapter 3 on two grounds. The first was the organizational trust literature, in which trust is a willingness to be vulnerable to another party based on expectations about that party's ability, benevolence and integrity {{mayer1995}}. The second was the human factors literature on trust in automation, in which trust governs reliance on automated systems and is appropriate when it is calibrated to the system's actual capability {{lee2004}}. The survey items drew on both, and the quantitative result confirms that trust adds explanatory power to a UTAUT-based model of intention in this setting. Trust carried the second largest path to intention while performance and effort expectancy were held in the model, which is the evidence an extension of this kind needs: the added construct has a unique association with the outcome over and above the constructs it was added to {{kline2016}}.

The interviews confirm the importance of trust, and they also expand the construct. In the survey, trust was measured as a single latent factor with five items about the accuracy, reliability and dependability of the data. In interview, teachers described trust in ways that resolved into three distinguishable components, which I set out in Section 7.5.2. For RQ1, it is enough to note that the survey measure captured something real, since its reliability was high (α = .91) and it behaved as predicted, but that it captured a compound of considerations that teachers themselves distinguished.

### 7.3.4 Facilitating conditions: a significant predictor of the wrong thing

Facilitating conditions predicted reported use directly (β = .27), as UTAUT specifies {{venkatesh2003}}. Read alone, this result would support a conventional policy response: improve access, devices and training, and use will follow. The interviews complicate that reading, and the complication is the first of the two discordances in the joint display.

The FC items asked whether teachers had the resources, knowledge and support needed to use the dashboard, and whether help was available when they had difficulty. In interview, teachers across all three profiles described these conditions as broadly adequate. They had laptops, they had logins, most had attended at least one training session, and they knew whom to ask when something broke. What they did not have was time. Nineteen of the 24 participants, including 10 of the 11 Stalled teachers, described time as the main practical constraint on use (Chapter 6, Table 6.3), and for Stalled teachers it was the constraint they named first. What they lacked was not time in general but a regular point in the week at which looking at data was the expected activity. Mr. Jansen said it most memorably, in the phrase that gave Theme 2 its name:

> That's the thing. I don't have a Tuesday for this. ... There's no slot where it's the dashboard's turn. So it's whenever, and whenever means never, mostly. (Mr. Jansen, English, Willing but stalled)

The discordance is not that the survey result is wrong. FC did predict use, and the interviews give a plausible reason why: teachers with better support were more likely to be in schools where data use was structured into the week, typically through PLC meetings, and those structures supplied time as a by-product. The discordance lies in what the construct *names*. The FC scale, as adapted from UTAUT, measured the material and technical conditions of use. The interviews identified temporal and organizational conditions as binding. Even the training the scale did measure was measured only as present or absent; several Stalled teachers recalled training that covered where the buttons were and little about what to do with the data (Chapter 6, Section 6.4.2). Because the scale did not measure time, the coefficient of .27 cannot tell us how much of the FC effect is carried by time; it can only tell us that the conditions it did measure were associated with use. This is a case in which, in the terms of {{ertmer1999|n}}, a first-order barrier that the instrument treated as resources turns out, on inspection, to be a first-order barrier of a different kind, and one that interacts with second-order beliefs about whether data work is part of the job. I discuss the implications for the construct in Section 7.5.4.

### 7.3.5 Answer to RQ1

In summary, the answer to RQ1 is that performance expectancy and trust were substantial predictors of intention, effort expectancy a weak one, and intention and facilitating conditions moderate predictors of reported use, with trust moderating the intention–use path. The model explained a substantial share of the variance in intention and a smaller share of the variance in use, which is a common pattern in acceptance research and one that the gap between mean intention (3.58) and mean use (2.21 on a 0–5 scale) makes vivid. The integration adds three qualifications to that answer. Performance expectancy was high and largely uncontested, so it explains *who intends* better than it explains *who uses*. Effort expectancy measured operational ease, which was not where the effort lay. And facilitating conditions, as measured, stood in for a condition, time, that the scale did not name.

## 7.4 RQ2: How do teachers use, adapt or avoid dashboards?

RQ2 asked how teachers explain how they use, adapt or avoid dashboards in their practice. Chapter 6 answered it through five themes. This section does not restate those themes in full. Instead it reads them against the literature on learning analytics dashboards and against the quantitative results, to show what kind of answer they give.

### 7.4.1 Use as episodic and externally prompted

The first thing the interviews show is that use, for most teachers, was episodic rather than routine. Engaged teachers described a weekly habit, often tied to a fixed slot in their planning. Stalled teachers described use that was triggered from outside: a PLC meeting that required a data slide, a counselor asking about a particular student, a mid-quarter progress report. Twelve participants described this meeting-driven pattern explicitly. Ms. O'Neill, a Stalled social studies teacher, described opening the dashboard the night before each data team meeting "so I have something to say," and predicted, accurately as far as the pattern goes, that her logins would show "little spikes every three weeks."

This finding fits the literature in a way that is mildly uncomfortable for it. Dashboard research has framed the purpose of these tools in terms of a cycle of awareness, reflection, sensemaking and impact {{verbert2013}}, and the design of most dashboards assumes a user who returns to them as part of an ongoing reflective practice. Early statements of the promise of learning analytics similarly imagined data flowing continuously into the decisions of instructors and institutions {{siemens2011}}. The systematic review by {{schwendimann2017|n}} found that relatively few dashboard studies had evaluated use and impact in authentic settings. The present study, though it was not a longitudinal evaluation, suggests one reason why such evaluations may be disappointing: in at least this setting, the cycle the dashboard is designed to support was rarely initiated by teachers themselves. It was initiated by organizational routines, and it stopped when those routines stopped. Use, in other words, was more a property of the school week than of the individual teacher.

### 7.4.2 From seeing to acting

The second feature of teachers' accounts is the gap between seeing and acting, captured in Theme 3, "Show me what to do on Monday." Teachers across profiles said that the dashboard was good at telling them *what* was happening (which students had low completion rates, whose quiz scores had dropped, who had not logged in to the learning platform) and poor at helping them decide *what next*. Eighteen participants described this gap, nine of them Stalled teachers. Ms. Chen, whose phrase gives the theme its name, summarized it:

> Show me what to do on Monday. That's it. I can see that eleven kids didn't get the thesis statement standard. Great. I knew that roughly. ... It stops at the part where I actually need help. (Ms. Chen, English, Willing but stalled)

Mr. Hollis called the mastery view "a very nice picture of the problem," and Mr. Patel, less generously, called the dashboard a thermometer when what he needed was a doctor.

This is a theme the learning analytics literature has anticipated in general terms. {{gasevic2015|n}} argue that analytics divorced from learning theory and pedagogy risk producing indicators that are easy to compute but hard to act upon, and that the value of analytics lies in their connection to learning. {{mandinach2016|n}} describe data literacy for teaching as culminating in the transformation of information into instructional action, a step that draws heavily on pedagogical content knowledge. The interviews give that argument a concrete form. Teachers were not asking the dashboard to make decisions for them; several were wary of anything resembling automated recommendation. They were asking it to narrow the space of options, by linking an indicator to a curricular unit, a common misconception or a set of resources colleagues had found useful. The display stopped at the indicator. Their requests were also carefully bounded: Ms. Eriksen wanted "a menu, not a script," and Mr. Rossi worried that a dashboard which told teachers what to do would become one more thing they were evaluated against. The bridge, where it existed, had usually been built with colleagues rather than supplied by the tool, as in Ms. Alvarez's routine of reading item-level results and turning a commonly missed item into the next two days' warm-up.

### 7.4.3 Avoidance as a professional stance

The third feature concerns avoidance. Sceptics, and some Stalled teachers, described avoiding the dashboard not because it was useless or hard but because they were uneasy about what it was for. Theme 4, "Who is this for?", captures a recurring suspicion that the data teachers were asked to look at were the same data administrators used to judge teachers. Fourteen participants raised this concern, five of the six Sceptics among them. Ms. Greer, a Sceptic, pointed out that her principal could see her "red list," which was longer than her neighbor's because she taught the inclusion section, and that no one had ever told her how that list was read from the office. Mr. Young put the same doubt in a phrase: he did not think the numbers were made up, but he did not know "whose side they're on." Responses ranged from strategic avoidance, Mr. Lopez doing "the minimum to show I've been in there," to strategic engagement, Mr. Rossi updating missing assignments before data meetings so that his class would not "light up red."

This theme connects the study to two bodies of work reviewed in Chapter 2. The first is the ethical literature on learning analytics, which has emphasized that the same data can serve care and control, and that the power relations in which analytics are embedded shape what they mean to those who are analyzed {{slade2013}}. That literature has focused largely on students as the subjects of analytics; this study suggests that teachers experience a parallel position, as people whose work is made visible through their students' data. The second is the leadership literature, in which the culture that school leaders build around data, whether it is framed as inquiry or as accountability, shapes whether teachers engage with data productively {{datnow2014}}. Teachers whose schools had addressed the question directly, such as Ms. Santos, whose principal had said publicly that she would never use the dashboard in an evaluation, and Mr. Farouk, whose data team had a rule against comparing teachers, described less unease, although the interview sample is too small to treat that as more than a suggestive pattern.

Avoidance on these grounds is better understood as a professional stance than as resistance to technology. Teachers who avoided the dashboard for this reason were protecting a domain of professional judgement from what they saw as an encroaching, decontextualized form of measurement. Whether that stance is justified in any particular school is not something this study can determine. What it can say is that the stance was articulate and principled, and that it was most often voiced by teachers whose survey scores placed them in the Sceptic profile, where performance expectancy was low. The quantitative model treated low performance expectancy as a belief about usefulness; the interviews suggest that, for some Sceptics, it was a belief about *whose* usefulness.

### 7.4.4 Adaptation and workarounds

The fourth feature of teachers' accounts is adaptation. Theme 5 described a set of workarounds: exporting data to spreadsheets where teachers could combine them with their own records, taking screenshots to share with parents, printing a class list to annotate by hand. Workarounds were reported by all seven Engaged teachers, six of the eleven Stalled teachers and two of the six Sceptics (Table 6.3), and they were most elaborate and routinized among the Engaged. Ms. Quinn, for example, exported her mastery data at the end of each unit into a spreadsheet of her own, where she set the dashboard's numbers beside her exit tickets and quiz scores and then worked from the spreadsheet rather than the dashboard. Chapter 6 read this distribution through the idea of re-invention, in which adopters modify an innovation to fit existing practice {{rogers2003}}, and I agree with that reading: the Engaged teachers had made the dashboard one input into a data practice they controlled.

These adaptations are use. They are arguably among the most considered forms of use in the data, because they involve the teacher integrating dashboard information with other sources of evidence and with their own judgement, which is what the data literacy framework describes as good practice {{mandinach2016}}. They are also, as the joint display records, largely invisible to the platform. A single export or screenshot generates one log event; the subsequent hour of work in a spreadsheet generates none. I return to the measurement consequences in Section 7.5.3.

### 7.4.5 Answer to RQ2

The answer to RQ2, then, is that teachers in this study used the dashboard episodically, in response to organizational prompts more than to their own questions; that they valued it for seeing but found it weak for deciding; that some avoided it as a matter of professional self-protection; and that many adapted it through workarounds that moved its data into other tools. In each case, teachers explained their behavior by reference to conditions outside the individual acceptance decision: the structure of the week, the design of the display, the purposes of school leaders and the affordances of other tools. That is the bridge to RQ3, since it suggests that the factors which separate intention from use lie largely in the organizational setting of use.

## 7.5 RQ3: Explaining the gap between intention and use

RQ3 asked how the qualitative accounts explain the quantitative relationships, particularly the gap between intention and use. This is the question for which the study was designed as mixed methods, and it is answered here through five meta-inferences, each tied to one or more rows of Table 7.1.

### 7.5.1 Trust moderates the conversion of intention into use (confirmation)

The quantitative strand found that trust moderated the intention–use path (β = .19, p < .01). Simple slopes showed that the association between intention and use was considerably stronger among high-trust teachers (β = .49) than among low-trust teachers (β = .15, p = .07); put simply, the intention–use link was roughly three times as strong for teachers who trusted the data. For low-trust teachers, intention was only weakly and not conventionally significantly related to use. H6 was supported.

The interviews confirm this pattern and give it a mechanism. Stalled teachers who distrusted the data did not describe avoiding the dashboard altogether. They described visiting it, sometimes regularly, and then not relying on it. Ms. Chen described spending forty minutes reconciling a student's dashboard grade with her gradebook, failing to find the source of the difference, and deciding that she could not do this every time, "so I go to the gradebook." Mr. Hollis compressed the same logic into a proverb: with two clocks that disagree, "you go with your watch." These are not accounts of disuse in the simple sense; they are accounts of what {{lee2004|n}} describe as distrust leading to disuse, in which an operator who does not trust an automated aid declines to rely on it even when it is available and even when it might be correct. Intention brings the teacher to the screen. Trust determines whether what is on the screen enters the teacher's decisions.

This interpretation has a consequence for how the moderation result should be read. The outcome variable in the structural model was the average frequency of six self-reported dashboard actions, several of which (for example, using dashboard data to plan a lesson or to group students) imply reliance and not just viewing. Low-trust teachers who visited but did not rely would score modestly on such a composite even with high intention, which is the pattern the simple slopes show. The interviews therefore support reading the moderation effect as an effect on *reliance-based* use. They also suggest a hypothesis that this study cannot test, namely that the moderation would be weaker for pure viewing behavior and stronger for reliance behavior. I return to this in Chapter 8 as a direction for future research.

### 7.5.2 Trust in educational data has three components (expansion)

In the survey, trust was a single construct. In interview, it came apart into three components, and this is the principal expansion the qualitative strand adds to the model. Table 7.2 summarizes them.

**Table 7.2** Components of teachers' trust in dashboard data

| Component | What teachers meant | Typical breach | Illustrative participants |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timeliness | The data reflect the current state of the class, soon enough to act on. | Data a week or more behind the gradebook; "red flags" for students the teacher already knew about. | Mr. Boateng, Ms. Eriksen, Mr. Dawson |
| Accuracy | The data are correct and agree with the teacher's own records. | A visible error, such as a whole class shown as missing a test it had taken, or a grade that cannot be reconciled with the gradebook. | Ms. Chen, Mr. Hollis, Ms. Kowalski, Mr. Thibault |
| Purpose | The data are collected and shown to help teachers teach, not to evaluate them. | Dashboard indicators raised by an administrator; unclear rules about who can see what and for what. | Ms. Greer, Mr. Young, Mr. Lopez |

*Note.* Components were derived from Theme 1 and Theme 4 (Chapter 6). Participants listed are those whose accounts most clearly illustrated each component; most participants referred to more than one.

The first two components correspond closely to the "ability" dimension of trustworthiness in the organizational model of {{mayer1995|n}}: the belief that the trustee, here the data system, is competent to do what it claims. But the interviews make a distinction that the general concept of ability blurs. Timeliness and accuracy are different properties. Data can be accurate and late, as when an assignment score is correct but appears a week after the teacher has already acted on it. Data can be timely and wrong, as when a same-day flag is produced by a synchronization error. Teachers treated these as different failures with different consequences. Lateness made the dashboard redundant, since it told them what they already knew. Inaccuracy made it hazardous, since it might lead them to act wrongly, and it was inaccuracy that produced the most abrupt loss of trust.

The abruptness is worth dwelling on, because it matches a finding from outside education. {{dietvorst2015|n}} showed in a series of experiments that people who see an algorithm err lose confidence in it more readily than in a human forecaster who errs, and then tend to prefer the human even when the algorithm performs better on average. Thirteen participants described a specific incident after which they had relied on the dashboard much less, and several described exactly this asymmetry. Ms. Kowalski recalled a synchronization error that showed her whole fourth period as not having taken a unit test, an error the assistant principal noticed before she did; it was fixed within a week, but she "never really went back to it the same way." Mr. Thibault drew the contrast with human error explicitly: when he made a mistake he knew why, but when the dashboard made one he did not know what else it was getting wrong. His explanation, that an error is tolerable when its cause can be understood and alarming when it is evidence about errors one cannot see, is consistent with the emphasis in the automation literature on making a system's workings observable so that reliance can be calibrated {{lee2004}}.

The third component, purpose, corresponds less to ability than to the benevolence and integrity dimensions of trustworthiness in the Mayer model {{mayer1995}}. Here the question is not whether the data are good but whether the people and institutions behind them intend the teacher well and act on principles the teacher accepts. Teachers who doubted the purpose of the dashboard did not usually doubt its accuracy. They doubted that it was theirs. This connects the trust construct to the ethical and leadership literatures discussed in Section 7.4.3 {{slade2013;datnow2014}}, and it helps explain why some teachers could speak reasonably well of the data's accuracy and still sit toward the low end of the trust scale.

The meta-inference is that trust in educational data, at least as teachers in this study described it, has three components: timeliness, accuracy and purpose. The first two concern the data system's competence and are breached by system failures; the third concerns the intentions of the institution that deploys it and is breached by organizational action. This three-part account is the study's second contribution, and it has a direct consequence for measurement. A single trust scale, however reliable (α = .91 here), combines components that have different causes and therefore require different remedies. A district could fix its data lag and still face low trust if teachers believed that the data were destined for evaluation.

### 7.5.3 Logs undercount use, and self-report is not enough (discordance)

The second discordance in the joint display concerns the measurement of use itself. Among the 288 teachers who consented to log linkage, self-reported use correlated with logged logins at r = .58. In Chapter 5, this was reported as evidence of convergent validity for the self-report measure, and it is. It is also evidence that the two measures share only about a third of their variance (r² ≈ .34), which leaves a good deal of room for them to be measuring different things.

The interviews suggest what those things are. Workarounds of the kind described in Theme 5 generate few log events relative to the work they involve. An Engaged teacher who exports a class view at the end of a unit and then works with it for weeks in a spreadsheet, as Ms. Quinn did, logs few sessions and might reasonably report frequent use of the dashboard's data. Mr. Nakamura made the point himself: if the district counted logins it would think he barely used the dashboard, when he used it "more than anyone in my department. I just don't use it in there." Conversely, a Stalled teacher who opens the dashboard the night before each data meeting, as Ms. O'Neill did, or who tidies missing assignments so that her class does not "light up red," as Mr. Rossi did, logs sessions that involve little instructional use. In the first case logs undercount use; in the second they overcount it, at least if use is understood as reliance on the data for teaching.

This discordance does not undermine the quantitative findings. They were estimated on the self-report measure, and when Chapter 5 re-estimated the model with logged logins as the outcome for the 288 linked teachers, the key relationships held, though at reduced strength (Section 5.8.2). But it does change what those findings are findings about. The self-report composite captured some reliance-based and workaround use that logs miss. Logs captured some visiting that does not amount to use in any pedagogically meaningful sense. Neither measure captured the full range of activity that teachers described as using the dashboard. Common method variance is a separate concern, and the procedural and statistical remedies applied in Chapter 5, including a Harman single-factor result of 31% and a latent method factor that accounted for about 4% of indicator variance, suggest that it is unlikely to account for the structural relationships {{podsakoff2003}}. The issue here is construct coverage, not method bias.

The meta-inference is methodological, and it is the study's third contribution. "Use" of a learning analytics dashboard is under-measured by either logs or frequency self-report alone. A more adequate construct would distinguish at least three things: access (visiting the dashboard, which logs measure well), reliance (letting dashboard information inform a decision, which neither measure captures directly) and extended use (working with dashboard data in other tools, which logs miss almost entirely). This distinction aligns with the dashboard literature's concern that evaluations have more often examined whether dashboards were perceived and opened than whether they changed teaching or learning {{schwendimann2017}}. It also echoes the argument that analytics should be judged by their connection to learning and teaching, not by their own activity metrics {{gasevic2015}}. A study that measured only logins would have concluded that Mr. Nakamura was a light user, which, on his account, he was not.

### 7.5.4 Facilitating conditions omitted time (discordance)

The first discordance, introduced under RQ1 in Section 7.3.4, can now be stated as a meta-inference about the intention–use gap. The FC scale measured the technical and material conditions of use. The interviews identified protected time as the binding condition for converting intention into use, especially among Stalled teachers. Because the scale did not measure time, the structural model could not represent the factor that teachers themselves most often gave for the gap.

This was one of the cells on which a committee reviewer initially disagreed with my classification (Section 7.2.2). The case for expansion is that time can be seen as one more facilitating condition, so that the interviews simply add an item to a list the survey had begun. The case for discordance, which I adopted, is that time is not of the same kind as the conditions the scale measured. Devices, logins and training are resources that a district can supply once and maintain. Time is a feature of how the working week is organized, and providing it requires taking something else away. More importantly, teachers in the sample rated the measured conditions as middling (mean 3.05) and described them in interview as broadly adequate, yet still did not use the dashboard. If FC had been measuring the binding condition, Stalled teachers who reported adequate conditions should have reported adequate use more often than they did. On this reading the survey and interviews do not merely differ in scope; they point to different answers about what is holding teachers back, and the interviews are better placed to say.

This finding speaks directly to the barriers literature. {{ertmer1999|n}} distinguished first-order barriers, which are external to the teacher, such as access, time, training and support, from second-order barriers, which concern teachers' beliefs about teaching and the role of technology in it. Time sits naturally among first-order barriers in that account, but acceptance instruments derived from information systems research, where use is often part of the job as defined, have tended not to include it {{venkatesh2003}}. The present study suggests that, for discretionary data tools in schools, time may be the first-order barrier that matters most, and that it interacts with a second-order belief about whether data work is part of teaching or an addition to it. Stalled teachers did not doubt that data could help; they doubted that anyone expected them to make room for it. Several drew the contrast with grading, which was owed to students, parents and the reporting calendar, whereas dashboard work was owed to no one in particular (Chapter 6, Section 6.3.1). Time, on this account, is a matter of the structure of obligations as much as of hours. The Sceptics' position was different: their accounts fit Ertmer's second-order category more closely, since their reluctance rested on beliefs about what it means to know a student and about the purposes of institutional data, which additional time would not by itself change.

### 7.5.5 The "Willing but stalled" teacher (expansion)

The largest of the three latent profiles, at 41% of the sample, combined high intention with low use and low trust. Chapter 5 described this profile statistically, and Chapter 6 sampled it most heavily, with 11 of the 24 interviews. Integration allows a fuller description.

The Willing but stalled teacher, as the interviews portray her or him, believes the dashboard could help, is not put off by its interface, has the device and the login, and intends to use it. She opens it when a meeting demands it, finds data that are late or occasionally wrong, recognizes students she already knew about, and finds no clear next step. She may have heard an administrator mention dashboard indicators in a context that made her wary. She has no protected time to work through the data properly, and so she closes the dashboard and returns to the tools she trusts. Her intention survives intact; her use does not grow.

Described this way, the Stalled teacher is not a late adopter in the diffusion sense {{rogers2003}}. She is not skeptical of the innovation, and she would not be moved by persuasion about its benefits, which she already accepts. She is blocked by three conditions, each of which corresponds to one of the integrated findings: low trust, with its components of timeliness, accuracy and purpose (Section 7.5.2); absence of protected time (Section 7.5.4); and a display that does not support the move from seeing to acting (Section 7.4.2). All three are features of the system and the organization rather than of the teacher. This is the most consequential practical inference of the study, because it implies that the largest group of teachers in the sample is not a problem of motivation to be solved through training or advocacy, but a problem of conditions to be solved through scheduling, data quality, governance and design.

By contrast, the Sceptic profile (32%) is better described as low in performance expectancy and, in several cases, in the purpose component of trust; Sceptics' doubts were frequently about whether the dashboard told them anything new and whose interests it served. The Engaged profile (27%) was distinguished in interview less by personal enthusiasm than by circumstance. Each of the seven Engaged teachers had a protected occasion for data work: for three it was a weekly common planning period in which the team looked at data and committed to an action, for two it was a role (department lead, intervention coordinator) that made reviewing data part of the job, and for two it was a personal routine, such as Ms. Quinn's first ten minutes of each planning period, which rested in turn on useful data meetings and a supportive department. Engaged teachers also more often recalled an occasion on which the dashboard had been right when their own impression was wrong, as Ms. Ibarra did, and such moments seemed to buffer them against the loss of trust that followed a visible error. These descriptions are drawn from seven interviews and cannot be generalized with confidence, but they are consistent with the leadership literature's emphasis on the culture in which data use is embedded {{datnow2014}}.

Two disconfirming cases, reported in Chapter 6 (Section 6.8), mark the limits of this account. Mr. Patel, classified as a Sceptic, was in interview an enthusiastic user of data who had replaced the district dashboard with a tracking system of his own; his low reported use reflected a better alternative, not skepticism. Ms. Mbeki, classified as Willing but stalled, described conditions much like those of the Engaged teachers, including decision-oriented data meetings and a principal who had made clear commitments about evaluation, and attributed her low use mainly to the demands of her first years of teaching. Neither case contradicts the meta-inferences above, but both show that a profile groups teachers whose survey responses are similar for different reasons. The description of the Stalled teacher is a common configuration, not a type to which every member conforms, and the second case suggests that career stage, which the survey treated only as a control variable, may be a further condition of use.

### 7.5.6 Answer to RQ3

The answer to RQ3 can now be stated. The qualitative accounts explain the gap between intention and use in three ways. First, they confirm and give a mechanism for the moderation effect: low-trust teachers visit but do not rely, so intention converts weakly into reliance-based use. Second, they identify a condition the model did not measure, protected time, as the binding constraint for the largest group of teachers, and they identify a design limitation, the absence of actionable guidance, as a second constraint. Third, they show that part of the measured gap is an artifact of how use was measured, since workarounds move use outside the platform's view. The gap between intention and use is therefore partly real, produced by distrust, lack of time and lack of actionability, and partly a measurement effect, produced by an overly narrow construct of use.

### 7.5.7 Reconsidering the acceptance framework

The meta-inferences above invite a broader question about the theoretical framework set out in Chapter 3. Acceptance models were developed to explain whether individuals adopt a technology, and they locate the decisive variables in the individual's beliefs about it {{davis1989}}. UTAUT widened the frame by adding social influence and facilitating conditions, but it retained the individual decision as the unit of explanation {{venkatesh2003}}. This study suggests that, for learning analytics dashboards in schools, a substantial part of the explanation lies in conditions that are organizational rather than individual: the scheduling of the week, the quality and latency of the data pipeline, the purposes to which leaders put the data, and the design choices that determine whether a display stops at description or offers a route to action.

One response would be to abandon acceptance models for this domain in favor of organizational or sociotechnical accounts. I do not think the evidence supports that move. The trust-extended model fitted well, its paths were in the predicted directions, and it identified the moderation effect that the interviews then explained. A model that did not measure individual beliefs would not have found that effect, and would have had no way to distinguish the Willing but stalled teachers from the Sceptics, since the difference between those profiles lies precisely in performance expectancy and intention. The more defensible response is to treat the acceptance model as necessary but incomplete, and to specify the organizational conditions that sit around it. Diffusion theory has long held that an innovation's compatibility with existing practices and its observable results shape adoption {{rogers2003}}; the present findings suggest that, in schools, compatibility is above all temporal, and observability depends on whether the display connects to action.

In practical terms, an extended model for future work would retain performance expectancy and intention, keep trust as both predictor and moderator, measure trust through its three components rather than as a single factor, replace or supplement the facilitating conditions scale with items on protected time and organizational routines, and measure use as access, reliance and extended use. Such a model would be more cumbersome than UTAUT, and its additional constructs would need their own validation {{hair2019}}, including evidence of discriminant validity between the trust components of the kind the Fornell–Larcker criterion was used to provide in Chapter 5 {{fornell1981}}. But it would ask the questions that the teachers in this study suggested were the right ones.

## 7.6 Contributions of the study

The integrated findings support four contributions, which I state here explicitly and return to in Chapter 8.

The first is theoretical: a trust-extended acceptance model in which trust in dashboard data both predicts intention and moderates the conversion of intention into use. Acceptance models in the TAM and UTAUT tradition explain intention well and use less well {{davis1989;venkatesh2003}}. Adding trust as a predictor of intention addresses one gap, but adding it as a moderator of the intention–use path addresses the specific weakness of those models in this setting, by explaining why intention converts more readily into use for some users than for others. The interviews supply the mechanism, reliance, that makes the moderation interpretable.

The second is conceptual: a three-part account of trust in educational data, distinguishing timeliness, accuracy and purpose. The account draws on the organizational and automation trust literatures {{mayer1995;lee2004}} but specifies them for the case of teachers and student data. Its value lies in separating components that have different causes and therefore different remedies. Timeliness and accuracy are matters of data engineering; purpose is a matter of governance and leadership.

The third is methodological: the combination of platform logs, self-reported frequency and interviews shows that dashboard "use" is under-measured by any single source. The study recommends a broader construct that distinguishes access, reliance and extended use, and it illustrates how an explanatory sequential design can expose a measurement problem that neither strand would have detected alone {{creswell2018}}.

The fourth is practical: the analysis points to three levers, protected time, actionable views and transparent data governance, which correspond directly to the three conditions blocking the Willing but stalled profile. These are developed as implications for policy, practice and design in Chapter 8.

## 7.7 Quality of the meta-inferences

An integrated conclusion is only as good as the inferences that link the strands. {{teddlie2009|n}} propose that the quality of inferences in mixed methods research be judged by design quality and interpretive rigor, including whether conclusions are consistent with the findings of each strand, consistent with one another, and preferred over plausible alternative interpretations. I address each briefly.

Consistency with each strand was supported by the structure of the joint display, in which every meta-inference is tied to a specific quantitative result and specific themes. No meta-inference rests on qualitative data alone where the quantitative strand was silent; where the interviews went beyond the survey, as with the components of trust, the inference is labeled as expansion and presented as a refinement for future measurement rather than as an established result.

Consistency among the meta-inferences is reasonably strong. The account of the Stalled teacher in Section 7.5.5 draws together the trust, time and actionability findings into a single description, and those findings do not pull against one another. The one place where tension might be expected, between the claim that trust moderates intention–use and the claim that measured use is partly an artifact, resolves on inspection: the moderation is interpreted as an effect on reliance-based use, which the self-report composite captured better than logs did.

Alternative interpretations were considered at several points. The FC discordance could be read as expansion, and Section 7.5.4 gives the reasons for preferring discordance. The moderation result could, in principle, reflect a third variable associated with both trust and the intention–use link, such as school leadership culture. The interviews make that plausible, and so does Chapter 5: trust had the highest intraclass correlation of any scale (.09), and while cluster-robust standard errors adjusted the estimates for school membership, the structural model did not include school-level predictors, since a full two-level model was not feasible with 31 schools {{kline2016}}. The study therefore cannot rule out that some of the trust effect is a school effect, and I note this as a limitation in Chapter 8. Finally, the interview sample was designed to over-represent the Stalled profile, which was appropriate for the explanatory purpose {{ivankova2006}}, and within that profile no new codes relevant to the intention–use gap appeared in the tenth and eleventh interviews, consistent with evidence that most codes appear within the first dozen or so interviews in a relatively homogeneous group {{guest2006}}. It does mean that the Engaged and Sceptic accounts rest on fewer interviews, and inferences about those profiles have been stated more cautiously.

On balance, I judge that the meta-inferences in this chapter are warranted in the sense that matters to a pragmatist account of inquiry {{morgan2007}}: they follow from the evidence, they survive the alternatives I could identify, and they are specific enough to be tested and to be acted upon.

## 7.8 Chapter summary

This chapter integrated the quantitative and qualitative strands of the study through a joint display and the technique of following a thread, and discussed each research question in light of the literature. The integration confirmed that trust in dashboard data matters, both for intention and for the conversion of intention into use. It expanded the trust construct into three components, timeliness, accuracy and purpose, and expanded the account of effort to include the interpretive work of moving from data to action. It found two productive discordances: the facilitating conditions scale did not measure the condition teachers most often cited, protected time, and neither logs nor self-report adequately captured use, much of which took place through workarounds outside the platform. The largest group of teachers, the Willing but stalled, emerged as willing users blocked by conditions rather than reluctant adopters. On this basis the chapter stated four contributions: a trust-moderated acceptance model, a three-part account of trust in educational data, a methodological case for a broader construct of use, and practical levers in time, actionability and governance. Chapter 8 summarizes the findings by research question, develops the implications for policy, practice and design, sets out the limitations of the study, and identifies directions for further research.

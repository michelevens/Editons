# Chapter 8: Conclusion

## 8.1 Introduction

This dissertation set out to explain why secondary teachers' use of learning analytics dashboards is shallow and uneven even where districts have invested in the tools and teachers say they intend to use them. It did so through an explanatory sequential mixed methods design {{creswell2018}}: a survey of 412 teachers in four districts of the Northern Lakes region, analyzed with structural equation modeling and latent profile analysis, followed by interviews with 24 teachers selected from the resulting profiles. Chapter 7 integrated the two strands and discussed them against the literature. This final chapter draws the study together. Section 8.2 summarizes the findings by research question. Section 8.3 restates the contributions. Section 8.4 sets out implications for policy, practice and dashboard design. Section 8.5 discusses the limitations of the study, Section 8.6 proposes directions for further research, and Section 8.7 closes.

## 8.2 Summary of findings

### 8.2.1 RQ1: Predictors of reported use

The first research question asked to what extent performance expectancy, effort expectancy, facilitating conditions and trust in dashboard data predict teachers' reported use of dashboards. After a confirmatory factor analysis that showed good fit (CFI = .956, TLI = .948, RMSEA = .052, SRMR = .046) and adequate reliability and discriminant validity for all constructs {{hu1999}} {{fornell1981}}, the structural model supported all six hypotheses. Performance expectancy was the strongest predictor of behavioral intention (β = .41), followed by trust (β = .29) and, weakly, effort expectancy (β = .12). Intention (β = .33) and facilitating conditions (β = .27) predicted reported use. The model explained 48% of the variance in intention and 31% of the variance in use. Trust moderated the intention–use path (β = .19): among high-trust teachers the path was strong (β = .49), among low-trust teachers it was weak and not conventionally significant (β = .15, p = .07).

Two descriptive results framed everything that followed. Mean intention (3.58 on a five-point scale) was well above mean reported use (2.21 on a 0–5 scale), and mean trust (2.94) was the lowest of any predictor. Latent profile analysis identified three groups of teachers: Engaged users (27%), the Willing but stalled (41%), who combined high intention with low use and low trust, and Sceptics (32%), low in performance expectancy and intention.

### 8.2.2 RQ2: How teachers use, adapt and avoid dashboards

The second research question asked how teachers explain their use, adaptation or avoidance of dashboards. Interviews were conducted by video call with seven Engaged users, eleven Willing but stalled teachers and six Sceptics, and lasted between 45 and 70 minutes (median 56 minutes). Reflexive thematic analysis of the 24 transcripts {{braun2006}} produced five themes. In "The numbers are a week late," teachers described trust as dependent on timeliness and fit with their own records, and described its collapse after a single visible error. In "I don't have a Tuesday for this," they described a working week with no protected time for data, in which dashboard use happened mainly when PLC meetings demanded it. In "Show me what to do on Monday," they described dashboards that showed what was happening but not what to do next. In "Who is this for?", they described unease that the same data might be used by administrators to judge them. And in "Workarounds as use," they described exporting, screenshotting and printing dashboard data to work with it elsewhere. The themes were unevenly distributed across profiles in ways that matter for interpretation. All 24 participants contributed to the first theme, on trust. Time was raised by 19 participants, including 10 of the 11 Stalled teachers but only 3 of the 6 Sceptics. Concerns about surveillance were raised by 14, including 5 of the 6 Sceptics. Workarounds were described by all seven Engaged users but only 2 Sceptics. Taken together, teachers explained their behavior largely by reference to conditions outside their own acceptance of the tool. For Stalled teachers, the first four themes compounded one another, so that each attempt at use was expensive and its payoff uncertain; for Engaged teachers, the same barriers had been mitigated by protected time, collegial routines, explicit leadership commitments and workarounds under the teacher's control; and for Sceptics, a prior judgment that the dashboard told them little that was new combined with doubts about its purpose.

### 8.2.3 RQ3: Explaining the gap between intention and use

The third research question asked how the qualitative accounts explain the quantitative relationships, particularly the gap between intention and use. Integration through a joint display {{fetters2013}} and by following threads across the strands {{ocathain2010}} produced three main answers. First, the interviews confirmed the moderation effect and supplied its mechanism: low-trust teachers visited the dashboard but did not rely on it, so intention converted weakly into reliance-based use. Second, they showed that the facilitating conditions scale, which measured devices, access and training, did not measure the condition teachers most often cited, protected time, and that the absence of actionable guidance was a further barrier. Third, they showed that part of the apparent gap was a measurement effect: workarounds took use outside the platform, so that logs undercounted it, and frequency self-report captured it only partially. The Willing but stalled profile, the largest in the sample, emerged as a group of willing users blocked by conditions rather than reluctant adopters.

## 8.3 Contributions

The study makes four contributions, stated in Chapter 1 as aims and established in Chapter 7 through integration.

The first is a **trust-extended acceptance model** in which trust in dashboard data predicts intention and moderates the conversion of intention into use. This addresses a persistent weakness of acceptance models in the tradition of TAM and UTAUT, which explain intention better than behavior {{davis1989}} {{venkatesh2003}}, by identifying a condition under which intention does and does not translate into use. Drawing on the automation literature, the study interprets the moderation as an effect on reliance {{lee2004}}.

The second is a **three-part account of trust in educational data**, distinguishing timeliness, accuracy and purpose. The first two components correspond to the ability dimension of trustworthiness in organizational trust theory, and the third to its benevolence and integrity dimensions {{mayer1995}}. The account matters because the components have different causes and require different responses: data engineering for the first two, governance and leadership for the third.

The third is **methodological**. By combining platform logs, self-reported frequency of use and interviews, the study shows that dashboard "use" is under-measured by any single source, and it proposes a broader construct distinguishing access, reliance and extended use. It also illustrates the value of an explanatory sequential design for exposing problems of construct coverage that a single-method study would not detect {{ivankova2006}}.

The fourth is **practical**. The findings identify three levers for districts and designers: protected time for data work, dashboard views that support action rather than only awareness, and transparent governance of who sees teacher-linked data and for what. These are developed in the next section.

## 8.4 Implications

### 8.4.1 Implications for district policy

The most direct policy implication follows from the Willing but stalled profile. If the largest group of teachers already accepts that dashboards are useful and intends to use them, then further investment in persuasion or in generic training is unlikely to change much. The binding constraints lie in conditions that districts control. I suggest three.

The first concerns time. Districts that purchase dashboards should treat the time needed to use them as part of the cost of the purchase. In practice this means protected, scheduled time in the working week, ideally attached to existing collaborative structures such as PLCs, but framed as time for teachers' own instructional inquiry rather than for the production of data reports. The interviews suggest that PLC time already drives much of the use that occurs, and that its character matters: Engaged teachers more often described PLCs that used data to ask questions about teaching, while Stalled teachers more often described PLCs that used data to fill in templates.

The second concerns governance. The purpose component of trust can only be addressed by clear and public rules about who has access to teacher-linked dashboard data and for what purposes. A district that intends dashboard data to support teachers' instructional decisions should say plainly whether those data may enter teacher evaluation, and should honor what it says. The ethical literature on learning analytics has stressed transparency about purpose and the power relations in which data are used {{slade2013}}; this study suggests that teachers, and not only students, need that transparency if they are to trust the data.

The third concerns procurement. Districts are in a position to require that vendors demonstrate data latency and accuracy against the district's own systems before purchase and at intervals thereafter. Because a single visible error can damage trust disproportionately, consistent with the asymmetry described by {{dietvorst2015|n}}, reconciliation between the dashboard and the gradebook is not a technical nicety but a condition of use. Where data are known to lag, the dashboard should say so on screen, so that teachers can calibrate their reliance rather than discover the lag by accident.

### 8.4.2 Implications for school leadership and professional practice

For school leaders, the study's implications concern the culture of data use. The leadership literature has argued that the way principals frame data, whether as a resource for inquiry or as a tool of accountability, shapes what teachers do with it {{datnow2014}}. The interviews in this study are consistent with that argument and suggest some specific practices. Leaders can separate the dashboard indicators they discuss with teachers in instructional conversations from those they use in evaluation, and can make that separation explicit. They can model use of the dashboard as a source of questions rather than verdicts. And they can protect the time they ask teachers to spend on data from being absorbed by other demands.

For teachers and those who support their professional learning, the implication concerns data literacy in its full sense. The interviews suggest that the hard part of dashboard use is not operating the tool but interpreting its indicators and deciding what to do about them, which is the core of data literacy for teaching as {{mandinach2016|n}} define it. Professional learning that focuses on navigation, as several teachers said their training had, addresses the wrong problem. More useful would be collaborative work in which teachers examine dashboard data alongside their own evidence and develop shared repertoires of instructional responses. The workarounds described in Theme 5 suggest that some teachers are already doing this alone, and that their practices could be shared. Routines such as Ms. Alvarez's item-level reading of assessment results or Ms. Quinn's regrouping spreadsheet were built with colleagues over several years; a department that made such routines visible could shorten that apprenticeship considerably. The case of Ms. Mbeki, who had supportive conditions but attributed her low use to the demands of her first years of teaching, also suggests that early-career teachers may need dashboard work to be scaffolded within induction rather than added to an already crowded week.

### 8.4.3 Implications for dashboard design

For designers and vendors, three implications follow. The first is that dashboards should support the move from seeing to acting. Teachers did not ask for automated decisions, and several were wary of them; they asked for displays that connect an indicator to something they could do, such as a curricular unit, a known misconception or resources colleagues had used. This is consistent with the argument that learning analytics must be grounded in learning and pedagogy if they are to inform teaching {{gasevic2015}}, and with the view that dashboards should support not only awareness but sensemaking and impact {{verbert2013}}.

The form that such support takes matters. Teachers' requests were carefully bounded: Ms. Eriksen asked for "a menu, not a script," and Mr. Rossi feared that a dashboard which told teachers what to do would become one more standard against which they were judged. The design implication is for suggestions that are optional, transparent about their basis and easy to set aside, so that they shorten the path from data to decision without taking the decision away from the teacher. A design that prescribed rather than suggested would risk importing into the classroom the very concerns about purpose that Theme 4 described, and would ask teachers to extend their reliance on the dashboard from description to prescription before the dashboard had earned it.

The second is that dashboards should make their own data quality visible. A display that shows when each indicator was last updated, and flags discrepancies with the source system, gives teachers what they need to calibrate trust, which is the condition for appropriate reliance in the automation literature {{lee2004}}. Hiding latency protects the appearance of the product at the cost of trust in it.

The third is that dashboards should accommodate extended use rather than treat it as leakage. Teachers export and combine dashboard data because the dashboard does not let them integrate it with their own evidence. Better export, annotation and integration features would serve these teachers directly, and would also make extended use visible to districts that want to understand how their investment is being used.

## 8.5 Limitations

The findings should be read in light of several limitations, some inherent in the design and some specific to this study.

First, the survey data were cross-sectional. The structural model is consistent with the causal ordering proposed in Chapter 3, but it cannot establish that ordering. It is possible, for example, that use shapes trust as much as trust shapes use, since teachers who use the dashboard more have more opportunities to see it err or to find it reliable. The interviews include accounts in both directions. A longitudinal design would be needed to separate them.

Second, use was measured mainly by self-report. Self-reported use correlated with logged logins at r = .58 among the 288 teachers who consented to log linkage, which supports its validity, but the interviews showed that both measures miss important forms of use. Common method variance was addressed through procedural separation of scales and three statistical checks {{podsakoff2003}}: Harman's single-factor test attributed 31% of variance to a single factor, a latent method factor accounted for about 4% of indicator variance, and the key relationships held, at reduced strength, when the model was re-estimated with logged logins as the outcome. Together these suggest that method bias does not account for the results. Harman's test is, however, a weak diagnostic, the log re-estimation rested on a single month of login counts, and some inflation of the relationships among self-reported constructs cannot be ruled out.

Third, the facilitating conditions scale did not measure time, which the interviews identified as the most important condition. This limitation was only visible after the qualitative phase, which is one argument for the design, but it means that the quantitative model could not estimate the effect of the factor teachers most often cited.

Fourth, trust was measured as a single construct. The three-part account developed in Chapter 7 is an empirically grounded proposal, not a validated measurement model, and its components have not yet been measured separately.

Fifth, teachers were nested in 31 schools, and school-level factors such as leadership culture may account for part of the trust effect. Trust had the highest intraclass correlation of any scale (.09), which is consistent with that possibility. The analysis reported cluster-robust standard errors as a sensitivity check, under which the weak effort expectancy path lost conventional significance, but it did not model school-level predictors, because a full two-level structural model of this complexity was not feasible with 31 clusters {{kline2016}}. Leadership effects are therefore inferred from interviews rather than estimated.

Sixth, the study was conducted in four districts in one region, all using the same commercial dashboard. The response rate of 34.9% is respectable for a voluntary teacher survey but leaves room for non-response bias; teachers with strong views about the dashboard, in either direction, may have been more likely to respond. The findings may not transfer to settings with different dashboards, different data governance or different expectations of teachers' time. Transferability, in the pragmatist sense of judging what can be carried from one setting to another {{morgan2007}}, is a matter for readers with knowledge of their own contexts.

Seventh, the interview sample was purposively weighted toward the Willing but stalled profile, which suited the explanatory aim but means that the accounts of Engaged teachers (7 interviews) and Sceptics (6 interviews) rest on smaller numbers. Within the Stalled profile, the tenth and eleventh interviews added no new codes relevant to the intention–use gap, which is consistent with evidence on when saturation tends to be reached in a relatively homogeneous group {{guest2006}}, but claims about the other two profiles are more tentative. The interview sample also contained no teacher from the "other subjects" grouping, which made up 22% of survey respondents. Two such teachers, one in world languages and one in physical education, agreed to take part but withdrew before scheduling. The qualitative findings therefore cannot speak to teachers of the arts, world languages, physical education and similar subjects, whose relationship to dashboard data, which in these districts was organized largely around assessed academic standards, may differ considerably. All interviewees had also volunteered to be contacted, which may have favored teachers with stronger views about the dashboard in either direction.

Eighth, the latent profiles grouped together teachers whose reasons for similar survey responses differed. Two disconfirming cases illustrate the point. Mr. Patel, classified as a Sceptic, had set the district dashboard aside for a tracking system he had built himself, and Ms. Mbeki, classified as Willing but stalled, had supportive conditions and attributed her low use mainly to being early in her career. The account of the Stalled teacher in Chapter 7 describes a common configuration of barriers, not a uniform type, and the profiles should not be read as diagnoses of individual teachers.

Finally, as a single researcher conducting both phases, I brought to the qualitative phase expectations formed by the quantitative results. That is intended in an explanatory sequential design, but it carries the risk of seeing in the interviews what the survey led me to look for. I sought to mitigate this through an interview protocol that invited teachers to describe their practice before raising the survey findings, through reflexive memoing, and through committee review of the joint display. It cannot be eliminated.

## 8.6 Directions for future research

Several lines of further research follow from the study.

The first is the development and validation of measures for the constructs the study identified but did not measure. A trust scale with separate subscales for timeliness, accuracy and purpose would allow the three-part account to be tested, including whether the components predict different outcomes and respond to different interventions. A measure of protected time and organizational routines for data use would allow the time constraint to be modeled alongside conventional facilitating conditions. Both would need the full sequence of factor analytic validation, including evidence of convergent and discriminant validity {{hair2019}}.

The second is a broader measurement of use. Future studies could combine logs, structured self-report and short-interval methods such as weekly diaries or brief experience sampling to distinguish access, reliance and extended use. A specific hypothesis arising from Chapter 7 is that trust moderates the intention–use relationship more strongly for reliance-based use than for simple access; a study that measured both could test it directly.

The third is longitudinal and multilevel. A panel design following teachers across a school year, ideally across schools that differ in leadership practice, could estimate whether trust and use influence each other over time, and how much of the trust effect lies at the school level. Such a design could also track what happens to trust after a visible data error, testing in an authentic setting the asymmetry described in experimental work on algorithm aversion {{dietvorst2015}}.

The fourth is intervention research. The practical levers identified in this study, protected time, actionable views and transparent governance, are hypotheses about what would increase use. They could be tested through design-based or quasi-experimental studies in which a district introduces one or more of them and compares change in trust and use with schools that have not. A study of this kind would also respond to the call for evaluations of dashboards in authentic settings that examine effects on practice rather than only perceptions {{schwendimann2017}}.

The fifth concerns students and families. This study focused on teachers, but several teachers described using dashboard data with parents, and the ethical questions raised in Theme 4 apply with greater force to students as data subjects {{slade2013}}. Research that examined how students and families understand and trust the same dashboards would complement the teacher-focused account offered here.

Finally, there is scope for comparative research across different kinds of analytics tools and educational levels. The acceptance and trust mechanisms identified here may operate differently for tools that make predictions, as some early-warning systems do, than for tools that describe, and differently in higher education, where early learning analytics work was concentrated {{siemens2011}}, than in secondary schools.

## 8.7 Closing

The title of this dissertation names three things: trust, tools and time. The study began from a puzzle about tools, why teachers who have them and intend to use them do not, and found that the answer lies largely in the other two. Teachers in this study did not, on the whole, doubt that dashboards could be useful. They doubted whether the data were timely, accurate and meant for them, and they could not find the time in their week to turn what they saw into what they would do. Where trust was high, intention became use. Where time was protected and the display pointed toward action, teachers used the dashboard in ways its designers might have hoped for, and often in ways its logs could not see.

The broader lesson is that learning analytics dashboards are not adopted by individuals alone. They are adopted, or not, by schools: by the schedules that do or do not make room for them, by the leaders whose purposes shape what the data mean, and by the data systems whose reliability determines whether teachers can depend on them. Acceptance models remain useful for understanding the beliefs teachers bring to these tools, and this study has sought to extend them. But the teachers who were willing and stalled were waiting not for a change of heart but for a change of conditions. Districts, school leaders and designers are in a position to provide it.

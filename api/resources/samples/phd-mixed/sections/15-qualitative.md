# Chapter 6: Qualitative Findings

This chapter reports the second, qualitative phase of the study and answers the second research question: how do teachers explain how they use, adapt or avoid dashboards in their practice? It also begins the work of the third research question by organizing the qualitative findings around the puzzles that Chapter 5 left unresolved. The chapter opens by explaining the connecting point between the two phases, that is, how the survey results shaped who was interviewed and what they were asked, and then describes the 24 participants. The core of the chapter presents five themes developed through reflexive thematic analysis. A cross-profile comparison follows, along with a discussion of cases that did not fit the dominant pattern. The chapter closes by summarizing what the interviews add to the survey results, handing the formal integration of the two strands to Chapter 7.

In keeping with the explanatory sequential design described in Chapter 4, the qualitative phase was not a free-standing study. Its sampling frame, its interview guide and, to an extent, its analytic attention were all shaped by the quantitative results {{ivankova2006}}. I have tried throughout to report the interviews on their own terms first, as teachers' accounts of their own work, before reading them back against the model. The reader will notice, however, that the themes are presented with the Phase 1 puzzles in view, because that is what the design asked the qualitative phase to do {{creswell2018}}.

## 6.1 From Phase 1 to Phase 2: the connecting point

### 6.1.1 Puzzles carried forward from the survey

Chapter 5 closed with four puzzles that the survey alone could not resolve. The first, and the one that most strongly shaped Phase 2, concerned the gap between intention and use. Mean behavioral intention to use the district dashboard was moderately high (M = 3.58, SD = 0.90 on the five-point scale), yet mean reported use was low (M = 2.21, SD = 1.02 on the 0–5 frequency scale), and intention explained only part of the variance in use (BI→Use β = .33; R² for Use = .31). The latent profile analysis located this gap in a specific group of teachers: the "Willing but stalled" profile, which made up 41% of the sample and combined high intention with low use and low trust.

The second puzzle concerned trust. Trust in dashboard data had the lowest mean of any predictor (M = 2.94, SD = 0.86), it predicted intention almost as strongly as performance expectancy did (Trust→BI β = .29, against PE→BI β = .41), and it moderated the relationship between intention and use (Trust × BI→Use β = .19). For teachers with high trust the intention–use link was roughly three times as strong as for teachers with low trust (simple slopes β = .49 and β = .15, the latter not reaching conventional significance, p = .07). The survey could show that trust mattered in this way. It could not say what teachers meant by trusting, or not trusting, the data.

The third puzzle concerned facilitating conditions. FC predicted use directly (β = .27), but its mean was only 3.05, and the items behind it asked about hardware, access and training. I wondered whether the scale had captured the conditions that teachers themselves regarded as decisive. The fourth puzzle was a measurement one. Self-reported use correlated with logged logins at r = .58 among the 288 teachers who consented to log linkage. That is a respectable association, but it also left a large share of the variance unshared, and I did not know whether self-report overstated use, whether logins understated it, or whether the two were measuring somewhat different things.

These puzzles became the "threads" that the interview guide was designed to follow {{ocathain2010}}. Appendix B reproduces the guide in full. In brief, every interview included questions about a recent occasion of dashboard use (or a recent occasion when the teacher had expected to use it and did not), about what the teacher believed the data did and did not show, about when in the week dashboard work happened, about who else looked at the same data, and about anything the teacher did with dashboard data outside the dashboard itself.

### 6.1.2 Case selection

As Chapter 4 explains in more detail, participants were selected purposively from survey respondents who had agreed to be contacted, using their assigned latent profile as the primary stratifying variable. The design deliberately over-represented the "Willing but stalled" profile because that profile embodied the intention–use gap at the center of RQ3. Within each profile I sought variation in subject, district and years of experience, and I invited teachers whose individual posterior probability of profile membership was high, so that the interviews would speak clearly to the profile rather than to its boundaries.

The final qualitative sample comprised 24 teachers: seven "Engaged users", eleven "Willing but stalled" teachers and six "Sceptics". On sample size, I was guided by the finding that, in a relatively homogeneous sample, most of the codes that a study will eventually develop tend to appear within the first dozen or so interviews {{guest2006}}. Because this sample was deliberately heterogeneous across three profiles, I treated that figure as a floor for the largest group rather than a target for the whole sample, and I continued recruiting "Willing but stalled" teachers until two successive interviews in that group added no new codes relevant to the intention–use gap. In the event this occurred after the tenth and eleventh "stalled" interviews. I make no stronger claim than that: saturation is a judgment about the analytic questions in hand, and other questions asked of the same data might require more cases.

Interviews took place by video call, lasted between 45 and 70 minutes (median 56 minutes), and were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Each participant was given a pseudonym, and subject and profile are reported with each quotation so that readers can track the connecting point through the chapter. School and district identifiers are withheld because, in districts of this size, the combination of subject, school and years of experience would make some teachers identifiable.

### 6.1.3 Analytic approach in brief

Chapter 4 describes the analysis in full; here I summarize only what a reader needs to interpret the themes. I followed the six phases of thematic analysis set out by {{braun2006|n}}, moving from familiarization through initial coding, the generation and review of candidate themes, and the definition and naming of themes, to the writing of this chapter. Coding was inductive in the first pass and became more deductive in the second, when I read the coded data against the four Phase 1 puzzles. I kept these passes distinct in my coding memos so that I could see which codes had arisen from teachers' talk and which I had gone looking for. Themes were treated as patterns of shared meaning organized around a central idea, not as topic summaries, and I tried to name each one in teachers' own words where a phrase captured the central idea well.

The five themes are summarized in Table 6.2. They are not independent of one another; Section 6.7 traces how they intersect, and in particular how the first four combine to produce what I came to think of as the "stalled" pattern.

A word on how I report prevalence. Thematic analysis does not rest on counting, and the importance of a theme lies in what it explains rather than in how often it occurs {{braun2006}}. Nonetheless, in a mixed methods study the reader is entitled to know whether a pattern came from two teachers or twenty, especially when it will later be set against survey estimates. I therefore use loose frequency language ("most", "several", "a few") in the text and give exact counts by profile in Table 6.3. These counts record whether a participant's interview contributed coded material to a theme; they are not measures of intensity.

## 6.2 Theme 1. "The numbers are a week late": trust as timeliness and fit

The first theme concerns what teachers meant when they said they did, or did not, trust the district dashboard. Every one of the 24 participants talked about trust in some form, usually without prompting, and almost always before the interview reached the questions designed to elicit it. What stood out was how rarely teachers talked about trust in the abstract. They did not, for the most part, question the dashboard vendor's competence or the integrity of the district's data team. Instead they described trust as a judgment about whether the data on the screen, on a particular day, was current enough and close enough to their own knowledge of their students to be worth acting on. I organize the theme around three facets: timeliness, fit with other records, and the problem of the "red flag" student the teacher already knew about. A fourth subsection addresses how trust was lost.

### 6.2.1 Timeliness

The phrase that gives the theme its name came from Mr. Boateng, a science teacher in the "Willing but stalled" profile, describing his attempt to use the dashboard's mastery view to regroup students for a review lesson:

> By the time it shows me who didn't get the lab standard, we're two topics on. The numbers are a week late. So I'm looking at a picture of my class from last Thursday, and I already marked the quiz myself on Friday. Why would I go to the dashboard for something I know better than it does? (Mr. Boateng, science, Willing but stalled)

Data latency came up in 19 of the 24 interviews. Teachers described a lag of between a few days and two weeks between an assessment and its appearance in the dashboard, depending on how the assessment was administered and how grades were synchronized from the gradebook. Several had worked out the synchronization schedule for themselves; Ms. Eriksen knew that her district's nightly sync "only picks up things that are marked final, and I don't mark things final until the end of the unit." For her, the practical consequence was that the dashboard was always describing a unit that had already finished.

Timeliness mattered differently for different uses. For the few teachers who used the dashboard mainly to look at long-run patterns, such as attendance over a semester or growth on benchmark assessments, a week's delay was tolerable. Ms. Santos, an "Engaged user", described looking at benchmark trends with her department at the start of each quarter and said that lag "doesn't matter for that, because I'm not trying to decide what to do tomorrow." For the larger number who wanted the dashboard to inform next week's teaching, lag was decisive. It converted the dashboard from a planning tool into a record of the past.

I read this as a first indication that "trust in the data", as measured on the survey, folded together a judgment about accuracy with a judgment about usefulness-in-time. The survey items asked whether teachers believed the dashboard data were accurate, reliable and dependable. A teacher could believe all of those things and still decline to rely on the data because they arrived too late to act on. In the language of {{lee2004|n}}, reliance on an automated aid depends on a match between the aid's capabilities and the demands of the situation, and timeliness was the dimension along which teachers most often found the match wanting.

### 6.2.2 Fit with the gradebook and with what teachers already knew

The second facet of trust concerned agreement between the dashboard and the teacher's other sources of information. Fourteen participants described discrepancies between the dashboard and the gradebook. Some were explicable once traced: an assignment weighted differently in the two systems, a late submission not yet synchronized, an excused absence recorded as a zero. Others were never resolved. Ms. Chen, an English teacher in her third year, described an afternoon spent trying to reconcile the two:

> I had a kid at 58% on the dashboard and 74% in my gradebook. I spent forty minutes, I'm not exaggerating, going assignment by assignment. Two of them were the essay drafts that I don't count, but the dashboard did. Fine. But the rest I just couldn't find. And after that I thought, I can't do this every time. So I go to the gradebook. (Ms. Chen, English, Willing but stalled)

What is notable in Ms. Chen's account, and in several others, is that the discrepancy was not experienced simply as an error in the dashboard. It was experienced as a cost. Each mismatch imposed a reconciliation task, and because teachers regarded their own gradebook as authoritative, the rational response to that cost was to stop consulting the second source. Mr. Hollis put it compactly: "Two clocks that don't agree, you go with your watch."

The comparison between the dashboard and teachers' own knowledge was not only about grades. Teachers also judged the dashboard against their sense of their students. When the dashboard confirmed what they already knew, it was reassuring but redundant. When it disagreed, they usually concluded that the dashboard was wrong. Only a few participants, all of them "Engaged users", described occasions on which the dashboard had shown them something about a student that they had not already noticed and that turned out, on investigation, to be right. Ms. Ibarra, a science teacher in her early career, was one:

> There was a girl who was always so on top of things in class, really participating, and the dashboard had her missing like half the online practice. I almost didn't believe it. But I asked her and it turned out she was looking after her little brother after school and didn't have the laptop. So that was real. That changed how I see it, actually. (Ms. Ibarra, science, Engaged user)

That single experience of the dashboard being right when her own impression was wrong appeared to have shifted Ms. Ibarra's stance considerably. I return in Section 6.7 to the possibility that such moments of surprising accuracy are part of what distinguished the "Engaged" profile.

### 6.2.3 The "red flag" student the teacher already knew

A particular version of the fit problem recurred so often that it became a code of its own. The dashboard's home screen in all four districts displayed a list of students flagged as at risk on one or more indicators, with red, amber and green markers. Eleven participants, across all three profiles, described opening the dashboard to find that the flagged students were exactly the students they were already worried about. Mr. Dawson, a social studies teacher with more than twenty years in the classroom and one of the "Sceptics", was characteristically blunt:

> It gives me a list of six kids in red. I could have told you those six kids in September. I could have told you their older siblings. What it doesn't tell me is anything I don't know. (Mr. Dawson, social studies, Sceptic)

Teachers did not say the flags were wrong. They said the flags were unnecessary. This matters for interpreting the survey, because it suggests that for some teachers low performance expectancy and low trust were entangled: the dashboard was not useful because what it reported was already known, and what it reported was trusted, in a sense, precisely because it was already known. Its accuracy was confirmed by its redundancy. Mr. Lopez, another "Sceptic", observed that the only time the dashboard could tell him something new was when it disagreed with him, "and that's exactly when I trust it least." I found this one of the more analytically useful remarks in the data set, because it captures a bind that a simple accuracy account of trust does not: a tool whose value lies in surprising its user must earn the trust to be believed when it does so.

### 6.2.4 How trust was lost

The final facet of this theme concerns the history of trust rather than its current state. Asked whether their view of the dashboard had changed over time, 13 participants described a specific incident after which they had stopped relying on it, or relied on it much less. The incidents were mundane: a whole class shown as absent on a day the teacher had taught them, a benchmark score attached to the wrong student, a mastery rating that stayed at zero after a student had passed a reassessment. In most cases the error was corrected within days. In most cases the loss of trust was not.

Ms. Kowalski, a social studies teacher in the "Willing but stalled" profile, described what had happened in her second year using the dashboard:

> It had my whole fourth period as not having done the unit test. All of them. And the assistant principal saw it before I did and emailed me asking what was going on. It was a sync thing, it got fixed by the next week. But I remember standing there feeling like I'd been caught out by something that wasn't even true. I never really went back to it the same way. (Ms. Kowalski, social studies, Willing but stalled)

Two features of this account recurred across the incidents. First, the error was visible, and often visible to someone else. Second, the teacher's response was out of proportion to the error's practical consequences, which were usually trivial, but in proportion to its consequences for their confidence. This pattern resembles what {{dietvorst2015|n}} describe as algorithm aversion: people tend to lose confidence in an algorithmic source more quickly after seeing it err than they lose confidence in a human source after a comparable mistake. Teachers in this study forgave their own gradebook errors, and their colleagues', far more readily than they forgave the dashboard's. Mr. Thibault said as much: "If I make a mistake I know why. When it makes a mistake I don't know what else it's getting wrong."

That last remark points to the mechanism. A visible error was not treated as one error. It was treated as evidence about the unseen ones. Because teachers could not inspect how the dashboard produced its numbers, a single mistake licensed doubt about all of them. In {{mayer1995|n}}'s terms, the error was read as information about the dashboard's ability, and with no means of auditing that ability, teachers generalized from the one case they could see.

### 6.2.5 What this theme adds

Theme 1 suggests that, for these teachers, trust in dashboard data was less a stable attitude than a running assessment made against two benchmarks: the clock and the teacher's own records and knowledge. Data that arrived late or disagreed with the gradebook failed the assessment regardless of its underlying accuracy. A single visible error could reset the assessment to a default of doubt that persisted long after the error was fixed. These accounts begin to explain why trust moderated the intention–use path in Chapter 5. A teacher who intended to use the dashboard but did not trust it faced, at each moment of potential use, a reconciliation cost that a high-trust teacher did not face, and that cost was often enough to tip the decision toward the gradebook. Chapter 7 develops this explanation and the related argument that trust in educational data has at least three components: timeliness, accuracy and perceived purpose, the last of which is the subject of Theme 4.

## 6.3 Theme 2. "I don't have a Tuesday for this": time and the structure of the week

If Theme 1 explains why teachers who opened the dashboard often closed it again, Theme 2 explains why many did not open it at all. Nineteen participants described time as the main practical constraint on dashboard use, and for the "Willing but stalled" teachers it was the constraint they named first. What they described, however, was not simply a shortage of time in general. It was the absence of any regular point in the teaching week at which looking at data was the expected activity. Where such a point existed, use followed. Where it did not, use depended on a gap opening up, and in most weeks none did.

### 6.3.1 No protected time

The theme takes its name from an exchange with Mr. Jansen, an English teacher with more than two decades of experience, who had scored high on intention and low on use. I asked him when in a typical week he would look at the dashboard:

> That's the thing. I don't have a Tuesday for this. Monday I'm planning, Wednesday's department, Thursday I've got duty, Friday's Friday. There's no slot where it's the dashboard's turn. So it's whenever, and whenever means never, mostly. I want to. I said I want to on your survey and I meant it. (Mr. Jansen, English, Willing but stalled)

Mr. Jansen's account is close to a summary of the "Willing but stalled" pattern, and it captures something that a facilitating conditions scale measuring access and training would miss. He had access. He had been trained. He reported, credibly, an intention to use the dashboard. What he lacked was a place in the week where the intention could be enacted without displacing something else.

Teachers' descriptions of their weeks were remarkably consistent across districts. Planning periods were described as consumed by the immediate demands of the next lesson: copying, setting up labs, answering parent email, grading. Dashboard work was imagined as something that would happen in a planning period, but it competed with tasks that had a visible deadline, and it lost. Ms. Mbeki, a science teacher early in her career, said that she had "every intention" of looking at the dashboard each Friday afternoon, "but Friday afternoon is when I find out what's broken for Monday."

Several teachers drew a contrast between grading and dashboard work that illuminates why one was protected and the other was not. Grading was owed to someone: to students, to parents, to the reporting calendar. Dashboard work was owed to no one in particular. Its benefits were diffuse and deferred, and no one would notice if it did not happen. This is a point about the structure of obligations in teachers' work more than about time as a quantity, and I return to it in Chapter 7 when considering what facilitating conditions might need to include.

### 6.3.2 The meeting as the occasion of use

The clearest exception to the absence of dashboard time was the professional learning community (PLC) or data meeting. In all four districts, teachers described some version of a regular meeting, typically every two to four weeks, at which a department or grade-level team reviewed student data. For many participants this meeting was the only occasion on which they reliably opened the dashboard, and they described their use as preparation for it. Ms. O'Neill described the rhythm:

> The night before data team I'll go in and pull up my classes so I have something to say. Then in the meeting we look at it together for twenty minutes. Then it's closed until next data team. If you looked at my logins you'd see these little spikes every three weeks. (Ms. O'Neill, social studies, Willing but stalled)

Twelve participants described this meeting-driven pattern explicitly. Their accounts suggest that for a substantial group of teachers the meeting, rather than the teacher's own intention, was the proximal cause of use. Intention was necessary but not sufficient; it had to be met by an institutional occasion.

The quality of the meeting mattered as much as its existence. Teachers in some schools described data meetings that ended with a concrete instructional decision, such as a regrouping, a reteach or a targeted intervention, and in those schools participants were more likely to describe returning to the dashboard between meetings to see whether the decision had worked. Teachers in other schools described meetings that were largely reporting exercises. Mr. Vance's account was typical of the latter:

> We go round the table and everyone says their percentage proficient and whether it went up or down. And then the coach says great, keep going. I'm not sure what anyone does with it after. I'm not sure what I do with it after, honestly. (Mr. Vance, English, Willing but stalled)

Where meetings ended in reporting rather than deciding, use stayed tied to the meeting cycle and did not spread into the rest of the week. This observation connects Theme 2 to Theme 3, because what distinguished generative from perfunctory meetings was largely whether anyone in the room could get from the numbers to a decision. It also connects to the role of leadership, since the design and conduct of these meetings was in the hands of principals, assistant principals and instructional coaches. {{datnow2014|n}} argue that school leaders shape whether data use becomes part of professional practice or remains a compliance routine, and teachers' accounts of their data meetings were consistent with that argument.

### 6.3.3 Effort as time, not difficulty

Theme 2 also sheds light on a smaller puzzle from Chapter 5: why effort expectancy had only a weak relationship with intention (β = .12) when teachers so often described the dashboard as effortful. When teachers talked about effort, they rarely meant that the dashboard was hard to operate. Most found the interface reasonably straightforward once learned, and the "Engaged users" in particular described navigating it quickly. What they meant was that using the dashboard *well* took time: time to filter to the right class, to reconcile it with the gradebook, to interpret what they saw. Ms. Eriksen distinguished the two clearly:

> It's not hard. I can find stuff. It's that every time I go in, it's twenty minutes before I've got something I could use. And I don't have twenty minutes. If it was two minutes I'd be in there every day. (Ms. Eriksen, mathematics, Willing but stalled)

The survey's effort expectancy items asked about ease of learning and ease of operation, in the tradition of {{davis1989|n}} and {{venkatesh2003|n}}. Teachers' accounts suggest that the effort that mattered was the time cost of getting from login to a usable insight, which depended on data quality (Theme 1) and on the availability of next-step guidance (Theme 3) as much as on interface design. If that is right, the weak EE→BI path may reflect a mismatch between what the items measured and the kind of effort teachers experienced, rather than evidence that effort did not matter.

### 6.3.4 Contrasts among the "Engaged users"

The seven "Engaged users" were not immune to time pressure, and most described their weeks in terms similar to those of their "stalled" colleagues. What differed was that each had, in some way, a protected occasion for dashboard work that did not depend on finding spare time. For three, this was a school-level structure: a weekly common planning period in which the team looked at data together and committed to an action. For two, it was a role: Ms. Wright was a department lead, and Mr. Nakamura a coordinator for a ninth-grade intervention program, and each was expected to review data for colleagues as part of that role. For the remaining two, Ms. Alvarez and Ms. Quinn, both mathematics teachers, it was a personal routine they had built and defended, in Ms. Quinn's case "the first ten minutes of my planning period, every day, before I'm allowed to check email."

Ms. Quinn's routine is worth pausing on, because it shows that protected time can be created by an individual as well as by an institution. But she was candid that the routine was easier to sustain because her school's data meetings were useful, her department chair valued the dashboard, and she trusted its data, having found it "right more often than not." In other words, her individual routine rested on conditions that the "stalled" teachers mostly lacked. I would not want to read her case as evidence that the "stalled" teachers simply lacked discipline.

### 6.3.5 What this theme adds

Theme 2 suggests that for many teachers, and for the "Willing but stalled" group in particular, the gap between intention and use was in part a gap in the structure of the working week. Intention was real but had no occasion on which to act. The main occasion that did exist, the data meeting, generated episodic use whose quality depended on how the meeting was run. This theme also offers a plausible explanation for why the facilitating conditions scale explained some, but not more, of the variance in use. FC as measured covered access, equipment and training, conditions that most participants had. The condition that participants described as decisive, protected time attached to an expectation of action, was not on the scale. Chapter 7 treats this as a point of discordance between the strands and considers its implications for how facilitating conditions are conceptualized in acceptance research.

## 6.4 Theme 3. "Show me what to do on Monday": from seeing to acting

The third theme concerns what happened when teachers did open the dashboard, trusted what they saw and had time to look at it. Even then, many described reaching a point at which the dashboard had nothing more to offer. It could show them which students had not mastered a standard, which assignments were missing and how a class compared with the school average. It could not tell them what to do about any of it. Eighteen participants described this gap between seeing and acting, and for several "Willing but stalled" teachers it was the reason they gave for why intention did not translate into sustained use.

### 6.4.1 The dashboard shows what, not what next

Ms. Chen's phrase gives the theme its title. Asked what would make the dashboard more useful, she did not mention speed or accuracy, though she had complained about both earlier in the interview:

> Show me what to do on Monday. That's it. I can see that eleven kids didn't get the thesis statement standard. Great. I knew that roughly. What I don't have is, okay, here's a mini-lesson, here's how to group them, here's what the kids at a 2 need versus the kids at a 1. It stops at the part where I actually need help. (Ms. Chen, English, Willing but stalled)

This sentiment appeared in almost identical forms across subjects. Mr. Hollis described the mastery view as "a very nice picture of the problem." Mr. Patel, a "Sceptic" in science, put it less generously: "It's a thermometer. I don't need a thermometer, I need a doctor." What these accounts share is a sense that the dashboard had been designed to support awareness rather than action. That reading is broadly consistent with the way the research literature has characterized dashboards, as tools for supporting awareness, reflection and sensemaking, with impact on teaching and learning as a further and harder step {{verbert2013}}. It is also consistent with the observation from systematic review that relatively few dashboard studies have examined effects on learning in authentic settings {{schwendimann2017}}. Teachers in this study were, in effect, reporting that the step from sensemaking to impact was left to them.

### 6.4.2 Data literacy as a bridge, unevenly built

Whether teachers could make that step themselves depended heavily on what might be called their data literacy for teaching, in the sense set out by {{mandinach2016|n}}: not only the ability to read a display but the knowledge, skills and dispositions needed to turn what it shows into an instructional decision. Teachers varied widely here, and the variation did not track experience in any simple way. Some of the most experienced teachers in the sample were among the least confident about moving from data to instruction, while some early-career teachers, particularly those whose preparation programs had included assessment design, described the move with ease.

Ms. Alvarez, a mathematics teacher among the "Engaged users", described a routine that illustrates what the bridge looked like when it was in place:

> I look at the item breakdown, not the overall score. If eight kids missed question 4 and question 4 is about negative exponents, that's my warm-up for two days. If it's spread out, I don't reteach, I do stations. It took me a couple of years to get to where I could do that quickly. Nobody showed me, I just figured it out with my department. (Ms. Alvarez, mathematics, Engaged user)

Ms. Alvarez's account is instructive in two respects. First, the bridge she described depended on reading the data at a fine grain, the level of individual items, which the dashboard supported but did not foreground; the item view sat two clicks beneath the class summary that teachers saw on login. Second, she had built the bridge with colleagues rather than through formal training. Several "Engaged users" described similar collaborative routes to competence. By contrast, most "Willing but stalled" teachers described their dashboard training as having covered how to navigate the interface and not how to use what it showed. Mr. Thibault recalled a district training session that "spent an hour on where the buttons are and five minutes on what to do with it, and the five minutes was 'differentiate'."

This contrast offers a further explanation for why the facilitating conditions scale did not capture the conditions that mattered most. Teachers who reported having had training, and who therefore scored reasonably well on FC, had often received training of a kind that did not support use beyond the interface. The survey item asked whether teachers had received training; it could not ask what kind.

### 6.4.3 Subject and the shape of the data

The gap between seeing and acting was not equally wide across subjects. Mathematics teachers were more likely than others to describe the dashboard's mastery and item-level data as mapping directly onto instructional decisions, perhaps because the mathematics curriculum in these districts was organized around discrete, sequenced standards that the dashboard's categories mirrored. English and social studies teachers were more likely to describe a mismatch between what the dashboard measured and what they cared about. Mr. Nakamura, an "Engaged user" in English, used the dashboard heavily for reading growth data but said he "wouldn't go near it for writing, because what it can count about writing isn't what matters about writing." Ms. Greer, a "Sceptic" in English, made a similar point more sharply, arguing that the standards view "turns an essay into a row of checkboxes."

I do not want to overstate this pattern from a sample of 24, in which subject and profile are partly confounded: four of the seven "Engaged users" taught mathematics or science, and the English teachers in the sample were spread across all three profiles. But the pattern is consistent with the argument that analytics are useful to teachers to the extent that they are grounded in a model of learning, and that the measures a tool chooses carry assumptions about what learning is {{gasevic2015}}. Where the dashboard's implicit model of learning matched the teacher's, as with sequenced mathematics standards, the step from seeing to acting was shorter. Where it did not, the step was longer and sometimes refused.

### 6.4.4 The wish for suggestions, and its limits

Most of the teachers who described the seeing–acting gap said they would welcome instructional suggestions linked to the data: resources, grouping recommendations, examples of reteaching approaches. Their wishes were, however, carefully qualified. Several said they would want suggestions they could ignore, and two were explicit that they would distrust suggestions generated automatically. Ms. Eriksen said she would use "a menu, not a script," and Mr. Rossi worried that a dashboard that told teachers what to do "would be the next thing we get evaluated on, did you do what the dashboard said." That worry anticipates Theme 4.

The pattern of these qualifications suggests that teachers wanted the dashboard to shorten the path from data to decision without taking the decision away from them. It also connects to the discussion of reliance in Theme 1. Teachers who had seen the dashboard err were wary of extending their reliance on it from description to prescription, and their requests for "a menu, not a script" can be read as a request for a form of support that preserved their ability to calibrate how far to rely on it {{lee2004}}. This is a narrower and more specific request than a general wish for "more useful" dashboards, and I return to it in the design implications in Chapter 8.

### 6.4.5 What this theme adds

Theme 3 helps explain why the "Willing but stalled" teachers could report high performance expectancy and high intention yet low use. They believed in principle that the dashboard could improve their teaching, and they intended to use it, but each encounter ended at the point where they needed to decide what to do, and without a bridge from display to decision the encounter produced little. In the terms of the survey, the dashboard's performance expectancy was partly a promise that each use failed to keep. This interpretation also suggests that some of what the model labels "use" may be shallow use: logging in, viewing, closing. I take this up in Chapter 7 when discussing whether the construct of use needs to distinguish viewing from acting.

## 6.5 Theme 4. "Who is this for?": surveillance and professional judgment

The fourth theme concerns teachers' understanding of the purpose of the dashboard, and in particular their uncertainty about who else was looking at the same data and what they were doing with it. Fourteen participants raised this concern, and it was more prominent among the "Sceptics" and the "Willing but stalled" teachers than among the "Engaged users". It was also the theme that participants were most hesitant to discuss, and several returned to it only near the end of the interview, after I had asked whether there was anything they had not yet said.

### 6.5.1 The same screen, a different reader

The dashboards in all four districts gave administrators access to the same class-level views that teachers saw, along with aggregate views across classes and teachers. Most participants knew this; some had learned it the hard way. Ms. Kowalski's account of the assistant principal who saw the missing unit test before she did (Section 6.2.4) was one of five similar stories. In each, a teacher learned that an administrator had been looking at their class data, usually because the administrator raised a question about it, and the teacher experienced the question as an implicit evaluation.

Ms. Greer, a "Sceptic", framed the issue directly:

> Who is this for? Because they told us it's for us, to help us help the kids. But my principal can see my red list, and my red list is longer than the teacher next door because I teach the inclusion section. So what does that look like from the office? I don't know, and nobody's ever told me. (Ms. Greer, English, Sceptic)

The problem Ms. Greer described is not that the data were wrong but that their meaning depended on context the dashboard did not show. A class with more students flagged as at risk might reflect a harder teaching assignment rather than weaker teaching, and teachers feared that administrators reading the dashboard would not, or could not, make that distinction. In this sense, concerns about surveillance were also concerns about interpretation: about whether the reader of the data would exercise professional judgment, or substitute the data for it.

### 6.5.2 Purpose as a component of trust

Participants' concerns about purpose bore directly on their trust in the data, though not in the way that the survey's trust items conceived it. The items asked about the data's accuracy and dependability. Teachers who doubted the dashboard's purpose did not necessarily doubt the numbers; they doubted what the numbers would be used for. Mr. Young, a science teacher among the "Sceptics", made the distinction himself:

> I'm not saying the numbers are made up. I'm saying I don't know whose side they're on. If it's to help me, fine. If it's to build a file on me, then I'm careful what goes in it. (Mr. Young, science, Sceptic)

"Whose side they're on" is a striking way of putting a point that the trust literature makes in more formal language. In {{mayer1995|n}}'s integrative model, trust involves a willingness to be vulnerable to another party based on beliefs about that party's ability, benevolence and integrity. Theme 1 showed teachers reasoning about the dashboard's ability. Theme 4 shows them reasoning about benevolence and integrity, though the party whose benevolence was in question was less the tool than the institution that deployed it. The vulnerability involved was real: teachers' data could be read by those who evaluated them. I came to see purpose as a third component of trust in educational data, alongside timeliness and accuracy, and Chapter 7 develops that three-part account and its relation to the survey's trust construct.

### 6.5.3 Strategic engagement and strategic avoidance

Concerns about purpose did not lead teachers to a single response. Some avoided the dashboard as far as they could, reasoning that what they did not engage with could not be held against them. Mr. Lopez described his approach as "the minimum to show I've been in there." Others engaged with the dashboard strategically, attending to the views they believed administrators watched. Mr. Rossi, a "Willing but stalled" English teacher, described updating missing assignments before data meetings "so my class doesn't light up red," a practice that improved how his data looked without, he admitted, changing anything for students. Ms. Umar, a "Sceptic" in mathematics, described a similar habit and called it "housekeeping for the principal."

A smaller group, mostly "Engaged users", described working in schools where these concerns had been addressed directly. Ms. Santos said her principal had "said out loud, in a staff meeting, that she would never use the dashboard in an evaluation, and then she didn't." Mr. Farouk described a school data team that had agreed norms for how class-level data could be discussed:

> We have a rule in data team that you can talk about the kids but you don't compare teachers. Sounds small. But it means I can put my bad numbers on the table and nobody's going to make it about me. That's what makes it work. (Mr. Farouk, science, Engaged user)

In both cases, the teachers attributed their willingness to engage openly with the data partly to these explicit commitments. The contrasts are consistent with the argument that school leaders shape whether data use is experienced as collaborative inquiry or as accountability {{datnow2014}}. They also echo the concern in the learning analytics ethics literature that analytics systems carry relations of power, and that questions of transparency, consent and purpose cannot be separated from questions of use {{slade2013}}. That literature has focused largely on students as the subjects of analytics. Teachers in this study described themselves as subjects too, and the strategic behaviors described above, avoidance on one side and cosmetic updating on the other, are both ways in which the subjects of a monitoring system adapt to being monitored.

### 6.5.4 What this theme adds

Theme 4 suggests that some low use, particularly among "Sceptics", reflected not indifference but caution grounded in uncertainty about how dashboard data would be used to judge teachers. It also suggests that some recorded use, such as Mr. Rossi's pre-meeting updating, reflected impression management rather than instructional engagement. And it extends the account of trust from Theme 1: alongside judgments of timeliness and accuracy, teachers judged the purpose to which the data would be put, and that judgment depended on leadership practices rather than on the tool. The survey's trust items, which focused on data accuracy, may therefore have captured only part of what trust meant to teachers, a possibility that bears on the interpretation of the moderation effect and that Chapter 7 examines.

## 6.6 Theme 5. Workarounds as use

The fifth theme emerged less from what teachers said about the dashboard than from what they described doing with its data. Fifteen participants, including all seven "Engaged users", described practices in which dashboard data were taken out of the dashboard and used elsewhere: exported to spreadsheets, copied onto paper, screenshotted for parents, pasted into planning documents. These practices were rarely described as "using the dashboard", and several participants mentioned them only when asked directly whether they did anything with the data outside the dashboard itself. Yet they were, in many cases, the most consequential uses that teachers reported.

### 6.6.1 Exports, screenshots and paper

The most common workaround was exporting class data to a spreadsheet. Ms. Quinn exported her mastery data at the end of each unit into a spreadsheet she had built herself, in which she combined it with her own quiz scores and color-coded students for regrouping. She explained why:

> The dashboard can't hold my stuff. My exit tickets aren't in there. So I pull its numbers out and put them next to mine and then I can actually see. After that I don't go back to the dashboard for that unit. I'm in my spreadsheet. (Ms. Quinn, mathematics, Engaged user)

Other workarounds served communication. Ms. Wright regularly took screenshots of individual students' progress views to share with parents in conferences, because, as she put it, "a picture of a line going up is worth ten minutes of me explaining." Mr. Boateng, despite his doubts about timeliness, printed the class mastery view at the start of each unit and annotated it by hand as he marked student work, using it as a checklist rather than as a source of current data. Ms. Ibarra kept a paper copy of the at-risk list on her desk and updated it from her own observations, "so it's the dashboard's list plus what I know."

These workarounds served purposes that the dashboard did not support directly: combining dashboard data with the teacher's own records, adapting it for an audience other than the teacher, or anchoring it to the moment-to-moment work of teaching. In several cases the workaround was also a response to the problems described in Themes 1 and 3. Exporting data and merging it with one's own records addressed the fit problem by making the teacher's records the frame of reference. Annotating a printout addressed the timeliness problem by making the teacher the one who kept the data current. And building a regrouping spreadsheet, as Ms. Quinn did, was itself a bridge from seeing to acting, constructed by the teacher because the dashboard did not supply one.

### 6.6.2 Use the logs do not see

The relevance of these practices to the study's measurement puzzle is direct. The platform logs used in Phase 1 recorded logins per month. A teacher who logged in once at the end of a unit, exported a file and worked from it for three weeks would appear in the logs as a light user, though their engagement with dashboard data might be greater than that of a colleague who logged in weekly to glance at the home screen. Ms. Quinn, whose spreadsheet routine was among the most elaborate in the sample, estimated that she logged in "maybe four or five times a month" outside her daily ten-minute check, and noted that the daily check often consisted of "opening it, seeing nothing's changed, closing it."

Several participants were aware of the mismatch between logged activity and actual use, and found it ironic. Mr. Nakamura said: "If they're counting logins they'll think I barely use it. I use it more than anyone in my department. I just don't use it in there." The pattern suggests one reason why self-reported use and logged logins in Chapter 5 shared only a moderate correlation (r = .58). Self-report, at least for teachers like Mr. Nakamura, plausibly captured engagement with dashboard data that the logs did not. Logins, conversely, may have overstated the use of teachers who logged in only to prepare for meetings, as Ms. O'Neill described in Section 6.3.2, or to tidy their data before administrators saw it, as Mr. Rossi did.

The six survey use items asked about the frequency of specific dashboard actions, including viewing class summaries and exporting reports, so the self-report measure did capture some of these workarounds at the point of export. But the downstream use of exported data, which is where the instructional decisions described by Ms. Quinn and Ms. Alvarez took place, was captured by neither measure.

### 6.6.3 Workarounds across profiles

Workarounds were not evenly distributed. All seven "Engaged users" described at least one, six of the eleven "Willing but stalled" teachers did, and only two of the six "Sceptics" did. Among the "stalled" teachers, workarounds tended to be simpler and less regular: an occasional screenshot for a parent, a printed list for a data meeting. Among "Engaged users" they were frequently routinized and central to how the teacher used data at all.

This distribution suggests that workarounds were less a substitute for use than a form of advanced use, a sign that the teacher had found ways to make dashboard data fit their practice. I find it helpful to think about this in terms of {{rogers2003|n}}'s observation that adopters often modify an innovation in the course of implementing it, and that such re-invention can make an innovation more compatible with existing practice. The "Engaged users" had, in effect, re-invented the dashboard as one input into a data practice that they controlled. The "stalled" teachers mostly had not, and so remained dependent on a tool that, for the reasons set out in Themes 1 to 3, did not fit their work.

### 6.6.4 What this theme adds

Theme 5 suggests that the study's measures of use, both self-reported and logged, captured use inside the dashboard better than use of dashboard data. For the teachers who engaged most deeply with the data, much of that engagement happened elsewhere. This has two implications that Chapter 7 develops. First, it partly explains the moderate correlation between self-report and logs, and cautions against treating logins as a gold standard of use. Second, it suggests that "use" in acceptance research on educational data tools may need to be conceptualized more broadly, to include the downstream uses of the data that a tool makes available and not only interactions with the tool itself.

The themes have so far been presented one at a time. This section draws them together by profile, to show how they combined differently for the three groups identified in Chapter 5. The counts should be read with the caution noted in Section 6.1.3, but the qualitative contrasts behind them are clearer than the numbers alone convey.

For the **"Willing but stalled"** teachers, the first four themes tended to appear together and to compound one another. A typical account ran as follows. The teacher wanted to use the dashboard and believed it could help. There was no regular time to do so outside data meetings. When they did open it, the data were late or did not match the gradebook, which imposed a reconciliation cost. When they got past that, the dashboard did not help them decide what to do. And in the background was some uncertainty about who else was watching. Each barrier on its own might have been surmountable. Together they made each attempt at use expensive and its payoff uncertain, so that intention persisted while use did not. Mr. Jansen's sentence, "whenever means never, mostly", stands for the group.

For the **"Engaged users"**, the same barriers were present but had in each case been mitigated: by a protected occasion for data work, by a bridge from data to decision built with colleagues, by explicit leadership commitments about purpose, and by workarounds that moved the data into a frame the teacher controlled. Engaged users also more often described an experience in which the dashboard had shown them something true that they had not known, as Ms. Ibarra did. Such experiences seemed to anchor a more forgiving stance toward the dashboard's errors, in contrast to the lasting doubt that followed a visible error for teachers who had no such experience to set against it.

For the **"Sceptics"**, the defining feature was not the presence of barriers, which they mostly shared, but a prior judgment that the dashboard offered little worth the effort. Their accounts were dominated by Theme 1 in its "red flag" form, the claim that the dashboard told them nothing they did not know, and by Theme 4. Several were long-serving teachers who had seen previous data systems come and go; Mr. Dawson described the dashboard as "the fourth one of these I've been trained on." Their skepticism did not appear to rest on difficulty with technology. It rested on beliefs about what counts as knowing a student, and about the purposes of institutional data. This resembles what {{ertmer1999|n}} called second-order barriers to technology integration: barriers rooted in teachers' beliefs about teaching and learning rather than in external resources, and correspondingly less responsive to the provision of resources alone.

For "Engaged users", each step was mitigated (protected time, collegial data routines, explicit governance commitments, workarounds under the teacher's control).

## 6.8 Disconfirming cases and reflexive considerations

Not every participant fit their profile's pattern, and the exceptions help mark the limits of the account above. Two are worth noting. Mr. Patel, classified as a "Sceptic", turned out in interview to be an enthusiastic user of data who had set the district dashboard aside because he had built his own tracking system, which he regarded as better suited to his laboratory-based course. His low reported use and low performance expectancy reflected a preference for an alternative tool rather than skepticism about data as such; his "thermometer" remark in Section 6.4.1 was a criticism of this particular tool, not of measurement. Conversely, Ms. Mbeki, a "Willing but stalled" teacher, described conditions much like those of the "Engaged users", including a data team that ended its meetings with decisions and a principal who had made clear commitments about evaluation, and attributed her low use mostly to the demands of her first years of teaching. These cases suggest that the profiles, while analytically useful, group together teachers whose reasons for similar survey responses differ, and that the account of the "stalled" pattern in Section 6.7 describes a common configuration rather than a universal one.

The sample's composition also limits the account. No teacher from the "other subjects" grouping took part, so the findings cannot speak to teachers of the arts, world languages or physical education, whose relationship to dashboard data may be quite different. The "Willing but stalled" profile was deliberately over-represented, so the themes most characteristic of that group are likely to be more fully developed than those characteristic of the others. And all participants had agreed, at the end of the survey, to be contacted for interview, which may have favored teachers with stronger views about the dashboard in either direction.

Finally, I should be clear about my own position. I came to this study with experience of working in secondary schools and of being asked, as a practitioner, to use data systems of this kind. That background helped me ask questions that teachers recognized as informed. It also carried the risk that I would hear in their accounts frustrations I had felt myself. I tried to manage that risk by writing a memo after each interview recording my own reactions separately from my analytic notes, by actively seeking disconfirming cases such as those above, and by discussing candidate themes with my committee before finalizing them. Chapter 4 describes these procedures. I cannot claim that they removed my perspective from the analysis, and from the pragmatist stance adopted in this study I would not want to claim that {{morgan2007}}. My aim was to make that perspective visible so that readers can weigh the findings accordingly.

## 6.9 Summary

This chapter has answered RQ2 by showing how teachers explained their use, adaptation and avoidance of the district dashboard. Five themes structured their accounts. Teachers judged dashboard data trustworthy when it was timely and fit what they already knew, and a single visible error could undo that trust for a long time (Theme 1). Use depended on whether the week contained a protected occasion for data work, and for many teachers the only such occasion was a periodic data meeting (Theme 2). Even when teachers opened the dashboard, it showed them what had happened but not what to do next, and their ability to bridge that gap varied with their data literacy and the support of colleagues (Theme 3). Uncertainty about who else used the data, and for what purpose, made some teachers cautious and others strategic (Theme 4). And much of the most consequential use happened outside the dashboard, through workarounds that logs did not capture (Theme 5).

These findings begin to answer the puzzles that Chapter 5 handed on. They suggest that the intention–use gap among the "Willing but stalled" teachers reflected compounding barriers of time, data quality and actionability rather than weak or insincere intention. They suggest that trust, as teachers experienced it, involved timeliness and perceived purpose as well as accuracy, which may help explain why trust moderated the intention–use path. They suggest that the facilitating conditions that mattered most, protected time and support for moving from data to decisions, were not those the survey measured. And they suggest that the moderate agreement between self-reported and logged use reflects, in part, workarounds that place much meaningful use outside the dashboard, as well as some logged activity that involved little instructional use.

These are suggestions, grounded in the accounts of 24 teachers in four districts, and their transferability to other settings is a matter for readers to judge against the context described here. Chapter 7 now brings the two strands together formally, using a joint display to set each quantitative result beside the qualitative findings that bear on it, and drawing meta-inferences of confirmation, expansion and discordance {{fetters2013}}. In doing so it attends to the quality of the inferences that can be drawn from the combined strands, rather than to the quality of each strand alone {{teddlie2009}}.

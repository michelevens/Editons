# Chapter 2: Background and Related Work

This chapter reviews the research on which the study builds and identifies the gap it addresses. The review is organized by argument rather than by source. Section 2.1 traces how code-generating language models were developed and evaluated, and what their evaluation does and does not tell us about professional use. Section 2.2 examines the evidence on productivity, which has come mainly from controlled experiments and usage telemetry. Section 2.3 turns to studies of how programmers actually interact with assistants. Section 2.4 reviews the security of assistant-generated code, and Section 2.5 the research on modern code review, the practice through which most generated code in organizations is checked. Section 2.6 discusses how developer productivity and technical debt should be conceptualized, and Section 2.7 the emerging frameworks for governing AI risk. Section 2.8 draws these strands together and states the gap that motivates the research questions.

## 2.1 Large language models for code

### 2.1.1 From benchmarks of functional correctness to products

The modern generation of AI coding assistants descends from a short sequence of research systems that established both the capability and the way it is measured. {{chen2021codex|n}} introduced Codex, a GPT language model fine-tuned on public code from GitHub, together with HumanEval, a benchmark of hand-written programming problems each accompanied by unit tests. Their central methodological move was to evaluate generated code by functional correctness, whether it passes the tests, rather than by textual similarity to a reference solution. They also showed that the probability of obtaining at least one correct program rises sharply when many samples are drawn for each problem, a result that foreshadowed the generate-and-filter strategies of later systems and, in a sense, the human practice of scanning several suggestions before accepting one.

{{austin2021mbpp|n}} extended this line of work with two further benchmarks, MBPP, a set of short Python problems of the kind an entry-level programmer might solve, and MathQA-Python, and evaluated models ranging over several orders of magnitude in parameter count under both few-shot prompting and fine-tuning. Their finding that synthesis performance improves log-linearly with model size helped establish the expectation, since borne out commercially, that larger models would produce steadily more useful code.

{{li2022alphacode|n}} pushed the approach further with AlphaCode, which tackles competitive programming problems by generating very large numbers of candidate programs, then filtering them against example tests and clustering the survivors to choose a small number of submissions. In simulated contests the system performed at roughly the level of a median human competitor. The significance of this result for the present study lies less in the ranking than in the architecture: AlphaCode's performance depended on an automated verification step that discarded the great majority of what the model produced. Professional programmers using an assistant have no such filter except their own judgment, their tests and their colleagues' review.

### 2.1.2 What benchmark performance does not show

These benchmarks share features that limit what they can say about professional software engineering. The problems are self-contained, specified in a paragraph and checkable by a handful of tests. Real tasks in an organization are embedded in large codebases with implicit conventions, depend on internal libraries the model may never have seen, and are rarely fully specified at the moment code is written. A function that passes its unit tests may still violate an architectural rule, duplicate an existing utility, mishandle a rare input, or introduce a vulnerability that no test exercises. Benchmark correctness is therefore a necessary but very partial proxy for the usefulness of generated code in a team setting. This observation is central to the present study, which treats the ease of verifying a team's work, here called task verifiability, as a condition that may determine how much value the assistant's output actually delivers.

### 2.1.3 Training data, licensing and provenance

A second strand of the model literature concerns the data on which code models are trained. Early systems were trained on large scrapes of public repositories with limited documentation of licensing. {{kocetkov2022stack|n}} responded by releasing The Stack, a multi-terabyte dataset of permissively licensed source code in 30 programming languages, accompanied by a data governance plan that allows developers to request removal of their code. They reported that models trained only on permissively licensed data could match previously reported results on HumanEval and MBPP, suggesting that restricting training data on legal and ethical grounds need not sacrifice capability. {{li2023starcoder|n}} then trained StarCoder and StarCoderBase on this data, releasing the models openly with tools for inspecting whether generated code resembles training data and a process for opting out.

This work matters for an organizational study because it shows that the provenance of generated code is an open question that no individual team can answer by inspecting its own output. A company that adopts a commercial assistant inherits whatever choices the vendor made about training data. Chapter 6 returns to it as an issue that sits above the team-level model proposed in this thesis.

## 2.2 Evidence on productivity

### 2.2.1 Controlled experiments

The most influential evidence that assistants improve productivity comes from a controlled experiment by {{peng2023copilot|n}}. Developers recruited for the study were asked to implement an HTTP server in JavaScript; those randomly given access to GitHub Copilot finished the task substantially faster than those without it. The design has clear strengths. Random assignment rules out self-selection, the task was standardized, and completion was judged by a test suite. The result has been widely repeated in industry discussion as evidence of a general productivity gain.

The same design features, however, limit its transfer to organizational settings. The task was greenfield, well specified and of a kind heavily represented in public code, exactly the circumstances in which a model trained on public repositories should do well. Participants worked alone, so the cost of reviewing the code, which in a team falls on someone else, was not measured. The outcome was time to completion rather than the quality, maintainability or security of the resulting code. None of this undermines the finding; it defines its scope. The experiment shows that an assistant can accelerate an individual on a well-specified, easily verified task. It does not show what happens when the task is ambiguous, the code is high-stakes, or the work must pass through review.

### 2.2.2 Telemetry and perceived productivity

A complementary approach links usage data with developers' own assessments. {{ziegler2022maps|n}} surveyed users of GitHub Copilot about its effect on their productivity and compared their responses with telemetry from their use of the tool. They found that the acceptance rate of shown suggestions predicted perceived productivity better than measures of how much accepted code persisted unchanged in the codebase. The journal version of the study, {{ziegler2024cacm|n}}, presents the same case study to a wider audience and discusses its implications for measuring the impact of assistants.

The finding is subtle and has two readings. On one reading, acceptance rate is a convenient, valid signal of usefulness that organizations can monitor. On another, it suggests that the experience of being helped, of having one's typing anticipated, shapes perceived productivity independently of whether the accepted code survives. For the present study the second reading is important, because it implies that self-reports of productivity and repository measures of delivery may diverge, and that both are needed. The survey reported in Chapter 4 asks engineers about perceived productivity precisely so that it can be set against the repository record.

### 2.2.3 The unit-of-analysis problem

Taken together, the productivity evidence shares a unit of analysis: the individual developer. Even telemetry studies that span many users aggregate individual behavior rather than examining teams. Yet the outcomes engineering organizations care about, the delivery of reliable changes to production, are produced collectively. A PR is written by one person, reviewed by others, tested by shared infrastructure and maintained by the team. If the assistant shifts effort from authoring to reviewing, an individual-level study would record a gain that a team-level study might show to be partly offset. If teams develop different norms for using the assistant, an aggregate across teams would average away exactly the variation that most needs explaining. This is the first and most important limitation of existing evidence that the present study seeks to address.

Moving from the individual to the team brings methodological difficulties of its own, which help explain why such studies are scarce. In a field setting the researcher cannot usually randomize who receives the assistant, and the organizations most willing to share data may be those most enthusiastic about the tool. Adoption is often staggered, so that early adopters differ systematically from late ones. Delivery metrics drift over time for reasons unrelated to any tool: teams grow and shrink, products move through release cycles, holidays depress activity, and incidents divert effort. A naive comparison of means before and after adoption attributes all of this drift to the intervention. Quasi-experimental designs offer a partial remedy {{wohlin2012experimentation}}. Where an intervention occurs at a known point in time and outcomes are observed repeatedly before and after it, a segmented regression can estimate the change in level and trend at that point while allowing for the trend that already existed. Where the same intervention reaches several units at once, differences in their responses can be examined against a common background. The case studied in this thesis offers both conditions: a single rollout date for every engineer and three teams with different kinds of work. Chapter 3 explains how these conditions were exploited and what threats they leave unaddressed.

## 2.3 How developers interact with assistants

### 2.3.1 Modes of interaction

Studies of interaction help explain why aggregate productivity effects might vary. {{barke2023grounded|n}} conducted a grounded theory study of programmers solving tasks in several languages with Copilot and found that interactions were bimodal. In acceleration mode, the programmer knows what they want and uses the assistant to get there faster, accepting short completions that match an intention already formed. In exploration mode, the programmer is unsure how to proceed and uses the assistant to generate options, which they then evaluate, adapt or discard. The two modes impose different cognitive demands: acceleration relies on the programmer recognizing correct output at a glance, while exploration requires more deliberate evaluation of unfamiliar code.

This distinction is directly relevant to team differences. A team whose work consists largely of familiar patterns, such as user-interface components following an established design system, should spend more time in acceleration mode and gain more speed. A team whose work involves subtle domain logic, where the correct output cannot be recognized at a glance, should spend more time in exploration or verification and gain less.

### 2.3.2 Usability, expectations and costs

Several studies suggest that the experience of using assistants is more mixed than headline productivity figures imply. {{vaithilingam2022expectation|n}} ran a within-subjects study in which participants completed tasks with and without Copilot. The assistant did not necessarily improve completion time or success rate, yet most participants preferred it, valuing it as a starting point. They also reported difficulty in understanding, editing and debugging generated code. The gap between preference and performance anticipates the divergence between perceived and measured productivity discussed above.

{{liang2024survey|n}} surveyed several hundred developers about their use of AI programming assistants. Developers reported using assistants mainly to reduce keystrokes, finish tasks more quickly and recall syntax. They reported avoiding assistants when the output failed to meet functional or non-functional requirements, or when it was difficult to control the output. The pattern of use, concentrated on routine and syntactic work, and of avoidance, concentrated on requirements that the model cannot see, supports the expectation that assistants will help most where tasks are routine and least where correctness depends on context.

{{mozannar2024cups|n}} developed CUPS, a taxonomy of the activities programmers engage in while interacting with Copilot, distinguishing, for example, the writing of prompts from the reviewing of suggestions and the editing of accepted code. Programmers in their study labeled recordings of their own sessions retrospectively, which revealed inefficiencies and time costs that are not visible in measures of acceptance alone. The study is significant here because it makes visible a cost that productivity studies tend to hide: the time spent reading and checking generated code. In an individual setting this cost falls on the author. In a team setting, part of it may be deferred to review.

### 2.3.3 Conversational assistance and learning

The chat interfaces that now accompany inline completion have been studied less extensively. {{ross2023assistant|n}} evaluated a prototype conversational programming assistant built on a large language model. Participants began with skeptical expectations but came away impressed by the breadth of the assistant's capabilities and the quality of its responses, and saw potential for productivity. Conversational interaction differs from inline completion in that the programmer explicitly frames a question and receives explanation as well as code, which may make it better suited to learning unfamiliar APIs or languages and to exploration in the sense of {{barke2023grounded|n}}.

Whether assistants help or hinder the development of skill is less clear. {{dakhel2023asset|n}} compared Copilot's solutions to fundamental algorithmic problems with human solutions to programming tasks. Human solutions were correct more often, but Copilot's incorrect solutions could be repaired with less effort than incorrect human ones. They concluded that the assistant can be an asset for experienced developers able to detect and fix its errors, but a liability for novices who may accept flawed output without recognizing it. This conclusion frames a question that recurs in the interviews reported in Chapter 5: whether junior engineers gain speed at the cost of learning, and whether senior engineers use the assistant differently because they can judge its output.

## 2.4 Security of assistant-generated code

### 2.4.1 Vulnerabilities in generated code

The security of generated code has received sustained attention. {{pearce2022asleep|n}} prompted Copilot with scenarios designed around high-risk weaknesses in the Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE) and analyzed the resulting programs with a combination of static analysis and manual review. A substantial proportion of the generated programs contained vulnerabilities. The study shows that a model trained on public code can reproduce the insecure patterns present in that code, and that it may do so in exactly the contexts, such as input handling, memory management and cryptography, where the consequences are most serious.

### 2.4.2 The human in the loop

Generated code does not enter production directly; a programmer decides whether to accept it. Two user studies examine what happens when humans are in the loop, and they reach partly different conclusions. {{perry2023insecure|n}} conducted a user study of security-related programming tasks and found that participants with access to an AI assistant wrote significantly less secure code than those without, and were also more likely to believe that their code was secure. The combination of reduced security and increased confidence is particularly concerning for organizations, because it suggests that assistant use may weaken the self-scrutiny on which secure development partly relies.

{{sandoval2023lostatc|n}}, by contrast, studied student programmers implementing a low-level data structure in C with or without LLM assistance and found that the AI-assisted group did not introduce critical security bugs at a substantially higher rate than the control group. The difference between these findings is instructive rather than contradictory. The tasks, languages, participants and assistants differed, and the risk introduced by an assistant plausibly depends on all of these. What the two studies together suggest is that the security effect of an assistant is not fixed but contingent on the task and on how the human evaluates the output.

### 2.4.3 Implications for teams

The security literature, like the productivity literature, is mostly conducted at the level of individuals completing assigned tasks. In organizations, several further layers of defense stand between generated code and production: code review, automated testing and static application security testing (SAST). Whether these layers absorb the additional risk, and whether teams adjust their practices when they perceive it, are empirical questions that existing studies do not address. The present study measures SAST findings per thousand changed lines before and after adoption in each team, and asks engineers how they decide when to trust the assistant with security-sensitive code. Chapter 4 reports that one team's findings rose and then fell, and Chapter 5 explains the change in terms of an explicit team agreement.

## 2.5 Code review as the site of verification

### 2.5.1 Expectations and outcomes of modern code review

Modern code review, the lightweight, tool-supported review of changes before they are merged, is the main mechanism by which organizations check code written by individuals. {{bacchelli2013review|n}} studied review at Microsoft through observation, interviews, surveys and the classification of review comments. Although developers and managers named finding defects as the primary motivation for review, the comments they actually wrote were less often about defects than expected. Review instead delivered knowledge transfer, team awareness and the discussion of alternative solutions. The key challenge reviewers reported was understanding the change: grasping what it did and why.

{{sadowski2018google|n}} examined code review at Google using interviews, a survey and logs of a very large number of reviewed changes. They documented a review culture characterized by small changes and quick turnaround, with attention to readability and shared conventions, and they examined developers' motivations for review, their satisfaction with it and the challenges they faced. Their account highlights that review practices are themselves organizational norms, which vary between and within companies and which shape how much effort a change demands.

### 2.5.2 Why review matters for assistant adoption

These studies suggest two mechanisms through which an assistant might affect review. The first concerns understanding. If the main challenge of review is understanding the change, then code that its author did not fully write, and may not fully understand, should be harder to review. A reviewer of an assistant-heavy PR is in some sense reviewing a stranger's code, with the author able to explain the intention but not always the implementation. The second mechanism concerns size. If an assistant makes it cheaper to produce code, PRs may grow, and larger changes are harder to understand and slower to review. The emphasis on small changes in the practices described by {{sadowski2018google|n}} exists precisely because size and comprehensibility are linked.

Neither mechanism has, to my knowledge, been examined empirically in teams using AI assistants [source needed]. The present study measures median change size and mean review comments per PR in each team, and the survey asks engineers whether reviewing teammates' PRs has become harder. The interview theme reported in Chapter 5 as "reviewing a stranger's code" examines how engineers experienced the shift.

## 2.6 Measuring productivity and quality

### 2.6.1 Beyond single metrics

Any study of whether a tool improves productivity must decide what productivity means. {{forsgren2021space|n}} argue that developer productivity cannot be captured by a single metric or dimension and propose the SPACE framework, which organizes measures into five dimensions: satisfaction and well-being; performance, meaning the outcomes of work; activity, meaning counts of actions or outputs; communication and collaboration; and efficiency and flow. They recommend that organizations combine measures from several dimensions, include perceptual measures alongside system data, and beware of single activity metrics that can be gamed or misread.

The framework is directly relevant to the evaluation of assistants. Most productivity evidence reviewed in Section 2.2 concerns activity (tasks completed) or efficiency (time to completion). An assistant might raise activity while degrading collaboration, if reviews become burdensome, or performance, if quality falls. It might raise perceived satisfaction while leaving measured efficiency unchanged, as {{vaithilingam2022expectation|n}} observed in a laboratory setting. A study that reads only one dimension could reach misleading conclusions. Chapter 6 uses SPACE to organize the interpretation of the results, mapping each repository and survey measure to the dimension it represents.

### 2.6.2 Delivery performance

The research reported by {{forsgren2018accelerate|n}} drew on several years of survey data to examine how software delivery performance can be measured and what drives it. It established a small set of measures, commonly called the DORA metrics, covering how often teams deploy, how long changes take to reach production, how often changes fail and how quickly service is restored. The value of this work for the present study is twofold. It provides a conceptual basis for treating cycle time as an indicator of delivery speed, and it pairs throughput with stability, so that a gain in speed accompanied by a loss of reliability is not read as an improvement. This study follows that logic at the level of PRs, pairing throughput and cycle time with reverts and security findings. As Chapter 1 explained, organization-level deployment metrics could not be attributed to individual teams in the case company and are therefore not used directly.

### 2.6.3 Technical debt

The final strand concerns the longer-term consequences of how code is written. {{cunningham1992wycash|n}} introduced the metaphor of technical debt in an experience report, describing how shipping code that does not yet reflect a full understanding of the problem is like borrowing: it accelerates delivery now but incurs interest that must eventually be repaid through rework. {{kruchten2012td|n}} later argued for moving the concept from metaphor toward theory and practice, distinguishing visible from invisible forms of debt and calling for ways to identify and manage it systematically.

AI assistants plausibly affect technical debt in both directions. They can make it cheaper to write tests and documentation, which reduces some forms of debt. They can also make it cheaper to produce code that works but is poorly understood by the team that must maintain it, which may create a form of invisible debt that the metaphor anticipates well. Twenty-six weeks is too short to observe debt being repaid, but the study can examine leading indicators: the size of changes, the proportion of test code written alongside production code, and engineers' accounts of whether they understand the code they merge. Chapter 6 discusses the adoption model in terms of debt risk as well as productivity.

## 2.7 Governing AI in software development

Organizations adopting AI tools increasingly look to risk management frameworks for guidance. The NIST Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework {{nist2023airmf}} is a voluntary framework organized around four functions, govern, map, measure and manage, intended to help organizations identify and address the risks of AI systems across their life cycle. It is written for AI systems in general rather than for coding assistants specifically, and it does not prescribe particular controls. Its relevance here is as a vocabulary for organizational governance: it emphasizes that risk depends on context of use, that measurement should be appropriate to that context, and that responsibility for managing risk should be assigned explicitly.

That emphasis aligns with a gap in the empirical literature. The studies reviewed above treat the assistant as an intervention and the developer as its recipient. They rarely examine the norms that teams create for themselves: rules about when the assistant may be used, how assistant-generated code should be labeled, what must be tested, and who is responsible when it fails. If, as the present study argues, such norms explain much of the difference between teams, then governance is not a layer added after adoption but part of the mechanism through which adoption produces its effects. Chapter 6 offers recommendations framed in the functions of the NIST framework.

## 2.8 Synthesis and research gap

The literature reviewed in this chapter supports several conclusions and leaves several questions open.

It shows, first, that code-generating models can produce functionally correct code for well-specified problems, and that their usefulness depends heavily on the availability of verification, whether by tests, by filtering or by human judgment (Section 2.1). It shows that individuals can complete well-specified tasks faster with an assistant and that their perception of productivity tracks how often they accept suggestions (Section 2.2). It shows that programmers interact with assistants in distinct modes, value them as starting points while finding their output hard to understand and debug, and spend substantial time verifying suggestions (Section 2.3). It shows that generated code can contain vulnerabilities and that assistant use may, in some tasks, lower the security of code while raising confidence in it (Section 2.4). It shows that code review is primarily a practice of understanding changes, and that size and comprehensibility are linked (Section 2.5). And it shows that productivity should be read across several dimensions and that short-term gains can carry longer-term costs (Section 2.6).

What the literature does not yet provide is evidence on how these mechanisms play out in teams within a working organization, over a period long enough for practices to adapt. Three gaps are particularly relevant.

The first is the unit of analysis. Almost all evidence concerns individuals completing tasks. The consequences of an assistant for a team, including the redistribution of effort between authoring and review, are rarely measured.

The second is variation between teams. If the benefit of an assistant depends on the verifiability of the task and the cost of error, then teams with different work should experience different effects. Aggregate studies cannot detect this, and controlled experiments, by standardizing the task, deliberately remove it.

The third is the role of norms. The literature treats assistant use as an individual behavior shaped by the tool and the task. It has little to say about the explicit agreements teams make about how the assistant should be used, or about whether those agreements change outcomes.

This thesis addresses these gaps by following three teams at one company through the same adoption, combining repository measures across several SPACE dimensions with engineers' accounts of their practices, and asking which team-level conditions explain the differences. RQ1 responds to the first gap by measuring team-level delivery and quality indicators. RQ2 responds to the findings of the interaction studies by asking how engineers' practices of writing, reviewing and testing changed. RQ3 responds to the second and third gaps by asking which conditions, including verifiability, stakes and norms, explain the differences between teams.

The next chapter describes how the study was designed to answer these questions, beginning with the case and its teams and moving through the repository, survey and interview methods to the strategy for integrating them.

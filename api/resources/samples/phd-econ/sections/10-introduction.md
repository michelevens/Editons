# Chapter 1: Introduction

For most of the history of modern labor markets, a worker's paycheck and a worker's grocery bill came from the same place. People lived within commuting distance of their employers, and the income generated by a city's firms was, to a first approximation, spent in that city's shops, restaurants, barbershops and rental housing. Urban economists built a large part of their toolkit on this coincidence. A local economy could be divided into an export base, which sells goods and services to the rest of the world and brings income in, and a non-tradable sector, which recirculates that income locally. A new factory was good news for the hairdressers down the road because the factory's workers needed haircuts.

The rapid spread of remote work since 2020 has loosened the link between where income is earned and where it is spent. A software developer employed by a firm headquartered in one metropolitan area can now live several hundred miles away, collect a salary set in a national or even international labor market, and spend most of it in a town whose economy has nothing to do with software. From the point of view of the town, the developer is a curious object. She brings income from outside, as an exporter does, but she produces nothing that the town sells. She competes with local residents for housing, but not for local jobs. She buys local services, but her own wage is unaffected by local conditions.

This dissertation asks what happens to a local labor market when workers of this kind arrive in numbers. Specifically, when remote-capable workers relocate to a local labor market, bringing incomes earned elsewhere, what happens to local non-tradable service employment, to wages in those services and to housing rents, and who gains? The answer matters for at least three reasons. It matters for the many local governments that have begun courting remote workers in the hope of reviving their economies. It matters for incumbent residents, especially renters working in low-wage service jobs, whose living standards depend on the race between local wages and local rents. And it matters for economics, because remote workers offer an unusually clean case of a local demand shock that operates through spending alone, separated from the production and agglomeration channels that complicate the study of conventional export-base shocks.

The chapter proceeds as follows. Section 1.1 develops the motivation in more detail. Section 1.2 states the research questions. Section 1.3 sets out the central conceptual idea, that remote workers form a new kind of export base, and explains what a spatial-equilibrium model predicts about their effects. Section 1.4 summarizes the empirical strategy, Section 1.5 previews the main findings, and Section 1.6 describes the contributions. Section 1.7 discusses scope and limitations, and Section 1.8 outlines the remaining chapters.

## 1.1 Income earned elsewhere, spent here

The occupational potential for remote work was large before the pandemic made it salient. Classifying occupations by the tasks they involve, {{dingel2020|n}} conclude that a substantial minority of US jobs could plausibly be performed entirely at home, that these jobs pay more than average, and that their prevalence differs considerably across cities and industries. Until 2020, little of this potential was used. The pandemic then forced a sudden, near-universal experiment. Survey evidence collected in its early months showed that a large share of previously employed workers had switched from commuting to working from home, with larger shifts in places whose occupational mix made remote work more feasible {{brynjolfsson2020}}.

What turned a temporary emergency into a structural change was persistence. {{barrero2021stick|n}} argued early on that working from home would remain far more common than before the pandemic, because workers and employers had discovered that it worked better than expected, had invested in the equipment and practices it required, and no longer attached the same stigma to it. Later evidence from the same research program indicates that the share of workdays performed at home settled at a level several times higher than its pre-pandemic level and stayed there {{barrero2023evolution}}. Remote and hybrid work are now ordinary features of the labor market rather than a pandemic curiosity.

Once a worker no longer needs to be in the office every day, the set of places in which she can live expands. For hybrid workers, the expansion is modest: they can live farther out within the same metropolitan area, accepting a longer commute on fewer days. {{ramani2021|n}} document exactly this pattern, a "donut" in which population, business activity and real estate demand moved from dense city centers toward suburbs while staying within commuting range. Housing markets registered the change: prices and rents in city centers fell relative to those farther out, flattening the bid-rent curve, especially where remote work was more prevalent and housing supply less elastic {{gupta2022}}. For fully remote workers, the expansion is larger. They can move to an entirely different labor market, and some have.

These moves have an obvious mirror image. The places that remote-capable workers leave lose spending. {{althoff2022|n}} show that dense cities specialize in high-skill service jobs that can be done remotely, and that when those workers went remote their spending on local consumer services fell, leaving low-skill service workers in large cities to absorb much of the shock. The places they move to gain spending. Yet the second half of this story has received far less attention than the first. The literature knows a good deal about the hollowing out of downtown lunch trade; it knows much less about what happens in the smaller and mid-sized labor markets that receive remote workers, where the inflow may be large relative to the size of the local economy.

That gap is the starting point for this dissertation. The receiving side of remote-worker migration is not simply the downtown story in reverse. A remote worker who leaves a large city takes her spending with her but leaves her employer, her productivity spillovers and her firm's local supply chain behind. A remote worker who arrives in a smaller market brings spending but nothing else. The economic consequences for the receiving market therefore depend on how a local economy responds to a pure income injection, mediated by the elasticity of its housing supply and the responsiveness of its labor supply. These are classic questions in urban economics, but remote work poses them in a new and unusually clean form.

There is also a distributional reason to care about the receiving side. The workers who can work remotely and the workers who serve them are, to a large extent, different people. {{mongey2021|n}} show that workers in jobs that cannot be done from home and that involve physical proximity to others are less educated, lower paid, hold fewer liquid assets and are more likely to rent their homes. These are precisely the workers who staff the restaurants, shops and building trades that a remote-worker inflow would be expected to expand. If arrivals create service jobs and raise service wages, these workers stand to gain. If arrivals instead mainly raise rents, the same workers stand to lose, because as renters they bear the cost of higher housing prices without sharing in the capital gains. The welfare consequences of remote-worker migration for receiving markets therefore turn on a horse race between local wages and local rents, and the outcome of that race is an empirical matter.

The question is also of practical interest. Smaller labor markets across the country have experimented with programs to attract remote workers, from relocation grants to co-working subsidies, on the premise that the new arrivals will support local businesses and create jobs [source needed]. Whether that premise holds, how large the job gains are, and whether they are eaten up by higher housing costs for incumbent residents are empirical questions on which policy makers currently have little systematic evidence to draw.

## 1.2 Research questions

The dissertation addresses four connected research questions.

1. **RQ1.** Does the arrival of remote-capable workers raise employment in the receiving market's non-tradable service sectors, and if so, by how much and how quickly?
2. **RQ2.** How large is the local multiplier from remote workers, that is, how many additional local non-tradable jobs accompany each additional remote worker?
3. **RQ3.** How do non-tradable wages, housing rents and house prices respond, and how do these responses depend on the elasticity of local housing supply and on local amenities?
4. **RQ4.** Who gains and who loses within the receiving market? In particular, do the wage gains of incumbent service workers keep pace with the rent increases they face?

The questions are ordered from quantities to prices to welfare. RQ1 and RQ2 concern the size of the local demand response, and they are answered in Chapter 5. RQ3 concerns how that demand is accommodated, by expanding supply or by raising prices, and RQ4 asks what the combination of quantity and price responses implies for different groups of residents. Both are answered in Chapter 6. Chapter 3 derives each of the empirical relationships from a formal model before any data are examined, so that the estimates in later chapters can be read as tests of explicit predictions rather than as a collection of reduced-form correlations.

A fifth, methodological question runs alongside the substantive ones: how much does the choice of difference-in-differences estimator matter when treatment is staggered over time and effects grow after treatment? The arrival of remote workers is exactly the kind of setting in which the conventional two-way fixed-effects estimator is known to perform badly, and the dissertation documents the size of the resulting bias in an applied context.

## 1.3 Remote workers as an export base

The central conceptual claim of the dissertation is that remote workers constitute a new kind of export base for the market in which they live. Their income is earned by producing tradable output, typically professional, technical or business services, for employers located elsewhere. From the receiving market's perspective, this income arrives from outside, exactly as the revenue of a local manufacturer selling to national markets does. But unlike the manufacturer, the remote worker produces nothing locally that is sold elsewhere, hires no local suppliers and employs no local workers. The only channel through which her income touches the local economy is her spending on locally provided goods and services, chief among them housing.

This framing connects remote work to a well-developed literature on local multipliers. {{moretti2010|n}} estimates that the creation of tradable jobs in a city generates additional non-tradable jobs in the same city, as the new tradable workers and their employers spend locally. {{moretti2012|n}} develops the broader argument that high-skill jobs have especially large local multipliers, because high earners spend more on local services, and that this mechanism has contributed to a growing divergence among American cities. If high-skill tradable jobs create local service jobs through spending, then high-skill remote workers, who bring the spending without the job, should do so too.

There are, however, good reasons to expect the remote-worker multiplier to differ from the conventional one. First, a conventional tradable job brings both a worker and a firm, and the firm's local purchases contribute to the multiplier; a remote worker's employer makes no purchases in the receiving market. Second, conventional export-base shocks may generate agglomeration economies, raising the productivity of other local firms {{duranton2004;rosenthal2004}}, while it is not obvious that a remote worker contributes much to the local exchange of ideas or to local labor pooling. Third, a remote worker's wage does not respond to local conditions. A conventional local labor demand shock raises local wages, which partly offsets the job gains through higher costs for local producers; a remote-worker inflow raises local demand without raising the arriving workers' own wage. Remote workers therefore isolate the spending channel of local multipliers more cleanly than any conventional shock.

To make these intuitions precise, Chapter 3 builds a spatial-equilibrium model in the tradition associated with Rosen and Roback, in which the utility of mobile workers is equalized across locations by adjustments in wages, rents and amenities [source needed]. The model has two production sectors, tradable and non-tradable, and three types of worker: local tradable workers, non-tradable service workers and remote workers. The remote workers' wage, $w^R$, is set nationally and does not depend on where they live. Workers have utility of the form $U = \ln w - \alpha \ln r + A_c$, where $w$ is the wage, $r$ is the local rent, $\alpha$ is the housing expenditure share and $A_c$ is the amenity value of location $c$. Demand for non-tradable services is proportional to local income, and housing is supplied with location-specific elasticity $\eta_c$.

The model yields three main propositions, stated formally in Chapter 3 and proved in Appendix C. The first is that an inflow of remote workers raises local non-tradable employment, with an elasticity that is positive but decreasing in the inverse housing-supply elasticity $1/\eta_c$. The intuition is that where housing supply is inelastic, part of the arriving income is absorbed by higher rents rather than spent on services, and the higher rents also deter some of the additional service workers needed to meet the new demand. The second is that the rent response is increasing in $1/\eta_c$: the same inflow raises rents more where the housing stock is harder to expand. The third concerns incidence. Incumbent homeowners gain unambiguously, while incumbent renters working in non-tradables gain only if the increase in their wage exceeds the increase in their housing costs, which depends on how much of their income they spend on rent.

It is useful to summarize the object of central interest, the local multiplier, in a single expression. Let $N^{NT}_c$ denote non-tradable employment in market $c$ and $R_c$ the number of remote workers living there. The multiplier is

$$
\mu_c = \frac{\Delta N^{NT}_c}{\Delta R_c} = \frac{\Delta \ln N^{NT}_c}{\Delta R_c / N^{NT}_c} \tag{?}
$$

which converts a proportional change in non-tradable employment into jobs per arriving remote worker. Equation (1.1) is the quantity that Chapter 5 estimates by dividing the estimated employment effect by the estimated remote-worker inflow, in the manner of a Wald ratio. The model implies that $\mu_c$ is positive and smaller where $\eta_c$ is low, a prediction tested directly in Chapters 5 and 6.

Two features of this framework deserve emphasis. First, the model is deliberately simple. It abstracts from the agglomeration economies that much of urban economics treats as central {{glaeser2001;combes2012}}, on the grounds that remote workers are unlikely to generate them in the receiving market; the empirical work tests this abstraction through the response of tradable employment, which the model predicts should be small. Second, the model is static. It describes how a market's equilibrium shifts in response to a permanent change in the number of remote workers, not the path of adjustment. The empirical work, which traces effects over eight quarters, therefore speaks to the model's comparative statics only as the market approaches its new equilibrium, a point to which Chapters 5 and 7 return.

## 1.4 Empirical strategy in brief

Testing the model requires variation in remote-worker arrivals across local labor markets and over time, an estimation strategy that can separate the effects of arrivals from the many other forces shaping local economies after 2020, and outcome measures for the non-tradable sector, wages, rents and house prices.

The unit of analysis is the local labor market. I work with a panel of 600 labor markets modelled on US commuting zones, which group counties by the strength of their commuting ties and have become the standard definition of a local labor market in US empirical work {{tolbert1996;fowler2016}}. The panel is observed quarterly from 2017Q1 to 2024Q4, giving 32 quarters and 19,200 market-quarter observations. Chapter 4 and Appendix A describe its construction in detail.

Treatment is defined by the arrival of remote-capable workers. Using an occupational measure of teleworkability in the spirit of {{dingel2020|n}}, I classify migrants by whether their occupation can be performed remotely. A market is treated from the first quarter in which its net in-migration of remote-capable workers exceeds 0.5 percent of its pre-2020 employment for two consecutive quarters, and it remains treated thereafter. Treatment is therefore absorbing and staggered. Of the 600 markets, 306 become treated during the sample period, in four cohorts: 84 markets in 2020Q3, 97 in 2021Q1, 73 in 2021Q3 and 52 in 2022Q1. The remaining 294 markets are never treated. Treated markets are on average fairly large, with mean employment of 142,000 jobs, but the distribution is skewed and the median treated market has 61,000 jobs. Among treated markets, the inflow of remote workers averaged 1.9 percent of baseline employment by eight quarters after treatment.

The main outcomes are the log of non-tradable service employment, defined to include retail, food service, personal services and the construction trades; the log of the mean weekly wage in those sectors; the log of the median asking rent; the log of a house price index; and the log of tradable employment, which serves as a partial placebo because the model predicts it should respond little.

The natural estimator for a setting of this kind has long been a two-way fixed-effects regression of the outcome on a treatment indicator, market fixed effects and period fixed effects. A recent and influential body of econometric work has shown that this estimator can be seriously misleading when treatment is adopted at different times and its effects vary across cohorts or over time since treatment {{goodmanbacon2021;dechaisemartin2020;sun2021}}. In that case, the two-way fixed-effects coefficient is a weighted average of many simple comparisons, some of which use already-treated markets as controls for later-treated ones, and some of whose weights can be negative. Both conditions are likely to hold here. Effects of remote-worker arrivals plausibly build over time as businesses open and housing adjusts, and they plausibly differ between the early cohorts, treated in the first months of the pandemic, and later ones.

I therefore estimate effects using three estimators designed to be robust to such heterogeneity: the group-time average treatment effect approach of {{callaway2021|n}}, using both not-yet-treated and never-treated markets as controls; the interaction-weighted event-study estimator of {{sun2021|n}}; and the imputation estimator of {{borusyak2024|n}}. Each recovers a well-defined average of cohort- and period-specific effects without relying on forbidden comparisons. I also report the conventional two-way fixed-effects estimate and decompose it to show exactly where its bias comes from, using the decomposition of {{goodmanbacon2021|n}} and the weights of {{dechaisemartin2020|n}}. The review of {{roth2023|n}} guides the overall organization of the empirical analysis.

Identification rests on a parallel-trends assumption: in the absence of remote-worker arrivals, outcomes in treated markets would have evolved like those in not-yet-treated and never-treated markets. This assumption cannot be tested directly, but the event-study design allows me to examine whether treated and comparison markets were on similar paths before treatment. To assess how robust the conclusions are to plausible violations of parallel trends, I apply the sensitivity analysis of {{rambachan2023|n}}, which bounds post-treatment violations relative to the largest violation observed before treatment. Standard errors are clustered by market throughout, following the guidance of {{bertrand2004|n}} on serial correlation in difference-in-differences designs, of {{cameron2015|n}} on cluster-robust inference, and of {{abadie2023|n}} on when clustering is warranted by the design; cohort-level estimates are accompanied by wild cluster bootstrap inference.

The local multiplier requires one further step. The difference-in-differences estimates deliver the proportional change in non-tradable employment caused by treatment, and a parallel set of estimates delivers the size of the remote-worker inflow that treatment represents. Dividing the first by the second, and converting from proportions to job counts using baseline non-tradable employment, yields an estimate of $\mu_c$ in Equation (1.1). This is a Wald-type ratio: treatment status plays the role of an instrument for the number of remote workers, and the multiplier is the reduced-form effect on employment scaled by the first-stage effect on the inflow. The interpretation as a causal multiplier requires that treatment affect non-tradable employment only through the arrival of remote workers, an exclusion restriction that Chapter 5 discusses at length. The most serious threat is that remote workers were drawn to markets that were already on the verge of expanding their service sectors, for example because of new amenities; the pre-trend evidence and the amenity heterogeneity analysis are designed to address it.

A word on why a simpler design would not suffice. One could, in principle, regress changes in local service employment on changes in the remote-worker population across markets, using a single long difference from before to after the pandemic. Such a regression would conflate the effect of arrivals with everything else that distinguished receiving markets, including the pandemic's uneven effects on local industries, differences in public-health restrictions, and differences in the public income support that sustained local spending during 2020 and 2021. The staggered design exploits a more informative source of variation: the timing of arrivals. Markets that received remote workers in 2020Q3 can be compared with markets that would receive them a year later, which by then had been exposed to the same aggregate shocks but had not yet been treated. This comparison does not eliminate all confounding, but it removes a great deal of it, and the pre-treatment event-study coefficients show whether what remains is visible in the data.

## 1.5 Preview of findings

The main findings can be summarized in six points.

First, the arrival of remote workers raises local non-tradable service employment. Using the Callaway–Sant'Anna estimator, the overall average treatment effect on the treated is 2.1 percent (standard error 0.6). The other robust estimators give very similar answers: 2.0 percent (0.6) using Sun–Abraham and 2.2 percent (0.5) using the Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess imputation estimator. The effect builds steadily after treatment, from 0.4 percent in the quarter of treatment to 1.9 percent after four quarters and 3.0 percent after eight. Before treatment, by contrast, the event-study coefficients for leads from eight to two quarters before treatment all lie between −0.3 and +0.3 percent, none is individually significant, and a joint test of their equality to zero has a p-value of 0.62. Sensitivity analysis shows that the effect four quarters after treatment remains positive even if post-treatment violations of parallel trends are allowed to be as large as 0.8 times the largest pre-treatment violation ($\bar{M} = 0.8$).

Second, the conventional two-way fixed-effects estimator substantially understates the effect. The static two-way fixed-effects coefficient is 1.3 percent (0.5), roughly 40 percent below the robust estimates. The Goodman-Bacon decomposition shows why: 38 percent of the estimator's weight falls on comparisons between later-treated markets and earlier-treated markets that serve as controls, and those comparisons average −0.4 percent, because the earlier-treated controls are themselves still experiencing growing treatment effects. Viewed through the lens of de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille, 21 percent of the weights on group-time effects are negative. This is a concrete applied illustration of a problem that the recent econometric literature has established in theory.

Third, the employment effect is largest for the earliest cohort and declines across later ones: 2.6 percent for markets treated in 2020Q3, 2.2 percent for 2021Q1, 1.8 percent for 2021Q3 and 1.2 percent for 2022Q1. Part of this pattern reflects the shorter post-treatment window available for later cohorts, but it is also consistent with the earliest destinations having been the most attractive and the most able to absorb new demand.

Fourth, the implied local multiplier is substantial but smaller than conventional estimates for high-skill tradable jobs. Combining the employment effect with the estimated inflow of remote workers, each additional remote worker is associated with 0.6 (standard error 0.2) additional local non-tradable jobs. Chapter 5 discusses why a multiplier of this size is consistent with the export-base interpretation, given that remote workers bring spending without bringing a local employer.

Fifth, a large part of the demand shock is absorbed by prices rather than quantities. Non-tradable wages rise by 1.2 percent (0.5), but median rents rise by 4.6 percent (1.1) and house prices by 6.3 percent (1.5). Tradable employment barely moves, at 0.2 percent (0.4), consistent with the model's prediction that remote workers do not generate meaningful local agglomeration effects. Housing supply elasticity shapes the split exactly as the model predicts: in markets with inelastic housing supply, rents rise by 6.8 percent against 2.9 percent in elastic markets, while employment rises by 1.6 percent against 2.7 percent. Amenity-rich markets, in the top tercile of an amenity index, see larger employment effects, of 2.9 percent.

Sixth, the gains are unequally distributed. A back-of-the-envelope incidence calculation in Chapter 6 shows that for a renter household working in the non-tradable sector, a 1.2 percent wage gain is more than offset by a 4.6 percent rent increase whenever rent exceeds 26 percent of household income. Incumbent homeowners, by contrast, gain from rising house values. Net local welfare rises on average, but the distribution of gains is regressive.

Taken together, these findings suggest that remote workers do act as an export base for the markets they move to, generating real local job growth in services, but that the benefits to incumbent workers depend heavily on whether local housing supply can expand to accommodate the new demand.

## 1.6 Contributions

The dissertation makes contributions to three literatures.

The first is the economics of remote work. Existing work has established how many jobs can be done remotely {{dingel2020;mongey2021}}, how quickly remote work spread and why it has persisted {{brynjolfsson2020;barrero2021stick;barrero2023evolution}}, how it affects productivity and careers within firms {{bloom2015}}, and how it has reshaped the internal geography of large metropolitan areas {{ramani2021;gupta2022;delventhal2022}} and the fortunes of service workers in the cities that remote workers left {{althoff2022}}. This dissertation turns to the receiving side of remote-worker migration and to the labor markets that gain residents. It provides a framework for thinking about remote workers as an export base, and it estimates both the local employment response and the price responses that determine how the gains are shared.

The second is the literature on local labor markets and local multipliers. Commuting zones have become the workhorse unit for studying how local economies respond to shocks, from import competition {{autor2013china}} to technological change {{autor2013services}}, and a central finding is that local shocks have persistent local consequences. The literature on local multipliers {{moretti2010;moretti2012}} has estimated how tradable job creation spills over into services. Remote workers allow the spending channel of the multiplier to be isolated from the production and agglomeration channels with which it is normally bundled. The estimated multiplier of 0.6 non-tradable jobs per remote worker is therefore informative about a component of the conventional multiplier that has been difficult to measure separately. The heterogeneity results, which show the multiplier shrinking where housing supply is inelastic, connect the multiplier literature to the spatial-equilibrium analysis of how local demand shocks are capitalized into land values.

The third contribution is to applied econometric practice. The setting is a textbook example of staggered adoption with dynamic, heterogeneous effects, and the dissertation documents in detail how conventional and robust estimators diverge in it. The two-way fixed-effects estimate is about 40 percent smaller than the robust estimates, and the decomposition attributes the gap to identifiable comparisons. The dissertation also demonstrates a complete modern workflow, from event-study pre-trend assessment through honest sensitivity bounds to clustered and bootstrap inference, that may be useful as a template for applied work in urban and regional economics.

Beyond these three literatures, the dissertation speaks to a policy debate. Local governments that seek to attract remote workers typically emphasize the jobs and business activity that new residents bring. The results suggest that this emphasis is incomplete. The job gains are real, but in markets where housing supply is constrained they come with rent increases large enough to leave many incumbent service workers worse off. The implication is not that attracting remote workers is a mistake, but that the policy is most likely to benefit incumbent residents broadly when it is paired with measures that allow housing supply to respond. Chapter 7 develops this argument and discusses its fiscal dimension, since rising property values also raise local tax bases.

## 1.7 Scope and limitations

Several boundaries of the analysis should be stated at the outset; Chapter 7 returns to them in more depth.

The first concerns measurement. The treatment definition relies on classifying migrants as remote-capable on the basis of their occupation. Not every worker in a teleworkable occupation works remotely, and some workers in less teleworkable occupations do. The classification therefore measures remote-capable arrivals rather than remote workers as such, and it may misclassify some moves. Chapter 4 discusses how this affects the interpretation of the estimates, and Appendix B shows that the main conclusions survive alternative thresholds.

The second concerns timing. Remote workers did not move at random. Their destinations were plausibly chosen in part because of expected local conditions, including amenities, housing costs and the availability of services. The design addresses this by comparing treated markets with not-yet-treated and never-treated markets and by examining pre-treatment trends, but it cannot rule out that some unobserved factor drove both arrivals and subsequent local growth. The sensitivity analysis quantifies how large such a confounding trend would need to be to overturn the conclusions.

The third concerns spillovers. The difference-in-differences framework assumes that one market's treatment does not affect outcomes in other markets. Remote-worker migration violates this assumption to some extent by construction, since a worker who arrives in one market has left another. Because origin markets are typically large and destination markets typically smaller, the bias this introduces is likely to be modest, but it cannot be eliminated.

Finally, the analysis is reduced-form within a structural interpretation. The model in Chapter 3 guides which relationships are estimated and how they are interpreted, but the dissertation does not estimate the model's parameters structurally, and the welfare calculations in Chapter 6 are back-of-the-envelope exercises rather than full general-equilibrium counterfactuals.

## 1.8 Structure of the dissertation

The dissertation is organized in seven chapters and three appendices.

Chapter 2 reviews the literatures on which the dissertation builds. It covers the feasibility, adoption and persistence of remote work; the geography of remote work within and across metropolitan areas; local labor markets, export bases and multipliers; agglomeration economies; and recent advances in difference-in-differences econometrics. It closes by identifying the gap that the dissertation fills.

Chapter 3 develops the spatial-equilibrium model with remote workers. It sets out the environment, characterizes the equilibrium, and derives Propositions 3.1 to 3.3 on the responses of non-tradable employment and rents to remote-worker arrivals and on the incidence of those responses.

Chapter 4 describes the data and the empirical strategy. It defines the labor-market panel, the treatment and the outcomes, presents descriptive statistics by treatment cohort, and sets out the estimating equations, from the two-way fixed-effects benchmark to the group-time average treatment effects and their aggregation. It also discusses identification and inference.

Chapter 5 presents the main results for non-tradable service employment. It reports the estimates from all four estimators, the event-study dynamics and pre-trend tests, the decomposition of the two-way fixed-effects estimate, the cohort-specific effects, a battery of robustness checks including the honest difference-in-differences bounds, and the estimate of the local multiplier.

Chapter 6 turns to prices and incidence. It reports effects on non-tradable wages, rents, house prices and tradable employment, examines heterogeneity by housing-supply elasticity and amenities, links these results to the model's propositions, and calculates the incidence of remote-worker arrivals for renters and owners.

Chapter 7 concludes. It summarizes the findings, draws out their implications for housing and local fiscal policy, discusses the limitations of the evidence and of the design in more depth, and suggests directions for future research.

Appendix A documents the construction of the labor-market panel. Appendix B reports additional robustness results, including alternative treatment thresholds, alternative control groups and alternative approaches to inference. Appendix C contains the proofs of the propositions stated in Chapter 3.

The next chapter begins this program by reviewing what is already known about remote work, about place, and about how local labor markets respond when new income arrives.

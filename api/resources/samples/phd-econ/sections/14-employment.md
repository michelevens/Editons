# Chapter 5: Remote Workers and Local Service Employment

## 5.1 Introduction

This chapter asks the first of the dissertation's empirical questions: when a local labor market begins to receive a sustained inflow of remote-capable workers, does employment in its non-tradable service sector grow, by how much, and how quickly? The spatial-equilibrium model of Chapter 3 gives a clear qualitative answer. Remote workers bring with them incomes that are set in a national market and earned in tradable work performed for employers elsewhere, but they spend a large share of those incomes locally, on meals, haircuts, childcare, home repairs and the many other services that must be produced where they are consumed. In the language of the urban literature, remote workers are a new kind of export base {{moretti2010;moretti2012}}. Proposition 3.1 states that the arrival of such workers raises non-tradable employment, and that the size of the response falls as the local housing supply becomes less elastic, because a larger share of the new spending is absorbed by rents rather than by additional service output. The purpose of this chapter is to estimate that response, to characterize its dynamics, and to translate it into a local multiplier that can be compared with the multipliers estimated for traditional export industries.

The empirical setting is the synthetic panel described in Chapter 4: 600 labor markets modelled on US commuting zones {{tolbert1996;fowler2016}}, observed quarterly from 2017Q1 to 2024Q4, for 19,200 market-quarters in total. A market becomes treated in the first quarter in which its net in-migration of remote-capable workers exceeds 0.5 percent of its pre-2020 employment for two consecutive quarters, and it remains treated thereafter. Treatment is staggered across four cohorts, first treated in 2020Q3 (84 markets), 2021Q1 (97), 2021Q3 (73) and 2022Q1 (52), while 294 markets are never treated. Remote-capable occupations are identified with the teleworkability classification of {{dingel2020|n}}, and the analysis follows the estimating equations set out in Chapter 4: the static two-way fixed-effects regression of Equation (4.1), the dynamic event-study specification of Equation (4.2), the group-time average treatment effects of Equation (4.3) and their aggregation in Equation (4.4).

Staggered adoption with plausibly heterogeneous and growing effects is exactly the setting in which the conventional two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) estimator is known to be unreliable {{goodmanbacon2021;dechaisemartin2020;sun2021}}. A second purpose of the chapter is therefore methodological in a modest, applied sense: it shows, in a setting where the economics predicts effects that build over time, how large the gap between the TWFE estimate and heterogeneity-robust estimates can be, and it decomposes the gap to show where it comes from. The decomposition is not a curiosity. A researcher who stopped at the TWFE coefficient would conclude that remote-worker arrivals raise local service employment by a little over one percent, and would compute a multiplier roughly forty percent smaller than the one the robust estimators support.

The chapter's main findings can be stated briefly. The preferred Callaway–Sant'Anna estimate of the overall average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) is 2.1 percent (standard error 0.6), and the Sun–Abraham and Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess estimators produce almost identical numbers, 2.0 and 2.2 percent. The static TWFE estimate, by contrast, is 1.3 percent. Event-study estimates show no detectable movement in the eight quarters before treatment and an effect that grows steadily after it, reaching 3.0 percent by the eighth quarter. The Goodman-Bacon decomposition attributes the TWFE shortfall to the 38 percent of identifying weight that falls on comparisons in which already-treated markets serve as controls. Effects are larger for the earliest cohorts. Expressed as a local multiplier, each additional remote worker is associated with about 0.6 additional non-tradable jobs, a figure that is economically meaningful but well below the multipliers estimated for traditional tradable employment.

The chapter is organized as follows. Section 5.2 recalls the specifications and explains the implementation choices. Section 5.3 presents the main estimates in Table 5.1. Section 5.4 turns to the event-study dynamics and the evidence on pre-treatment trends. Section 5.5 diagnoses the TWFE bias. Section 5.6 examines heterogeneity across treatment cohorts. Section 5.7 reports robustness checks, including the sensitivity analysis of {{rambachan2023|n}}. Section 5.8 converts the employment effects into a local multiplier, and Section 5.9 relates the results to the model of Chapter 3 before Section 5.10 concludes.

## 5.2 Specification and Implementation

### 5.2.1 Outcome and sample

The outcome throughout this chapter is the natural logarithm of non-tradable service employment in market $c$ and quarter $t$, which I write $y_{ct}$. Following the definitions of Chapter 4, the non-tradable sector comprises retail trade, food service, personal services and the construction trades. These industries share two features that make them the natural place to look for a local demand response. Their output is consumed close to where it is produced, so that demand in a market depends on the income of the people who live there, and their employment can adjust relatively quickly, since entry requires modest capital and the workforce is comparatively mobile across employers. Construction trades are included because household spending on renovation and repair is part of the local demand that remote workers bring, though, as discussed in Section 5.7, the results do not depend on their inclusion.

All coefficients are reported in percent, that is, as 100 times the estimated change in log employment. For effects of the magnitude found here the distinction between log points and percentage changes is immaterial, and I use the language of percentages throughout. The estimation sample is the full balanced panel of 600 markets and 32 quarters. Because every treatment cohort is observed for at least eight quarters before and at least eight quarters after its first treated quarter, the event window from $e = -8$ to $e = 8$ is balanced in event time: each of the four cohorts contributes to every event-time coefficient in that window. This matters for interpretation. In unbalanced event studies, movement in the estimated path can reflect changes in which cohorts are being averaged rather than changes in the effect itself, and a balanced window removes that source of compositional drift.

### 5.2.2 Estimators

The four estimators reported in this chapter differ in how they construct the counterfactual for treated markets, and therefore in what they assume about the homogeneity of treatment effects.

The static TWFE estimator of Equation (4.1) regresses $y_{ct}$ on market fixed effects, quarter fixed effects and a single treatment indicator. It recovers the average treatment effect only if effects are constant across cohorts and over time since treatment. When effects vary, the coefficient is a weighted average of many simpler comparisons, some of which use already-treated markets as controls {{goodmanbacon2021}}, and some of whose implicit weights on group-time effects can be negative {{dechaisemartin2020}}. I report it as a benchmark, because it remains the specification most readers would expect to see, and because its failure is informative.

The preferred estimator is that of {{callaway2021|n}}, which estimates each group-time average treatment effect $ATT(g,t)$ of Equation (4.3) by comparing the change in outcomes for cohort $g$ between the last pre-treatment quarter $g-1$ and quarter $t$ with the corresponding change for a set of comparison markets. I use as comparison markets the union of the never-treated markets and the markets not yet treated by quarter $t$. This choice uses more of the data than a never-treated-only comparison group and is, under the assumption of no anticipation, equally valid; Appendix B reports the never-treated-only alternative. The group-time effects are then aggregated, using Equation (4.4), into an overall ATT, into an event-time profile and into cohort-specific averages. The overall ATT weights each post-treatment group-time cell by the size of its cohort, so cohorts with more markets and more post-treatment quarters receive more weight.

The interaction-weighted estimator of {{sun2021|n}} saturates the event-study regression with interactions between cohort indicators and relative-time indicators, using the never-treated markets as the control cohort, and then averages the cohort-specific coefficients with weights equal to cohort shares. It avoids the contamination of each relative-time coefficient by effects at other horizons that afflicts the conventional dynamic TWFE specification.

The imputation estimator of {{borusyak2024|n}} fits market and quarter fixed effects using only untreated observations, namely all observations of never-treated markets and the pre-treatment observations of eventually treated markets, imputes the untreated potential outcome for every treated observation, and averages the differences between observed and imputed outcomes. Under parallel trends and no anticipation it is the efficient estimator in its class, which is why its standard error is the smallest of the four.

These estimators rest on the same identifying assumption, parallel trends in untreated potential outcomes, and differ mainly in how they use the comparison data. Agreement among them is therefore not independent confirmation of identification, a point that {{roth2023|n}} make forcefully. It is, however, confirmation that the estimate is not an artifact of a particular weighting of heterogeneous effects, and that is the question this section is designed to answer.

### 5.2.3 Inference

Standard errors are clustered by market. Treatment is assigned at the level of the market and the outcome is serially correlated within markets, which is the setting in which unclustered difference-in-differences standard errors are most misleading {{bertrand2004}}. With 600 clusters, of which 306 are treated, asymptotic cluster-robust inference is reliable for the pooled estimates {{cameron2015}}. The argument of {{abadie2023|n}} that clustering should follow the level of treatment assignment points in the same direction: assignment here is by market, so the market is the appropriate cluster. For the cohort-specific estimates in Section 5.6, where the smallest cohort has only 52 treated markets, I report wild cluster bootstrap standard errors and confidence intervals, which perform better when the effective number of treated clusters is modest {{cameron2015}}. For the Callaway–Sant'Anna event-study estimates I also report simultaneous confidence bands from the multiplier bootstrap, which account for the fact that seventeen coefficients are being examined at once {{callaway2021}}.

Three features of Table 5.1 deserve comment. The first is the close agreement among the three heterogeneity-robust estimators in columns (2) to (4). The Callaway–Sant'Anna estimate of 2.1 percent, the Sun–Abraham estimate of 2.0 percent and the Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess estimate of 2.2 percent differ by less than half of any of their standard errors. These estimators construct their counterfactuals in different ways: one by pairwise long differences against a pooled comparison group, one by a saturated regression against the never-treated cohort, and one by imputation from a fixed-effects model fitted to untreated observations. That they agree indicates that the estimate is not sensitive to how the comparison data are weighted, and in particular that using not-yet-treated markets as controls, which the first and third estimators do and the second does not, has little effect on the answer.

The second feature is the size of the effect. An overall ATT of about 2 percent means that, averaged over the post-treatment period, non-tradable service employment in a treated market was about 2 percent higher than it would have been without the remote-worker inflow. For the mean treated market, which had 142,000 jobs in total before treatment, this is a meaningful number of service jobs, and it was generated by an inflow of remote workers that reached, on average, 1.9 percent of baseline employment by the eighth quarter after treatment. Whether the effect is large or small relative to the inflow is the question of Section 5.8; here it suffices to note that the effect is several standard errors from zero for each robust estimator.

The third feature is the gap between column (1) and the others. The static TWFE estimate of 1.3 percent is about 40 percent smaller than the Callaway–Sant'Anna estimate. Its standard error is slightly smaller, so a researcher relying on it would be both less accurate and more confident. Taken on its own, the gap could be mistaken for sampling noise, since the TWFE coefficient lies just inside the 95 percent confidence interval of the imputation estimator, which runs from roughly 1.2 to 3.2 percent. Section 5.5 shows, however, that the gap is not noise: it has a precise mechanical source in the structure of the comparisons TWFE makes, and it would persist in an arbitrarily large sample. Before turning to that diagnosis, it is useful to look at the dynamics, because the time profile of the effect is what produces the bias.

The aggregation in column (2) also deserves a word of explanation, because the overall ATT is not the simple average of the event-time coefficients reported in the next section. The overall ATT averages over every post-treatment quarter of every cohort, and the earliest cohort, first treated in 2020Q3, is observed for eighteen post-treatment quarters, while the latest, first treated in 2022Q1, is observed for twelve. Since effects grow with time since treatment, the cohorts with longer exposure contribute both more cells and larger effects. Weighting the cohort-specific ATTs of Section 5.6 by cohort size and number of post-treatment quarters reproduces the overall estimate of 2.1 percent; weighting by cohort size alone gives 2.0 percent; and the unweighted average of the nine post-treatment event-time coefficients from $e = 0$ to $e = 8$ is 1.8 percent. These are different estimands, each legitimate, and the choice among them should be guided by the question asked {{callaway2021;roth2023}}. For the multiplier calculation in Section 5.8, which compares the employment response with the size of the inflow at a common horizon, the event-time profile is the relevant object.

The post-treatment profile has a simple shape. The effect in the quarter of treatment is small, 0.4 percent, and not distinguishable from zero. It grows by roughly 0.4 percentage points per quarter over the first year, to 0.8 percent at $e = 1$, 1.2 percent at $e = 2$ and 1.6 percent at $e = 3$, and it first becomes statistically significant at $e = 2$. Growth then slows: the effect reaches 1.9 percent at $e = 4$, 2.3 percent at $e = 5$ and 2.6 percent at $e = 6$, and it is 2.8 and 3.0 percent at $e = 7$ and $e = 8$. Standard errors widen with event time, from about 0.5 at $e = 0$ to about 0.9 at $e = 8$, reflecting both the growing variance of long differences and the fact that later event times are estimated from comparisons with fewer not-yet-treated markets, since by the end of the window more of the eventually treated markets have themselves been treated.

Two economic features of this profile matter for what follows. The first is that the response is gradual. Even though the treatment definition requires that the inflow already be substantial at $e = 0$, non-tradable employment barely moves in that quarter. This is what one would expect if service employment adjusts to a demand shift through the entry of new establishments and the expansion of existing ones, both of which take time, rather than through an immediate change in hiring. It is also what one would expect if the inflow itself continues to build after the treatment date, which it does: the cumulative remote-worker inflow among treated markets rises over the post-treatment period and reaches 1.9 percent of baseline employment only by $e = 8$. Separating these two sources of gradualism, the lag of employment behind demand and the growth of demand itself, is one of the tasks of Section 5.8.

The second feature is that the profile has not obviously flattened by $e = 8$. The quarterly increments shrink from about 0.4 percentage points in the first year to about 0.2 to 0.3 points in the second, which suggests convergence, but the window ends before a plateau is clearly established. The estimated effects should therefore be read as medium-run responses at a two-year horizon rather than as long-run equilibrium effects of the kind that the comparative statics of Chapter 3 describe. The balanced design limits how far beyond $e = 8$ the profile can be traced: the 2022Q1 cohort drops out after $e = 11$, only three cohorts are observed at $e = 12$, and only the earliest at $e = 16$, so longer-horizon coefficients would confound the dynamics of the effect with differences between cohorts. I return to this limitation in Section 5.6.

### 5.4.2 Pre-treatment coefficients

The seven pre-treatment coefficients for $e = -8$ to $e = -2$ all lie within the band from −0.3 to +0.3 percent; in fact none exceeds 0.1 percentage points in absolute value, and no two adjacent coefficients differ by more than 0.2 points. None is individually significant, and they show no systematic pattern: their signs alternate rather than trending toward the treatment date. A joint Wald test that all seven are zero yields a $p$-value of 0.62. The simultaneous confidence band from the multiplier bootstrap, which is wider than the pointwise intervals shown in Figure 5.1, contains zero at every pre-treatment horizon.

This is the evidence one would like to see, but it should be interpreted with the care that the recent literature has urged. A failure to reject parallel pre-trends is not a demonstration that parallel trends hold, for two reasons {{roth2023;rambachan2023}}. First, pre-trend tests can have low power against violations of plausible size, so that a non-rejection may simply reflect imprecision. Here the pointwise standard errors on the pre-treatment coefficients are about 0.3 to 0.4 percentage points, so the test has reasonable power against a pre-trend steep enough to produce a 1 percent cumulative difference over the eight pre-treatment quarters, but much less against gentler drifts. Second, parallel trends in the pre-treatment period do not logically imply parallel trends after treatment; a shock coinciding with treatment would leave pre-trends clean while biasing the post-treatment estimates. Section 5.7.1 addresses the first concern directly with the sensitivity analysis of {{rambachan2023|n}}, which asks how large a post-treatment violation of parallel trends would have to be, relative to the observed pre-treatment variation, to overturn the conclusion. The second concern is addressed by the covariate-adjusted and placebo specifications in Sections 5.7.2 to 5.7.4.

There is a specific reason to scrutinize the quarters immediately before treatment. Because a market is classified as treated only once its inflow has exceeded the 0.5 percent threshold for two consecutive quarters, and the treatment date is set to the first of those quarters, some markets will have experienced inflows just below the threshold in the quarters before $e = 0$. If those sub-threshold inflows had local effects, they would appear as a rise in the coefficients at $e = -2$ and $e = -1$; and since $e = -1$ is the reference period, they would appear as a negative coefficient at $e = -2$ and a corresponding attenuation of the post-treatment estimates. The estimate at $e = -2$ is +0.1 percent, which shows no sign of this, and Section 5.7.3 confirms that re-normalizing to $e = -2$ to allow for one quarter of anticipation leaves the results essentially unchanged.

### 5.4.3 Why the dynamic TWFE specification is not used

Readers accustomed to the dynamic TWFE event-study regression of Equation (4.2), with relative-time indicators for each lead and lag, may wonder why it is not reported alongside Figure 5.1. The reason is the contamination result of {{sun2021|n}}: when effects differ across cohorts, the coefficient on a given relative-time indicator in that regression is a weighted combination of cohort-specific effects at that horizon and at other horizons, including post-treatment horizons. In a setting like this one, where effects grow over time and differ across cohorts, the contamination can produce spurious pre-trends or mask genuine ones. Since the purpose of the event study is to test for pre-trends and to trace the dynamics of the effect, an estimator that can manufacture pre-trends from treatment-effect heterogeneity is unsuited to the task. The Sun–Abraham interaction-weighted event study, which is designed to avoid this problem, produces a profile that is visually indistinguishable from Figure 5.1, with all pre-treatment coefficients within 0.3 percentage points of zero and post-treatment coefficients within 0.2 points of the Callaway–Sant'Anna estimates at every horizon.

## 5.5 Diagnosing the Two-Way Fixed-Effects Estimate

### 5.5.1 The Goodman-Bacon decomposition

Why is the static TWFE estimate so much smaller than the robust estimates? {{goodmanbacon2021|n}} shows that, with staggered timing and no covariates, the TWFE coefficient is exactly a weighted average of all the two-group, two-period difference-in-differences comparisons that can be formed from the timing groups in the data, with weights that depend on group sizes and on the variance of the treatment indicator within each comparison. With four treatment cohorts and one never-treated group there are sixteen such comparisons. Four compare a treated cohort with the never-treated markets. Six compare an earlier-treated cohort with a later-treated cohort during the window before the later cohort is treated, so that the later cohort serves as a legitimate not-yet-treated control. The remaining six compare a later-treated cohort with an earlier-treated cohort during the window after the earlier cohort has already been treated, so that the earlier cohort, whose outcomes are already affected by treatment, serves as the control. These last comparisons are the ones that the recent literature has come to call forbidden. Table 5.2 groups the sixteen comparisons into these three types.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Treated vs. never-treated | 4 | 0.47 | 2.4 | 1.13 |
| Earlier-treated vs. later-treated (later cohort not yet treated) | 6 | 0.15 | 2.2 | 0.33 |
| Later-treated vs. earlier-treated (earlier cohort already treated) | 6 | 0.38 | −0.4 | −0.15 |
| Total | 16 | 1.00 | | 1.30 |

Weights sum to one; contributions are weights multiplied by the weighted average estimate within each type, and their sum reproduces the TWFE coefficient of 1.3 up to rounding. The decomposition is stark. The clean comparisons, those in the first two rows, carry 62 percent of the weight and produce estimates of 2.4 and 2.2 percent, in line with the robust estimates of Table 5.1. The forbidden comparisons carry the remaining 38 percent of the weight and produce an average estimate of −0.4 percent. Taken alone, the clean comparisons would imply an estimate of about 2.35 percent, which is the weighted average of the first two rows after renormalizing their weights to sum to one. It is slightly above the Callaway–Sant'Anna estimate because the variance weights in the decomposition favor cohorts treated near the middle of the panel, and the cohorts first treated in 2020Q3 and 2021Q1, which sit closest to the middle of the 32-quarter window, are also the cohorts with the largest effects (Section 5.6). The forbidden comparisons pull the TWFE estimate down by roughly one percentage point, which is almost the whole of the gap between columns (1) and (2) of Table 5.1.

The negative sign of the forbidden comparisons is not a sign that remote-worker arrivals reduce employment in later-treated markets. It is a direct consequence of the dynamic profile in Figure 5.1. Consider the comparison between the cohort first treated in 2022Q1 and the cohort first treated in 2020Q3, using the latter as the control. In the pre-period of this comparison, the quarters between 2020Q3 and 2022Q1, the 2020Q3 cohort is between zero and five quarters into its treatment, with effects that, from the event-time profile, rise from near zero toward about 2.3 percent. In the post-period, from 2022Q1 onward, the 2020Q3 cohort is six or more quarters into treatment, with effects of 2.6 percent and rising. The difference-in-differences subtracts the change in the control cohort's outcomes from the change in the treated cohort's outcomes. The control cohort's change includes the continued growth of its own treatment effect, while the treated cohort's change includes only the early, small part of its effect. The comparison therefore subtracts more treatment effect than it adds, and its expected value can be negative even though both cohorts benefit from treatment. This is precisely the mechanism that {{goodmanbacon2021|n}} identifies: when effects grow over time, already-treated controls are trending upward for reasons that have nothing to do with the counterfactual, and the bias runs in the direction opposite to the effect.

The weight of 38 percent on these comparisons is high, but not unusual for a design of this shape. The forbidden weight is large when many units are treated, when treatment dates are spread across the middle of the panel, and when the never-treated group is not dominant. Here 306 of 600 markets are eventually treated, the four treatment dates fall between the fifteenth and the twenty-first quarter of a 32-quarter panel, and the never-treated group, while the largest single timing group, accounts for less than half of the markets. Each of these features increases the share of identifying variation that TWFE extracts from timing differences among treated cohorts, and therefore the scope for bias.

### 5.5.2 Negative weights on group-time effects

The decomposition of {{dechaisemartin2020|n}} offers a complementary view. Rather than decomposing the TWFE coefficient into comparisons, it expresses it as a weighted sum of the group-time average treatment effects $ATT(g,t)$ that the Callaway–Sant'Anna estimator targets, and it computes the implied weights. In this panel 21 percent of the weights on treated group-time cells are negative. The negative weights are not scattered at random. They fall on the cells of the earliest cohorts in the latest calendar quarters, that is, on the cells with the longest exposure to treatment. These are, by the dynamic profile, the cells with the largest effects. A weighting scheme that assigns negative weight to the largest effects and positive weight to the smallest will understate the average, and that is what happens here.

The two diagnostics tell the same story from different angles, and together they explain why the TWFE estimate is biased downward rather than merely noisy. The bias does not depend on any treatment effect being negative; every group-time effect in this panel is estimated to be positive or close to zero. It arises because TWFE implicitly assumes that effects are constant over time, and uses already-treated markets as controls on that assumption. When the assumption fails, as the gradual adjustment of service employment makes almost certain, the estimator confounds the growth of the treatment effect with the counterfactual trend. The practical lesson, which echoes the synthesis of {{roth2023|n}}, is that the static TWFE coefficient should not be reported as the headline estimate in staggered designs where effects are expected to evolve, and that a decomposition of this kind should accompany it whenever it is reported at all.

### 5.5.3 What the diagnosis does and does not imply

It is worth being precise about the scope of this diagnosis. The Goodman-Bacon and de Chaisemartin–D'Haultfœuille decompositions show that the TWFE estimate is a poor summary of the group-time effects; they do not show that the group-time effects themselves are well identified. The robust estimators in Table 5.1 avoid forbidden comparisons, but they inherit the parallel-trends assumption, and the evidence for that assumption is the pre-trend evidence of Section 5.4.2 and the sensitivity analysis of Section 5.7.1. Nor does the diagnosis imply that the TWFE estimate is uninformative. Because its bias runs in a known direction in this setting, it can be read as a conservative summary of the average effect, and the fact that even this conservative estimate is positive and significant is a modest piece of corroborating evidence. But the estimate that answers the question posed in Section 5.1 is the robust one.

The pattern is monotone. The cohort first treated in 2020Q3 has the largest average effect, 2.6 percent, and each later cohort has a smaller one: 2.2 percent for 2021Q1, 1.8 percent for 2021Q3 and 1.2 percent for 2022Q1. The last of these is not individually distinguishable from zero, which partly reflects the cohort's small size and partly its short post-treatment window. A joint test of equality across the four cohort ATTs does not reject at conventional levels ($p = 0.24$), so the evidence for heterogeneity is suggestive rather than decisive. The point estimates are nonetheless informative about mechanism, provided one is careful about what they measure.

### 5.6.2 Exposure length versus cohort-specific effects

The first and most mechanical explanation for the declining pattern is exposure length. Each cohort ATT averages over all of the cohort's post-treatment quarters, and those quarters differ in number: eighteen for the 2020Q3 cohort and twelve for the 2022Q1 cohort. Since effects grow with time since treatment, a cohort observed for longer accumulates more high-exposure quarters, and its average effect is larger even if its event-time profile is identical to that of a later cohort. This is a compositional feature of the cohort aggregation rather than a substantive difference between cohorts, and it is also why weighting the four cohort ATTs by both cohort size and number of post-treatment quarters reproduces the overall estimate of 2.1 percent (Section 5.3).

To separate exposure from genuine differences between cohorts, I re-estimated the cohort ATTs restricting each cohort to its first nine post-treatment quarters, $e = 0$ to $e = 8$, the window over which all cohorts are observed. The ordering of the cohorts survives this restriction but the differences between them shrink, and the earliest and latest cohorts are then separated by less than one standard error. Exposure length thus accounts for a substantial part of the spread in Table 5.3, and what remains is a modest, imprecisely estimated tendency for earlier cohorts to have larger effects at a given horizon.

### 5.6.3 Economic interpretations of the residual gradient

Three economic explanations are consistent with the residual gradient, and the design cannot fully distinguish among them.

The first concerns the nature of the moves. The earliest cohort was treated in the third quarter of 2020, when remote work had just become widespread {{brynjolfsson2020}}, and the relocations that occurred then were, plausibly, those of workers who expected to remain fully remote. Later relocations occurred as employers settled on hybrid arrangements, under which workers are expected in an office for part of the week {{barrero2021stick;barrero2023evolution}}. Evidence that much pandemic-era relocation took the form of moves from city centers toward suburbs within the same metropolitan area, keeping workers within commuting range {{ramani2021}}, suggests that hybrid arrangements tie relocation to commuting distance. In a panel of commuting-zone-like markets, moves within a market do not generate treatment at all, but moves to an adjacent market can, and a worker who commutes back to the origin market two or three days a week spends part of the week, and part of the budget, there. A remote worker who is only partly present is a weaker source of local demand than one who is fully present, which would produce smaller effects for later cohorts.

The second concerns the destinations. If the earliest-treated markets are, on average, richer in the amenities captured by the synthetic amenity index than later-treated markets, as the descriptive comparison of cohorts in Chapter 4 suggests, then cohort differences may partly reflect destination characteristics. Amenity-rich markets are those where local consumption of services is a large part of what makes the place attractive, and, as Chapter 6 reports, effects on non-tradable employment reach 2.9 percent in the top tercile of the amenity index. Differences in amenity composition across cohorts could account for part of the gradient.

The third concerns housing. Later cohorts were treated after a period of rising house prices and rents, during which the scope for accommodating new residents without crowding out other spending was smaller. Proposition 3.1 predicts that the employment response is smaller where housing supply is less elastic, because a larger share of new income is absorbed by rent; Chapter 6 confirms that the employment effect is 1.6 percent in markets with inelastic housing supply and 2.7 percent in markets with elastic supply. Evidence that pandemic-era revaluations of real estate were more pronounced where housing supply was less elastic {{gupta2022}} reinforces the plausibility of this channel. If later cohorts faced tighter housing markets, their employment effects would be smaller for the reasons the model describes.

These three explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the design is not equipped to apportion the gradient among them. Treatment cohorts are not randomly assigned, and cohort membership is correlated with market characteristics that are themselves plausible moderators of the effect. I therefore treat the cohort gradient as descriptive. The more robust conclusion is the negative one: there is no cohort for which the evidence points to a negative effect, and the heterogeneity across cohorts is modest relative to the overall effect.

### 5.6.4 Implications for aggregation

The cohort heterogeneity has a direct implication for how the overall ATT should be read. Because the earliest cohorts have both the largest effects and the longest post-treatment windows, they weigh heavily in the overall estimate. A reader interested in the effect of a remote-worker inflow arriving today, under contemporary hybrid arrangements and housing conditions, might reasonably place more weight on the later cohorts, whose effects are smaller. The cohort-size-weighted average of 2.0 percent and the event-time average of 1.8 percent over $e = 0$ to $e = 8$ provide lower reference points. None of these is the true effect in any privileged sense; they answer different questions, and reporting them side by side is more informative than selecting one {{callaway2021}}.

## 5.7 Robustness

This section asks whether the main results survive plausible violations of the identifying assumptions and reasonable alternative choices in implementation. The most important check, the sensitivity of the event-study conclusions to violations of parallel trends, comes first. Further results, including alternative treatment thresholds, alternative control groups and alternative approaches to inference, are reported in Appendix B and summarized here.

### 5.7.1 Sensitivity to violations of parallel trends

The pre-treatment evidence in Section 5.4.2 shows no detectable pre-trend, but, as noted there, the absence of detectable pre-trends is not proof of parallel trends. {{rambachan2023|n}} propose a way of making this concern quantitative. Rather than assuming that post-treatment deviations from parallel trends are zero, the researcher assumes that they are bounded by some multiple of the deviations observed before treatment, and reports confidence sets for the treatment effect that are valid under that bound. The relative-magnitudes version of the restriction bounds the change in the deviation from parallel trends between consecutive post-treatment quarters by $\bar{M}$ times the largest such change observed between consecutive pre-treatment quarters. Setting $\bar{M} = 0$ recovers the standard assumption of exact parallel trends; setting $\bar{M} = 1$ allows post-treatment violations as large as the worst pre-treatment violation in every quarter.

I apply this analysis to the effect at $e = 4$, one year after treatment, which is the horizon by which the event-time profile has reached most of its medium-run level and at which all four cohorts are well within their observed windows. The robust confidence set for the effect at $e = 4$ excludes zero for all values of $\bar{M}$ up to 0.8. A breakdown value of about 0.8 means that, to overturn the conclusion that non-tradable employment had risen one year after treatment, one would need to believe that the post-treatment violation of parallel trends in each quarter was at least 80 percent as large as the largest quarter-to-quarter movement observed in the eight pre-treatment quarters, and that these violations accumulated in a single direction over five consecutive quarters. Because the largest pre-treatment movement is itself small, a change of 0.2 percentage points between adjacent coefficients, this would require a sustained and systematic post-treatment trend in service employment, specific to treated markets and coinciding with the arrival of remote workers. At longer horizons the breakdown value falls, as it must under the relative-magnitudes restriction, because permitted violations cumulate quarter by quarter; the conclusions at $e = 8$ are correspondingly less robust than those at $e = 4$, and I calibrate the language of Section 5.8 accordingly.

The interpretation of a breakdown value is a matter of judgment; the sensitivity framework reports how much violation a conclusion can tolerate, not how much violation is plausible. In this setting the main threat to parallel trends is that remote-capable workers chose destinations whose service sectors were already on a stronger path, for example because those places were recovering faster from the pandemic. Section 5.7.2 addresses that threat directly. The sensitivity analysis complements the direct checks by showing that the conclusion does not hinge on parallel trends holding exactly.

### 5.7.2 Differential pandemic exposure

The treatment period coincides with the pandemic and its aftermath, during which local service employment fell sharply and recovered unevenly. If markets that attracted remote workers also differed in their exposure to the pandemic shock, the post-treatment estimates could reflect a differential recovery rather than the effect of remote workers. Two features of the setting make this concern pressing. Workers in jobs that cannot be done at home and that require physical proximity were, as {{mongey2021|n}} document, more exposed to the employment losses of social distancing, and such jobs are concentrated in exactly the non-tradable industries studied here. And when high-skill workers in dense cities went remote, their spending on local consumer services fell, so that the service-sector losses were concentrated in those cities {{althoff2022}}, which are more likely to be origins than destinations of the moves studied here.

I address this concern by estimating the Callaway–Sant'Anna model under conditional parallel trends, using the doubly robust estimator with three baseline covariates measured in 2019: the share of employment in contact-intensive non-tradable industries, the share of employment in remote-capable occupations, and log baseline employment. Conditioning on these covariates allows the counterfactual trend to differ between markets with different pandemic exposure. The overall ATT under conditional parallel trends is very close to the unconditional estimate, and the event-time profile retains its shape, with pre-treatment coefficients still within the ±0.3 band. A second specification restricts the comparison group to markets in the same quintile of baseline contact-intensive employment share as the treated market, which forces comparisons between markets with similar pandemic exposure; the estimates are less precise but remain within one standard error of the baseline. These checks do not rule out every form of differential recovery, but they show that the result is not driven by the most obvious one.

### 5.7.3 Anticipation and the treatment threshold

The treatment definition raises two further concerns. The first is anticipation: if remote workers began arriving in meaningful numbers before the treatment date, the reference quarter $e = -1$ would already be contaminated, and the post-treatment estimates would be attenuated. Re-normalizing the event study to $e = -2$, which allows for one quarter of anticipation, leaves the post-treatment profile and the overall ATT essentially unchanged; the coefficient at $e = -1$ relative to $e = -2$ is small and insignificant. The second concern is the threshold itself. The 0.5 percent threshold and the two-consecutive-quarter rule are choices, and different choices would reclassify markets near the margin. Table B.1 in Appendix B reports estimates for alternative thresholds and persistence rules. Raising the threshold selects markets with larger inflows and yields larger employment effects, while lowering it admits markets with smaller inflows and yields smaller effects, which is the pattern one would expect if the effect scales with the size of the inflow. Table B.2 in Appendix B shows that using only never-treated markets, or only not-yet-treated markets, as controls leaves the conclusions intact.

### 5.7.4 Placebo outcome and sector definitions

If the estimated effect on non-tradable employment reflected a general boom in treated markets rather than a local demand response, one would expect tradable employment to rise as well. It does not. As Chapter 6 reports in Table 6.1, the ATT for log tradable employment is 0.2 percent with a standard error of 0.4, small and insignificant. This is the pattern the export-base logic predicts: remote workers raise local demand for goods and services that must be produced locally, but their arrival does not, at least at a two-year horizon, generate a detectable expansion of local tradable activity. The tradable placebo is not a perfect test, since agglomeration forces of the kinds organized by {{duranton2004|n}} into sharing, matching and learning could in principle raise tradable employment over longer horizons, but at the horizons studied here it helps to rule out generic local shocks.

The results are also robust to the definition of the non-tradable sector. Dropping construction trades, whose response might reflect housing investment rather than service demand, slightly reduces the precision of the estimate but leaves its magnitude close to the baseline. Estimating the effect separately for food service and for personal services, the most clearly consumption-driven of the four industries, gives effects at least as large as the pooled estimate.

### 5.7.5 Inference

Table B.3 in Appendix B compares standard errors under alternative approaches to inference: conventional cluster-robust standard errors at the market level, wild cluster bootstrap standard errors, and standard errors clustered at a coarser level that groups adjacent markets. The conclusions are unaffected. Market-level clustering is justified by the design-based argument of {{abadie2023|n}}, because treatment is assigned by market; clustering at a coarser level, which would be appropriate if treatment were effectively assigned to groups of adjacent markets, widens the confidence intervals modestly without changing any conclusion about significance.

## 5.8 The Local Multiplier of Remote Workers

### 5.8.1 From percentages to jobs

The estimates so far are expressed as percentage changes in non-tradable employment. For comparison with the literature on local multipliers, and for the welfare calculations of Chapter 6, it is useful to express them instead as the number of additional non-tradable jobs associated with each additional remote worker. This is the quantity that {{moretti2010|n}} estimates for tradable employment, and it is the empirical counterpart of the comparative-static derivative $d\ln N^{NT}_c / d\ln R_c$ studied in Chapter 3, converted from elasticities into levels.

Let $N^{NT}_{c,e}$ denote non-tradable employment in market $c$ at event time $e$, $R_{c,e}$ the cumulative net inflow of remote-capable workers since the reference quarter, and $E_{c,0}$ total employment in the market before 2020. Scaling both quantities by baseline employment puts them in common units, jobs per hundred baseline jobs, and avoids giving large markets disproportionate influence. The local multiplier at horizon $e$ is then

$$
m_e = \frac{\Delta N^{NT}_{c,e} / E_{c,0}}{R_{c,e} / E_{c,0}} \tag{?}
$$

where $\Delta N^{NT}_{c,e}$ is the change in non-tradable employment caused by the inflow. Neither the numerator nor the denominator is observed directly as a causal quantity, but each can be estimated with the same design. The numerator is the treatment effect on non-tradable employment, re-expressed in jobs per hundred baseline jobs rather than in logs; I call this the reduced form. The denominator is the treatment effect on the cumulative remote-worker inflow, also per hundred baseline jobs; I call this the first stage. Because never-treated and not-yet-treated markets also receive some remote workers, though fewer than the threshold requires, the first stage is a difference in inflows between treated and comparison markets rather than the raw inflow into treated markets. The ratio of the two is a Wald estimator, in the same sense as an instrumental-variables estimate with a binary instrument:

$$
\hat{m}_e = \frac{\widehat{ATT}^{\,N}_e}{\widehat{ATT}^{\,R}_e} \tag{?}
$$

Here $\widehat{ATT}^{\,N}_e$ and $\widehat{ATT}^{\,R}_e$ are Callaway–Sant'Anna event-time estimates at horizon $e$ for the scaled employment and scaled inflow outcomes, computed exactly as in Equations (4.3) and (4.4) but with these outcomes in place of log employment. The same estimate can be obtained by two-stage least squares, regressing the scaled employment change on the scaled inflow and instrumenting the inflow with treatment status, provided the regression is estimated on the clean comparisons only; I use the ratio form because it inherits the robustness of the group-time estimator directly.

### 5.8.2 Estimates

At the eight-quarter horizon, the first stage is 1.9: by $e = 8$ the cumulative remote-worker inflow into treated markets exceeds that into comparison markets by an amount equal to 1.9 percent of baseline employment, the figure reported in Chapter 4. The reduced form at the same horizon is 1.14: non-tradable employment in treated markets is higher, relative to the counterfactual, by an amount equal to 1.14 percent of baseline total employment. The ratio gives the headline multiplier,

$$
\hat{m}_8 = \frac{1.14}{1.9} = 0.6 \tag{?}
$$

with a standard error of 0.2, computed by the delta method from the joint distribution of the two estimates and confirmed by a cluster bootstrap that resamples markets. Each additional remote worker is therefore associated with about 0.6 additional local non-tradable jobs two years after a market's inflow begins. The 95 percent confidence interval runs from roughly 0.2 to 1.0, so the data rule out both a zero multiplier and a multiplier of one or more, the latter being the benchmark at which each arrival would generate at least one additional local service job.

The multiplier at shorter horizons is smaller. At $e = 0$ the ratio is about half its eight-quarter value, because employment responds with a lag while the inflow is already substantial by construction of the treatment definition. The ratio rises through the first year and is stable at about 0.6 from $e = 4$ onward, so that by the second year the employment effect and the inflow are growing roughly in proportion. This pattern helps resolve the question left open in Section 5.4.1, of whether the gradual rise of the employment effect reflects slow adjustment of employment or continued growth of the inflow. Both matter: in the first year the employment effect grows faster than the inflow, as service employment catches up with a demand shock that has already arrived, while in the second year the two grow together, and the continued rise in the employment effect largely reflects continued arrivals. The stability of the ratio from $e = 4$ is also why I report the eight-quarter ratio as the headline figure, rather than the shorter-horizon ratios, which mix adjustment dynamics into the multiplier. Given the sensitivity analysis of Section 5.7.1, which shows that conclusions at $e = 8$ are less robust to violations of parallel trends than those at $e = 4$, it is reassuring that the ratio at $e = 4$ is essentially the same.

### 5.8.3 Interpretation and comparison

How should a multiplier of 0.6 be read? The natural benchmark is the estimate of {{moretti2010|n}} that each additional tradable job in a US city generates about 1.6 additional non-tradable jobs in the long run. The remote-worker multiplier estimated here is well under half that figure. This gap is, at first sight, surprising. Remote-capable jobs pay more than average {{dingel2020}}, and {{moretti2012|n}} emphasizes that high-skill jobs generate larger local multipliers than other tradable jobs, because their holders spend more on local services. On this reasoning, one might have expected a remote-worker multiplier above, not below, the benchmark.

Several features of remote work, and of the design, help to explain the gap, and each is of independent interest.

First, the two multipliers measure different things. A tradable job in the conventional calculation is a job located in the city, and the multiplier captures not only the spending of the worker who holds it but also the local purchases of the employer, from business services to maintenance to catering. A remote worker brings household spending but no employer. The employer's local purchases accrue to the market where the employer is located. In this sense, the remote-worker multiplier isolates the household-spending channel of the conventional multiplier, and it is unsurprising that it is smaller.

Second, remote workers' spending may be only partly local. Hybrid workers who travel periodically to an employer's location spend part of their working week, and some of their budget, there; a share of all households' spending flows to online retailers and to services produced elsewhere. The more of a remote worker's consumption leaks out of the destination market, the smaller the local multiplier. The cohort pattern of Section 5.6, in which later cohorts arriving under more hybrid arrangements have smaller effects, is consistent with this channel, although, as noted there, it is not decisive.

Third, housing absorbs part of the new income. The model of Chapter 3 implies that when housing supply is not perfectly elastic, part of the increase in local demand is capitalized into rents rather than translated into additional service output, and Chapter 6 shows that rents in treated markets rose by 4.6 percent. Spending that goes to landlords generates local service demand only insofar as landlords themselves live and spend locally. The heterogeneity by housing supply elasticity, with employment effects of 1.6 percent where supply is inelastic and 2.7 percent where it is elastic, is direct evidence that this channel operates, and it implies that the multiplier would be larger in markets where housing could expand more easily.

Fourth, the horizons differ. Moretti's estimates are long-run, based on changes over a decade, while the estimates here are at a two-year horizon. The event-time profile in Figure 5.1 has not fully flattened by $e = 8$, and if employment continues to adjust, the long-run multiplier could exceed 0.6. The stability of the ratio from $e = 4$ onward suggests that this effect is modest, but the window is too short to rule out further adjustment through the entry of new establishments, the slow process by which local service supply responds to persistent demand.

Fifth, the non-tradable sector is defined narrowly. The four industries studied here exclude health care, education and local government, which also respond to population growth, though with longer lags and through channels, such as fiscal transfers and public budgets, that differ from private demand. A broader definition would raise the measured multiplier, possibly considerably, but at the cost of mixing private demand responses with public provision. I keep the narrow definition because it corresponds most directly to the non-tradable sector of the model.

Taken together, these considerations suggest that the remote-worker multiplier is best understood not as a smaller version of the conventional tradable-job multiplier but as a different object: the local employment return to imported household income, net of leakage and of capitalization into housing. Read that way, a value of 0.6 is plausible, and its components point to the policy margin that Chapter 6 and Chapter 7 develop, namely that the employment benefits of remote-worker arrivals depend on how much of the new demand is absorbed by housing costs.

### 5.8.4 Caveats

Two caveats qualify the multiplier estimate. The first is that it is a ratio of two estimated treatment effects, and the exclusion restriction that justifies reading it as the effect of remote workers on local jobs is that treatment affects local non-tradable employment only through the inflow of remote workers. If treated markets also experienced other changes correlated with the inflow, such as an increase in visitors, second-home owners or in-migrants who are not remote workers, the multiplier would attribute their effects to remote workers. The tradable placebo and the covariate-adjusted specifications in Section 5.7 provide some reassurance, but they cannot rule out every such channel. The second caveat concerns measurement. Remote-capable status is assigned by occupation, following the teleworkability classification of {{dingel2020|n}}, rather than observed directly. Some in-migrants classified as remote-capable may in fact work locally, and some classified as non-remote may work remotely. Misclassification of this kind attenuates the first stage toward zero and, because the reduced form is unaffected, inflates the multiplier. If the misclassification is substantial, the true multiplier could be smaller than 0.6. I return to measurement of remote status as a limitation in Chapter 7.

## 5.9 Relating the Results to the Model

The empirical results of this chapter correspond closely to the predictions of the spatial-equilibrium model of Chapter 3, and it is useful to set out the correspondence explicitly.

Proposition 3.1 states that an inflow of remote workers raises local non-tradable employment, and that the response is smaller where housing supply is less elastic. The first part of the proposition is the main finding of Table 5.1, robust across estimators and supported by the event-study evidence of Figure 5.1. The second part is supported by the heterogeneity results that Chapter 6 reports, in which the employment effect is 1.6 percent in markets with inelastic housing supply and 2.7 percent in markets with elastic supply. The mechanism behind the second part, that inelastic housing supply diverts new income into rents, links this chapter directly to Proposition 3.2, which predicts that the rent response is larger where housing supply is less elastic, and which Chapter 6 tests.

The model also accounts for the absence of a tradable-employment response. In the model, tradable output is sold in a national market at a fixed price, and local tradable employment responds to remote-worker arrivals only through general-equilibrium effects on local wages and housing costs, which tend to reduce it slightly. The estimated tradable effect of 0.2 percent, close to zero and imprecise, is consistent with this prediction and inconsistent with a story in which remote-worker arrivals coincided with a generic boom. In this respect the model reproduces a central feature of the export-base logic {{moretti2010;moretti2012}}: the non-tradable sector expands because local demand expands, while the tradable sector is disciplined by prices set elsewhere.

Where the model is silent, and where the evidence is therefore most interesting, is in the dynamics. The comparative statics of Chapter 3 describe a move between steady states and say nothing about how long the move takes. The event-time profile shows that it takes at least a year for most of the employment response to materialize, and that the response is still growing two years after treatment. This gradualism has implications beyond the econometrics. It means that the immediate effect of remote-worker arrivals on local service employment is small, while their effect on housing costs, as Chapter 6 shows, appears more quickly. For a non-tradable worker who rents, the arrival of remote workers may therefore bring higher rents well before it brings more jobs or higher pay, a timing asymmetry that the static welfare analysis of Chapter 6 abstracts from but that bears on how the gains are distributed. Proposition 3.3 states the steady-state version of this distributional point: incumbent owners capture the rent increase that renters pay, and a renter household loses on net when its housing expenditure share exceeds the ratio of the wage response to the rent response, $d\ln w / d\ln r$. The gradual employment response documented here suggests that, along the transition, the relevant ratio is lower still in the early quarters.

The model also abstracts from agglomeration in the non-tradable sector. If larger local markets support more specialized services, through the sharing and matching mechanisms surveyed by {{duranton2004|n}} and documented empirically by {{rosenthal2004|n}}, the non-tradable employment response could exceed what a constant-returns model predicts, and could continue to grow as the market becomes thicker. The persistence of the upward slope in Figure 5.1 is consistent with such effects but does not establish them. Distinguishing a slow adjustment to a fixed demand shift from an agglomeration-driven expansion would require either a longer panel or establishment-level data that can track the entry of new kinds of services, along the lines of the productivity decompositions of {{combes2012|n}}, and I leave this to future work.

## 5.10 Conclusion

This chapter has estimated the effect of remote-worker arrivals on non-tradable service employment in a panel of 600 local labor markets. The central finding is that markets crossing the treatment threshold experienced an increase in non-tradable employment of about 2 percent on average over the post-treatment period, with heterogeneity-robust estimators agreeing closely: 2.1 percent for Callaway–Sant'Anna, 2.0 percent for Sun–Abraham and 2.2 percent for Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess. The effect builds gradually, from near zero in the quarter of treatment to 3.0 percent two years later, with no evidence of differential trends in the eight quarters before treatment. Its sign at a one-year horizon survives post-treatment violations of parallel trends up to 80 percent as large as the largest pre-treatment movement.

The static TWFE estimate, 1.3 percent, understates the effect substantially, and the decompositions of Section 5.5 show why: 38 percent of its identifying weight falls on comparisons that use already-treated markets as controls, and those comparisons, because effects grow over time, produce negative estimates. This is not a minor methodological refinement. Had the analysis relied on TWFE, it would have understated both the employment effect and the multiplier by roughly 40 percent.

Effects are largest for the earliest-treated cohort and decline across later cohorts, a pattern that reflects in part the longer exposure of early cohorts and in part, plausibly, the shift toward hybrid work and the tightening of housing markets over the period. Expressed in jobs, each additional remote worker is associated with about 0.6 additional non-tradable jobs at a two-year horizon, a multiplier well below conventional estimates for tradable jobs. I have argued that the gap reflects the absence of employer spending, the leakage of remote workers' consumption and the capitalization of new demand into housing costs.

The last of these points leads directly to the next chapter. If part of the income that remote workers bring is absorbed by rents rather than by local services, then the arrival of remote workers changes not only the number of local jobs but also who gains from them. Chapter 6 turns to wages, rents and house prices, estimates how the gains and losses are distributed between renters and homeowners and between workers in different sectors, and asks whether the employment gains documented here translate into gains in living standards for the incumbent workers who fill the new jobs.

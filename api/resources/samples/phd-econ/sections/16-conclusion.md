# Chapter 7: Conclusion

This dissertation began from a simple observation about the geography of income. For most workers, earning and spending used to happen in the same labor market. The large-scale shift toward working from home that began in 2020 loosened that link for a substantial group of workers. A software developer, an accountant or an analyst whose employer is located in one metropolitan area can now, in many cases, live in another. Her income is earned in tradable work done for a distant employer, but she buys groceries, haircuts, restaurant meals, home repairs and housing where she lives. The central claim of this dissertation is that such workers are best understood as a new kind of local export base, and that the tools developed to study local multipliers and spatial equilibrium can be used to predict, measure and evaluate what their arrival does to the places that receive them.

This final chapter draws the argument together. Section 7.1 restates the research question and summarizes how each chapter answered its part of it. Section 7.2 sets out the contributions of the dissertation to the economics of remote work, to the study of local labor markets, and to applied practice with staggered difference-in-differences designs. Section 7.3 discusses the implications for policy, focusing on housing supply and on local public finance. Section 7.4 is a deliberately specific account of the limitations of the evidence, both those that arise from the synthetic panel on which the analysis rests and those that would remain in any application of the same design to administrative data. Section 7.5 proposes a research agenda, and Section 7.6 closes.

## 7.1 The question and the answers

The question posed in Chapter 1 was this: when remote-capable workers relocate to a local labor market, bringing incomes earned elsewhere, what happens to local non-tradable service employment, wages and housing rents, and who gains? The question has three parts. The first is positive and concerns quantities: does the arrival of remote workers create local jobs in non-tradable services, and how many? The second is positive and concerns prices: how much of the shock is absorbed by wages and how much by rents? The third is normative and concerns incidence: given the quantity and price responses, which groups of local residents are made better or worse off?

Chapter 2 placed the question in four literatures: on the feasibility and persistence of remote work {{dingel2020;brynjolfsson2020;barrero2021stick;barrero2023evolution}}; on its geography, including the movement of population and real estate demand away from dense centers {{althoff2022;ramani2021;gupta2022;delventhal2022}}; on local labor markets and local multipliers {{autor2013china;autor2013services;moretti2010;moretti2012}}; and on agglomeration {{glaeser2001;duranton2004;rosenthal2004;combes2012}}. The review concluded that these literatures had not yet been joined: the remote-work literature had focused mainly on the cities that remote workers left, and the local-multiplier literature had focused on shocks to employment in tradable industries located in the receiving place.

Chapter 3 joined them in a Rosen–Roback style spatial equilibrium model with tradable and non-tradable sectors and three types of worker: local tradable workers, non-tradable workers and remote workers whose wage $w^R$ is set in a national market. Remote income enters the local economy exactly as export-base income does, raising local demand for non-tradable services. Because non-tradable workers are mobile and must be indifferent across markets, any increase in their nominal wage is tied to the increase in housing rents through the housing share $\alpha$ in preferences. The model delivered three propositions, proved in Appendix C. Proposition 3.1 states that the elasticity of local non-tradable employment $N^{NT}_c$ with respect to the remote-worker stock $R_c$ is positive and declines as housing supply becomes less elastic, that is, as $1/\eta_c$ rises. Proposition 3.2 states that the response of rents, and with it the response of non-tradable wages, is positive and increases in $1/\eta_c$. Proposition 3.3 characterizes incidence: the marginal mobile worker is held to her reservation utility, incumbent owners of housing gain, and an incumbent renter in non-tradables loses whenever the share of her income spent on rent exceeds the ratio of the wage response to the rent response.

Chapter 4 described the panel, the treatment definition and the empirical strategy. The analysis uses a synthetic quarterly panel of 600 labor markets modelled on US commuting zones {{tolbert1996;fowler2016}}, observed from 2017Q1 to 2024Q4, for a total of 19,200 market-quarters. A market is treated from the first quarter in which its net in-migration of remote-capable workers, classified by occupational teleworkability following {{dingel2020|n}}, exceeds 0.5 percent of its pre-2020 employment for two consecutive quarters. Treatment is absorbing. Four cohorts are treated, in 2020Q3 (84 markets), 2021Q1 (97), 2021Q3 (73) and 2022Q1 (52), and 294 markets are never treated. Chapter 4 also explained why the staggered rollout makes the conventional two-way fixed-effects estimator unreliable when effects vary across cohorts and over time {{goodmanbacon2021;dechaisemartin2020;sun2021}}, and why the analysis therefore relies on estimators built for that setting {{callaway2021;sun2021;borusyak2024}} and on a synthesis of current recommended practice {{roth2023}}.

Chapter 5 answered the quantity question. Using the Callaway–Sant'Anna estimator with not-yet-treated and never-treated markets as controls, the overall average treatment effect on the treated for log non-tradable service employment is 2.1 percent (SE 0.6). The effect builds gradually, from 0.4 percent in the quarter of treatment to 3.0 percent eight quarters later, which is the pattern one expects if local service supply adjusts with a lag to a persistent increase in local demand. The interaction-weighted estimator of {{sun2021|n}} gives 2.0 percent (0.6) and the imputation estimator of {{borusyak2024|n}} gives 2.2 percent (0.5). Pre-treatment coefficients for event times −8 to −2 lie between −0.3 and +0.3 percent, none is individually significant, and a joint test does not reject their equality to zero (p = 0.62). The sensitivity analysis of {{rambachan2023|n}} shows that the effect at four quarters after treatment remains positive for post-treatment violations of parallel trends up to 0.8 times the largest pre-treatment violation. Scaling the employment effect by the inflow of remote workers, which averaged 1.9 percent of baseline employment eight quarters after treatment, gives a local multiplier of 0.6 (SE 0.2) non-tradable jobs per additional remote worker.

Chapter 5 also showed why the choice of estimator matters. The static two-way fixed-effects estimate is 1.3 percent (SE 0.5), roughly 40 percent below the heterogeneity-robust estimates. The Goodman-Bacon decomposition attributes 38 percent of the two-way fixed-effects weight to comparisons in which earlier-treated markets serve as controls for later-treated markets, and those comparisons average −0.4 percent. Under the decomposition of {{dechaisemartin2020|n}}, 21 percent of the weights are negative. Because the effects grow with time since treatment and are larger for earlier cohorts (2.6 percent for the 2020Q3 cohort, falling to 1.2 percent for the 2022Q1 cohort), the conventional estimator is pulled toward zero in exactly the way the econometric literature predicts.

Chapter 6 answered the price and incidence questions. The average treatment effect on log mean weekly wages in non-tradable services is 1.2 percent (SE 0.5); on log median asking rents, 4.6 percent (SE 1.1); on the log house price index, 6.3 percent (SE 1.5). Tradable employment, which the model predicts should not respond to first order, moves by 0.2 percent (SE 0.4), indistinguishable from zero. The heterogeneity results line up with the comparative statics of Chapter 3. Rent effects are larger in markets with inelastic housing supply (6.8 percent against 2.9 percent), and non-tradable employment effects are smaller there (1.6 percent against 2.7 percent). Markets in the top tercile of the amenity index see an employment effect of 2.9 percent. The incidence calculation showed that, for a renter household working in non-tradable services, the 1.2 percent wage gain is more than offset by the 4.6 percent rent increase whenever rent exceeds 26 percent of household income, while incumbent homeowners gain from the capitalization of demand into house prices. Net local welfare effects are positive on average but regressive.

The answer to the dissertation's question is therefore qualified but clear. In this panel, remote-worker arrivals create local service jobs, at a rate of a little over half a job per arrival, and they raise local wages and, by more, local housing costs. The gains are real but unevenly shared. Where housing is scarce, the arrival of remote workers is converted disproportionately into higher rents rather than into more local jobs, and the households most likely to lose are renters working in the very sectors whose employment expands.

## 7.2 Contributions

### 7.2.1 Remote workers as an export base

The first contribution is conceptual. Much of the early economics of remote work studied either productivity at the level of the worker and firm {{bloom2015}} or the reallocation of spending and real estate demand away from dense city centers {{althoff2022;ramani2021;gupta2022}}. These are important questions, but they treat the receiving places as a residual. This dissertation reverses the perspective and asks what happens to the places that remote workers move to. The organizing idea, that remote income is export-base income earned elsewhere and spent locally, makes it possible to borrow a mature body of theory and evidence. The local-multiplier framework of {{moretti2010|n}} was developed for shocks to tradable employment located in a city; the model of Chapter 3 shows that the same logic applies, with modifications, when the tradable activity is located elsewhere and only the worker and her spending are located locally.

The modifications matter. In the classic multiplier, a new tradable job brings a new tradable worker, a new tradable wage bill and, potentially, agglomeration benefits for other tradable firms {{moretti2012}}. With remote workers, the spending channel is present but the local productivity channel is weak or absent, because the remote worker's colleagues and clients are elsewhere. This yields a sharper prediction, borne out in Chapter 6, that tradable employment in receiving markets should be essentially unaffected. The comparison also helps interpret the magnitude of the estimated multiplier. The estimate of 0.6 non-tradable jobs per remote worker is smaller than the long-run multiplier for tradable jobs reported by {{moretti2010|n}}, about 1.6 nontradable jobs per tradable job, and the export-base interpretation suggests why. A remote arrival injects spending but no local tradable production, no local supply chain and none of the learning or matching externalities that a co-located workplace would generate {{duranton2004;glaeser2001}}. The window of observation, at most seventeen quarters after treatment for the earliest cohort, is also short relative to a long-run multiplier. The two magnitudes are therefore not directly comparable, and I do not claim that one bounds the other; the point is that the theory predicts the direction of the gap.

### 7.2.2 A model that links quantities, prices and incidence

The second contribution is a tractable model whose comparative statics map directly into estimable objects. Rosen–Roback models are usually used to interpret wage and rent differentials across places at a point in time. The version developed in Chapter 3 is used instead to sign responses to a shock, and its central parameter, the housing supply elasticity $\eta_c$, governs the split between quantity and price responses. Proposition 3.1 and Proposition 3.2 imply that heterogeneity by housing supply elasticity should move employment and rent effects in opposite directions. That prediction is not mechanical in the empirical design; the estimates could have shown larger effects on both margins in inelastic markets if, for instance, those markets also received larger inflows. The fact that the heterogeneity results in Chapter 6 move in the predicted opposite directions is, in my view, the most informative test of the framework in the dissertation, because it is a test of the mechanism and not only of the sign of an average effect.

The model also gives the incidence calculation a disciplined form. Proposition 3.3 shows that the welfare of an incumbent renter in non-tradables depends on a single comparison between her rent share and the ratio of the wage response to the rent response. The empirical counterpart of that ratio is $1.2/4.6 \approx 0.26$, which is the 26 percent threshold in Chapter 6. Presenting incidence this way makes the result transparent and portable: a reader who believes different wage or rent effects apply in a particular place can recompute the threshold without re-estimating anything.

### 7.2.3 Applied practice with staggered adoption

The third contribution is methodological in an applied sense. The dissertation does not propose a new estimator. It shows, in a setting where the treatment timing is staggered and the treatment effects plausibly grow with exposure, how much the choice of estimator matters and how to diagnose the problem. The gap between the two-way fixed-effects estimate of 1.3 percent and the heterogeneity-robust estimates of 2.0 to 2.2 percent is large in economic terms; a policy analyst who relied on the former would understate the local employment response by roughly two-fifths and, through the multiplier, would understate the number of local jobs generated per arrival in the same proportion.

The dissertation also shows that the diagnostics of {{goodmanbacon2021|n}} and {{dechaisemartin2020|n}} are not only warnings but explanations. The decomposition tells us which comparisons drive the bias and in which direction, and that knowledge connects the econometric problem to the economics: later-treated markets are compared with earlier-treated markets whose employment is still rising because of an effect that builds over time, so the "control" trend is contaminated by treatment dynamics. The joint use of the Callaway–Sant'Anna, Sun–Abraham and Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess estimators, the event-study display and the bounds of {{rambachan2023|n}} follows the recommendations synthesized by {{roth2023|n}}, and the close agreement among the robust estimators is itself evidence that the result does not depend on any one estimator's assumptions. Throughout, inference is clustered at the level at which treatment is assigned, the labor market, following the design-based argument of {{abadie2023|n}} and the practical guidance of {{bertrand2004|n}} and {{cameron2015|n}}.
## 7.3 Implications for policy

The results are drawn from a synthetic panel, and the policy discussion that follows should be read accordingly: it sets out what the model and the estimated patterns imply if they hold in real labor markets, not what any particular place should do. With that qualification, two areas of policy follow directly from the findings.

### 7.3.1 Housing supply

The most robust policy implication concerns housing supply. In the model, the housing supply elasticity is the parameter that decides whether a remote-worker arrival becomes jobs or becomes rents. In the estimates, markets with inelastic supply saw rent increases more than twice as large as markets with elastic supply, and employment gains roughly 40 percent smaller. If those patterns carry over to real markets, a place that wishes its residents to benefit from remote-worker arrivals has a strong interest in allowing housing supply to respond. Measures that shorten approval times, permit smaller and denser units, or relax constraints on accessory dwellings would raise the effective $\eta_c$ in the language of the model, shifting the incidence of arrivals away from rent increases and toward job creation.

The model is also clear about who should be expected to resist such measures. Proposition 3.3 shows that incumbent owners of housing gain from arrivals precisely because supply is inelastic: the capitalization of local demand into house prices, 6.3 percent in the estimates, is a transfer to them. A supply expansion that lowers the rent response therefore also lowers their gain. The political economy of local land-use regulation is outside the scope of this dissertation, but the distributional result explains why the policy that most benefits renters may not be the policy that local electorates, in which owners are often overrepresented, will choose [source needed].

A second housing implication concerns timing. The event-study results in Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 show that employment responds gradually over two years. Asking rents can reprice quickly, whereas new service establishments take time to open and hire. To the extent that rent responses are front-loaded relative to employment responses, a question the rent event study in Figure 6.1 is designed to inform, renters bear the cost of arrivals before the full benefit of local job creation materializes. Policies that accelerate supply in the first year after a surge in arrivals would be more valuable, per unit, than the same policies introduced later.

### 7.3.2 Local public finance

The second area is local fiscal policy. Remote workers pay local property taxes, directly as owners or indirectly as renters, and in many jurisdictions local sales taxes, but they may pay income taxes in the jurisdiction of their employer rather than their residence, depending on the rules that apply [source needed]. The export-base view implies that remote arrivals raise the local tax base through spending and property values while imposing demands on local services, schools and infrastructure. Whether the net fiscal effect is positive depends on the composition of local revenue. Jurisdictions that rely mainly on property taxes would, on the estimates here, see their base rise with house prices; jurisdictions that rely on income taxes collected at the place of work would capture less.

Some places have offered direct subsidies to attract remote workers. The framework of this dissertation suggests caution in evaluating such programs. A subsidy that attracts a remote worker to a market with inelastic housing will generate fewer local jobs and larger rent increases than one that attracts the same worker to a market with elastic housing. The local multiplier of 0.6 also places an upper bound on what a subsidy program can claim in job creation per arrival, at least in the medium run, and claims that each subsidized arrival creates several local jobs would be inconsistent with the evidence here. Amenity-rich markets show larger employment responses, but they may also be the markets in which subsidies are least needed. In general, the case for public money depends on whether the subsidized arrival would have happened anyway, a question the design here does not address.

The regressive incidence also has a fiscal dimension: a local government could in principle use part of the increase in property tax revenue to offset renters' losses, and the incidence calculation supplies the quantities any evaluation of such transfers would need.

### 7.3.3 What the results do not license

The results do not show that remote work is good or bad for receiving places in general: the average welfare gain conceals losses for a well-defined group. They say nothing about the places remote workers leave, which may experience mirror-image effects {{althoff2022;ramani2021}}. Nor do they speak to national aggregates, since an arrival in one market is a departure from another.

## 7.4 Limitations

Every empirical dissertation has limitations, and the most useful thing a concluding chapter can do with them is to say precisely what each one threatens and how serious it is likely to be. I group them into those that arise from the synthetic data and those that arise from the design and would persist with real data.

### 7.4.1 The synthetic panel

The most fundamental limitation is that the panel analyzed in this dissertation is synthetic. It was constructed to resemble a panel of US commuting zones in its structure, size distribution and timing, and the construction is documented in Appendix A, but none of its values describes any real place. The estimates in Chapters 5 and 6 therefore demonstrate how the framework and the estimators behave on a panel of this kind, and how the results would be read and reported; they are not estimates of real-world parameters. This affects everything that follows. The magnitude of the multiplier, the size of the rent response, the 26 percent incidence threshold and the heterogeneity patterns are all properties of the synthetic data-generating process. I have tried throughout to write as though the estimates were real, because that is the exercise the dissertation is meant to illustrate, but no reader should cite them as evidence about any economy.

The synthetic panel also flatters the design. Real occupation-coded migration flows are noisy and available at coarser frequencies; real markets faced many other pandemic-era shocks, some correlated with arrivals; and real treatment timing would not cluster so neatly into four cohorts. Each feature would widen standard errors and complicate identification.

### 7.4.2 Measuring remote status

Even with real data, the treatment is measured indirectly. The definition classifies migrants as remote-capable using the teleworkability of their occupation {{dingel2020}}, not their actual work arrangement. A worker in a teleworkable occupation who moves to take a local, in-person job is counted as a remote arrival, and a worker in a partially teleworkable occupation who moves while keeping a remote job may not be. Occupation-based measures of feasibility are also not measures of practice; feasibility and actual work from home diverged during and after the pandemic in ways that varied by worker and place {{barrero2023evolution;mongey2021}}.

The consequence is measurement error in treatment intensity and, at the margin, in treatment timing. Misclassification of local job-takers as remote workers would bias the multiplier in a specific direction: such a worker brings a local job with her, and the employment effect per "remote" arrival would be inflated if her job is in non-tradables. Appendix B shows that the estimates are stable across alternative thresholds, which offers some reassurance about timing but none about this kind of misclassification. With real data, linking migration records to employer location, or using survey measures of work arrangements, would be the natural remedy.

### 7.4.3 Migration timing and anticipation

The treatment is defined by migration, and migration is a choice. The identifying assumption is that, conditional on market and quarter effects, the non-tradable employment of markets that received inflows would have evolved like that of markets that had not yet received them. Two threats to this assumption are specific to migration.

The first is anticipation. Remote workers may move toward markets where local service amenities are already growing, so that the arrival follows rather than causes the growth. The flat pre-trends, with event-time coefficients between −0.3 and +0.3 percent and a joint p-value of 0.62, argue against a strong form of this concern, and the honest bounds show that the effect at four quarters after treatment survives violations of parallel trends up to 0.8 times the largest pre-period violation. But pre-trend tests have limited power {{roth2023}}, and a selection process in which remote workers respond to expected future growth that is not yet visible in employment would not appear in the pre-period at all.

The second is the coincidence of treatment timing with the pandemic. All four cohorts are treated between 2020Q3 and 2022Q1, a period in which service employment was recovering unevenly across places for reasons unrelated to migration. Calendar-time effects absorb common shocks, and the not-yet-treated comparison absorbs shocks common to future-treated markets, but a recovery whose pace was correlated with the characteristics that attracted remote workers would bias the estimates. The amenity heterogeneity in Chapter 6 is double-edged here: it is consistent with the model, in which amenities raise the appeal of a place, but it also identifies a characteristic that could plausibly drive both arrivals and the pace of service recovery.

### 7.4.4 SUTVA and spillovers

The design assumes that a market's outcomes depend only on its own treatment status. That assumption is strained in two ways. First, remote workers who move into one market leave another. If an origin market is in the sample, its non-tradable employment falls as its residents leave, and if origin markets are disproportionately among the never-treated or not-yet-treated controls, the comparison overstates the effect on the destination by subtracting a negative effect on the control. The direction of this bias is clear, but its size depends on the share of flows that originate within the sample's control group, which the synthetic panel does not allow me to estimate credibly.

Second, neighboring markets are linked by commuting at their edges and by shopping trips, so that some of the spending of remote arrivals leaks into adjacent markets. Commuting zones are designed to minimize cross-boundary commuting {{tolbert1996}}, but their boundaries are sensitive to the methods used to draw them {{fowler2016}}, and for remote workers, whose commute is to a home office, the relevant boundary for spending may differ from the boundary defined by journeys to work. Spillovers of this kind would bias the estimated effects toward zero if neighbors are among the controls. A natural extension is to estimate effects on untreated neighbors of treated markets directly, as a test of this concern.

### 7.4.5 Interpretation of the multiplier

The local multiplier of 0.6 is computed as the ratio of the employment effect to the size of the inflow. That is a Wald-type ratio, and its interpretation as a causal effect per remote worker requires that the remote inflow is the only channel through which treatment affects non-tradable employment. If treatment also captures arrivals of non-remote family members, or changes in local spending by incumbents who see their house values rise, the ratio attributes those effects to remote workers. The model in Chapter 3 includes the second of these channels implicitly, through local income, so the ratio should be read as the total effect per arriving remote worker, including induced effects, rather than as the direct effect of her own spending.

### 7.4.6 External validity

Finally, the period studied is unusual: all cohorts are treated during or just after a pandemic. With smaller and more gradual flows, local services and housing would have more time to adjust, and the composition of remote workers may change as practices settle. The persistence of remote work after the pandemic {{barrero2021stick;barrero2023evolution}} suggests that the phenomenon is durable, but not that its local effects will remain the same.

## 7.5 Directions for future research

The most immediate next step is to take the framework to administrative data. The template in Appendix A identifies the inputs needed: occupation-coded flows of workers between local labor markets, at a quarterly or semiannual frequency, linked where possible to the location of the employer; establishment-level employment and earnings by detailed industry, to construct non-tradable outcomes; and rent and price indices for housing at the local labor market level. Linking residence and employer location would address the measurement problem discussed in Section 7.4.2 directly, by identifying remote workers by the distance between home and employer rather than by occupation. With such data, the analysis could also be extended to a continuous treatment, the share of remote arrivals in local employment, rather than the binary threshold used here, which would allow the multiplier to be estimated directly rather than as a ratio.

A second direction is to study origin and destination together. The SUTVA concern in Section 7.4.4 is also an opportunity: if flows can be traced from origin to destination, a single framework can estimate the loss of non-tradable jobs in origins and the gain in destinations, and hence the net national effect. Quantitative spatial models of the kind used by {{delventhal2022|n}} for one metropolitan area could be estimated across the full system of labor markets, with the reduced-form multipliers from this dissertation as targeted moments.

A third direction concerns agglomeration. The model of Chapter 3 abstracts from productivity spillovers in receiving markets. Yet remote workers are disproportionately high-skill {{dingel2020;althoff2022}}, and the literature on learning and matching in cities {{glaeser2001;duranton2004}} suggests that a concentration of high-skill residents could generate local spillovers even if their employers are elsewhere, for instance through new firm formation or through the local hiring of remote workers by local firms. Empirical evidence on whether such effects arise, and on how agglomeration economies attenuate when workers share a place but not a workplace {{rosenthal2004;combes2012}}, would test whether the export-base view is complete or whether remote workers eventually become a source of local tradable activity as well.

A fourth direction is distributional. The incidence calculation in Chapter 6 is a back-of-envelope exercise that treats households as fixed types. Household-level panel data on rents, earnings and moves would allow a direct study of who is displaced when remote workers arrive, whether displaced renters move to cheaper markets or to cheaper neighborhoods within the same market, and whether the welfare losses implied by Proposition 3.3 are borne by incumbents or are partly avoided through mobility. The evidence that workers who cannot work from home are less educated, lower paid and more likely to rent {{mongey2021}} suggests that this distributional question deserves priority.
## 7.6 Closing remarks

The arrival of a remote worker in a new place is an event that the standard categories of local economics were not built to describe. She is not a tradable worker in the local sense, because her employer and her output are elsewhere; she is not a non-tradable worker; and she is not a retiree living on transfers. This dissertation has argued that she is best thought of as a carrier of export-base income, and that once she is thought of in that way, a century of thinking about local multipliers and half a century of spatial equilibrium theory become available to analyze her effects. In the panel studied here, the result is a modest local multiplier, a moderate wage gain, a larger rent increase, and a pattern of incidence in which the average gain conceals losses for renters in the sectors that expand. The most consequential local margin is housing supply, because it determines whether the income that remote workers bring becomes jobs or becomes rents. The empirical findings rest on synthetic data, and the claims above must be tested on real labor markets before they can inform any decision. What the dissertation offers in the meantime is a framework, a set of estimators fit for the staggered structure of the problem, and a clear statement of what would need to be true for remote work to spread its gains widely in the places that receive it.

# Chapter 2: Remote Work, Place and Local Labor Markets: A Review

This chapter reviews the research on which the dissertation builds and locates the question it asks. Five bodies of work are relevant, and they have developed largely in isolation from one another. The first, mostly written since 2020, measures which jobs can be done remotely, how quickly remote work spread and why it has persisted. The second studies the geography of remote work: where remote-capable workers live, how their spending and housing demand moved when they stopped commuting, and how cities may reorganize as a result. The third is the older literature on local labor markets, export bases and local multipliers, which asks how a shock to one part of a local economy spreads to the rest. The fourth concerns agglomeration economies, the productivity advantages of density, which determine whether a local demand shock has effects beyond spending. The fifth is the recent econometric literature on difference-in-differences designs with staggered treatment timing, which governs how the empirical chapters estimate the effects of interest.

The chapter is organized by argument rather than by author. Section 2.1 reviews evidence on the feasibility and persistence of remote work and draws out what it implies for the mobility of remote-capable workers. Section 2.2 turns to the geography of remote work and argues that the literature has concentrated on origin cities and on reorganization within metropolitan areas, leaving the receiving side of between-market moves comparatively unexplored. Section 2.3 reviews the local labor market and multiplier literature and makes the case that remote workers are best understood as an export base. Section 2.4 discusses agglomeration economies and explains why the dissertation's model abstracts from them. Section 2.5 surveys the econometrics of staggered difference-in-differences and sets out the estimators and diagnostics used later. Section 2.6 draws the threads together and states the gap that the dissertation fills.

## 2.1 Remote work: feasibility, adoption and persistence

### 2.1.1 Which jobs can be done at home

Any study of the economic consequences of remote work must start with a measure of which work can be done remotely. Before 2020, systematic measures were scarce, partly because the question seemed of limited practical importance. The pandemic changed that, and two closely related contributions quickly became the standard references.

{{dingel2020|n}} classify each occupation according to whether its tasks can be performed entirely at home, using the detailed descriptions of work activities and work context in the O*NET database. An occupation is deemed not feasible for home working if, for example, it requires being outdoors most of the time, operating vehicles or equipment, handling objects, or dealing directly with the public. Merging this occupation-level classification with employment counts, they estimate that a substantial minority of US jobs could plausibly be done entirely at home. Two further features of their findings matter for this dissertation. Jobs that can be done at home pay more on average than jobs that cannot, so that remote-capable workers carry above-average incomes. And the share of such jobs varies widely across cities and industries, which means that the potential for remote work, and hence for remote-worker migration, is unevenly distributed across places.

{{mongey2021|n}} construct closely related occupation-level measures of work-from-home feasibility and of physical proximity to others at work, also from O*NET, and validate them against time-use data. Their emphasis is distributional. Workers in jobs with low feasibility for home working and high physical proximity are, on average, less educated and lower paid, hold fewer liquid assets and are more likely to rent their homes; in the early pandemic they also experienced larger employment declines. Read alongside {{dingel2020|n}}, this work establishes a sharp occupational divide: the workers who can work remotely are, in the main, not the workers who provide local in-person services.

That divide is central to the argument of this dissertation. If remote-capable workers are high earners whose jobs are tradable across space, and in-person service workers are lower earners who rent their housing, then any reallocation of remote-capable workers across places will affect the second group mainly through two channels: local demand for services and local demand for housing. Section 2.3 shows how the local labor market literature thinks about these channels; here it is enough to note that the occupational measures supply the classification on which the treatment definition in Chapter 4 relies.

Occupational classifications also have known limitations. They describe the feasibility of remote work, not its practice. A worker in a teleworkable occupation may be required by her employer to come to the office, and a worker in an occupation classified as not teleworkable may perform some tasks at home. The classification is therefore best understood as a measure of the potential for remote work. When it is used to classify migrants, as in this dissertation, it identifies the arrival of remote-capable workers rather than of remote workers per se. Chapter 4 returns to the implications of this distinction for interpretation, and Chapter 7 discusses it as a limitation.

### 2.1.2 The pandemic shift and its persistence

The shift to remote work in spring 2020 was abrupt and large. Using a nationally representative online survey fielded during the first months of the pandemic, {{brynjolfsson2020|n}} find that roughly half of previously employed workers were working from home, that a large fraction of them had been commuting until shortly before, and that the shift was larger in states whose occupational mix included more remote-capable jobs. This last result is important for the present purpose because it confirms that the occupational measures of feasibility predict actual remote work at the level of geographic units, not only of individual jobs.

An early shift of this kind could have proved temporary. Many observers expected that offices would refill once public-health restrictions were lifted. {{barrero2021stick|n}} challenged this expectation using repeated waves of a large survey of working arrangements and attitudes. They project that working from home would remain several times more common after the pandemic than before it and identify several reasons for persistence: workers' and managers' experiences of remote work were better than they had expected, firms and households made substantial investments in the equipment and practices that remote work requires, the stigma attached to working from home declined, and many workers came to value the option highly. They also predict that the shift would reduce spending in major city centers, as workers who no longer commute spend less near their offices.

Subsequent evidence has largely borne out the prediction of persistence. In a review of the evolution of working from home, {{barrero2023evolution|n}} document that the share of paid workdays performed at home in the United States remained, several years after the start of the pandemic, at a level several times higher than in 2019. They discuss the reasons the shift has endured, the ways in which it varies with worker characteristics and place, and its implications for pay, productivity and innovation. For this dissertation the crucial implication of persistence is that remote work is a durable feature of the labor market, so that decisions about where to live made on the assumption of continued remote work are not easily reversed. A remote worker who relocated to a smaller labor market in 2021 did so in the reasonable expectation that her arrangement would continue, and the receiving market's adjustment to her arrival is therefore an adjustment to a permanent change.

Persistence also matters for the econometric design. If remote work had faded after 2021, treatment in the sense defined in Chapter 4 would not be absorbing: markets that received remote workers might lose them again. The evidence on persistence supports modeling treatment as absorbing, which is the setting for which the estimators reviewed in Section 2.5 were developed.

### 2.1.3 Productivity, careers and the employer's view

A further strand of the remote-work literature examines its consequences inside the firm. The most influential study predates the pandemic. {{bloom2015|n}} report a randomized experiment at a large Chinese travel agency in which call-center employees who volunteered for home working were randomly assigned to work from home or to remain in the office. Home working raised performance and reduced attrition. But home workers were less likely to be promoted conditional on their performance, an early indication that physical presence may carry career benefits that productivity measures do not capture.

This evidence bears on the present question indirectly. It suggests that remote work need not reduce the productivity of remote workers, so that their national wage, $w^R$ in the model of Chapter 3, need not fall when they relocate. It also suggests a reason why some remote-capable workers may choose not to relocate far from their employers: the career costs of reduced presence. These costs plausibly encourage hybrid arrangements over fully remote ones, which bears on the geography of remote work discussed next. The model in Chapter 3 treats the remote wage as exogenous to the receiving market, an assumption the productivity evidence makes reasonable for the purposes of a local analysis.

### 2.1.4 Implications for mobility

The evidence reviewed in this section establishes three facts that underpin the rest of the dissertation. First, a substantial share of jobs can be done remotely, and these jobs are concentrated among higher earners. Second, the use of remote work rose sharply in 2020 and has persisted at a much higher level than before. Third, remote work need not reduce productivity, so that a remote worker's earnings are largely independent of where she lives. Together these facts imply that, for a meaningful share of the workforce, the choice of residence has been partly decoupled from the choice of employer. The remaining sections ask what that decoupling does to places.

## 2.2 The geography of remote work

### 2.2.1 Where remote-capable work is located

Remote-capable work is not evenly distributed across space. {{althoff2022|n}} show that denser US cities specialize in high-skill service jobs that can be done remotely, so that the potential for remote work is concentrated in the largest metropolitan areas. This spatial concentration had a direct consequence when the pandemic arrived. When high-skill workers in these cities went remote, their spending on local consumer services, from restaurants and bars to dry cleaners and personal care, fell sharply, because so much of that spending had taken place near their workplaces. The authors argue that low-skill service workers in large cities bore a substantial share of the economic impact of the shift, a burden distinct from the direct effects of the pandemic on health and public-health restrictions.

The contribution of {{althoff2022|n}} is central to this dissertation for two reasons. Substantively, it demonstrates that remote work transmits economic effects to local service workers through the spending of remote-capable workers, which is exactly the channel at the core of the export-base framing developed in Section 2.3. Methodologically, it frames the question at the level of the local economy rather than the individual worker. Its focus, however, is on the places remote workers left, or more precisely on the places whose central business districts they stopped visiting. The symmetric question of what happens in the places where remote workers spend more is left open.

### 2.2.2 Reorganization within metropolitan areas

A second group of studies examines how remote work has reshaped the internal geography of metropolitan areas. Using change-of-address data and online real estate prices and rents, {{ramani2021|n}} document what they call a donut effect: population, business activity and real estate demand moved from dense city centers toward the suburbs of large metros after 2020, while the outer edge of the metro area changed comparatively little. The authors attribute the pattern largely to hybrid work. A worker who commutes two or three days a week rather than five can tolerate a longer commute on those days, and so moves to a larger, cheaper home farther from the center while remaining within the same labor market.

{{gupta2022|n}} reach a complementary conclusion from the housing market. They show that house prices and rents fell in city centers and rose farther out after the onset of the pandemic, flattening the bid-rent curve in most large US metros. Two features of their results anticipate this dissertation's findings. The flattening was greater where remote work was more prevalent, consistent with remote work rather than other pandemic forces being the cause. And it was greater where housing supply was less elastic, consistent with the general principle that demand shocks are capitalized into prices where quantities cannot adjust. The same principle, applied to between-market rather than within-market moves, underlies Proposition 3.2 in Chapter 3 and the heterogeneity results in Chapter 6.

These within-metro studies establish that remote work moves housing demand and that the price consequences depend on supply elasticity. But their unit of analysis is the location within a metro, and their comparisons are between the center and the suburbs. The movements they document are, for the most part, movements within a single local labor market. A worker who moves from the center to the suburbs of the same commuting zone has not changed labor markets at all. Such moves redistribute spending within a market, but they do not inject new income into it. The export-base mechanism that is the focus of this dissertation operates only when remote-capable workers move between labor markets.

### 2.2.3 Quantitative spatial models of remote work

A third approach uses quantitative spatial models to simulate the long-run consequences of remote work. {{delventhal2022|n}} build a model of the Los Angeles metropolitan area with agglomeration externalities and endogenous congestion, and simulate a permanent rise in telecommuting. The model predicts that jobs concentrate further in the core while residents move to the periphery, that commuting times fall, and that real estate prices in the core decline relative to those in the periphery. The approach has the advantage of internal consistency: the general-equilibrium interactions among housing, commuting and agglomeration are modeled explicitly, and welfare can be computed for different groups.

The model in Chapter 3 shares the spatial-equilibrium logic of this approach but differs in two respects. It is deliberately simpler, sacrificing quantitative realism for transparent comparative statics that can be tested in a reduced-form panel. And it is set across labor markets rather than within one, so that the central margin is the choice of market by remote workers rather than the choice of neighborhood by hybrid workers. The two approaches are complements. Quantitative models of single metros are well suited to studying within-market reorganization; a stylized multi-market model paired with a panel of markets is better suited to studying the export-base effect of between-market moves.

### 2.2.4 Hybrid and fully remote workers

A distinction implicit in the studies above deserves to be made explicit, because it determines which remote workers can generate an export-base effect at all. Hybrid workers, who commute to an employer's site on some days, remain tied to the labor market in which that employer is located. They can move within it, which is the margin that {{ramani2021|n}} and {{gupta2022|n}} study, but they cannot easily leave it. Fully remote workers face no such constraint. They can, in principle, live in any labor market with adequate connectivity, and their choice among markets is governed by housing costs, amenities and personal ties rather than by commuting distance.

For the receiving market, only the second group generates a genuine income injection in the sense of Section 2.3. A hybrid worker who moves from a city center to its suburbs relocates spending within a market; a fully remote worker who moves from a large metro to a smaller market brings spending into a new one. The evidence on persistence {{barrero2023evolution}} suggests that both arrangements have become common, but it does not by itself reveal how many workers have used their flexibility to change labor markets. This is one reason why the treatment definition in Chapter 4 is based on observed net in-migration of remote-capable workers into a market rather than on the prevalence of remote work among its residents. A market can have many residents who work from home some of the time without having received any income injection from outside; what matters for the export-base mechanism is the arrival of workers whose earnings originate elsewhere.

The distinction also clarifies why the receiving-side effects studied here need not be the mirror image of the origin-side effects documented by {{althoff2022|n}}. Origin cities lost spending from both hybrid and fully remote workers who stopped visiting their central business districts, much of which was redistributed within the same metro. Receiving markets gain only the spending of workers who actually moved. The two sets of effects are related but not symmetric, and neither can be inferred from the other.

### 2.2.5 What the geography literature leaves open

The literature on the geography of remote work has made rapid progress, but it has concentrated on two questions: what happened to the centers of large cities when their workers stopped commuting, and how metropolitan areas reorganize internally when commuting becomes less frequent. It has said comparatively little about the labor markets that gained residents through between-market moves. This is understandable. The early effects on city centers were dramatic and immediately visible, and the data needed to study within-metro moves, such as change-of-address records and online listings, were available quickly. Between-market moves of remote workers are harder to measure, because identifying them requires knowing both that a worker moved and that her job can be done remotely.

The receiving side of between-market moves is nonetheless where the export-base mechanism operates most clearly. In a smaller labor market, a modest inflow of remote workers can represent a meaningful addition to local income, and the consequences for local services, wages and housing may be large relative to the size of the local economy. Moreover, the receiving markets are diverse. Some have elastic housing supply and some do not; some are amenity-rich destinations and some are not. This diversity provides the variation needed to test the model's predictions about how the effects of remote-worker arrivals depend on local conditions. The dissertation's empirical strategy is designed to exploit it.

## 2.3 Local labor markets, export bases and multipliers

### 2.3.1 Defining a local labor market

Any analysis of local labor markets requires a definition of the local. Political boundaries such as counties or states are convenient but economically arbitrary: a county may contain only part of a labor market, and a state usually contains many. The standard solution in US empirical work is the commuting zone. {{tolbert1996|n}} use journey-to-work data from the 1990 Census and hierarchical cluster analysis of county-to-county commuting flows to group all US counties into several hundred commuting zones, each intended to capture an area within which people both live and work. Because commuting zones are defined by observed commuting patterns rather than by administrative boundaries, they correspond more closely than counties to the economic concept of a local labor market.

The delineation of commuting zones is not without difficulty. {{fowler2016|n}} replicate and evaluate the original delineations, produce new zones for 2010 and revised zones for earlier years using a consistent and purely algorithmic method, and show that the resulting zones are sensitive to methodological choices such as the clustering threshold. Their work is a reminder that commuting zones are a modeling choice rather than a natural fact, and that results based on them should be robust to reasonable alternatives. The panel used in this dissertation is modelled on commuting zones in the sense of these two contributions; Appendix A discusses how the definitions were operationalized and what sensitivity checks were performed.

A subtle issue arises when commuting zones are used to study remote work. Commuting zones are defined by commuting patterns, and remote work by definition weakens the link between residence and workplace. A remote worker who lives in one commuting zone and works for an employer in another does not appear in the commuting flows that define either zone, because she does not commute. For the purpose of this dissertation, this is not a weakness but the point: the remote worker's residence is in one local labor market, and her employment is effectively outside any local labor market. The commuting-zone definition of the local therefore correctly places her spending in the market where she lives, while her earnings are determined elsewhere.

### 2.3.2 Local shocks and their persistence

Commuting zones became the workhorse unit for the study of local labor markets largely through two influential papers. {{autor2013china|n}} measure each commuting zone's exposure to rising Chinese imports through its initial industry mix, instrumenting with Chinese imports to other high-income countries to isolate the component of import growth driven by supply conditions in China. They find that more exposed local labor markets experienced higher unemployment, lower labor-force participation and lower wages, as well as larger increases in transfer payments. {{autor2013services|n}} use the same geographic unit to study technological change. They link the growth of low-skill service occupations and the polarization of the US labor market to the falling cost of automating routine tasks, and show that commuting zones initially specialized in routine work adopted computers more intensively, reallocated low-skill workers into service occupations and experienced greater polarization of wages and employment.

These papers matter here for three reasons. First, they establish that local labor markets do not adjust instantaneously to shocks: the effects of trade and technology shocks persist, because workers do not move freely and local wages and employment do not quickly return to their previous paths. This persistence is what makes local shocks worth studying and what makes the dynamic patterns in Chapter 5 economically meaningful. Second, they demonstrate a research design, exposure-based variation across commuting zones combined with careful attention to confounding, that has become a template for the field. Third, {{autor2013services|n}} highlight the importance of local low-skill service employment as a margin of adjustment. The service jobs that their analysis shows absorbing workers displaced by automation are the same non-tradable jobs whose growth this dissertation attributes to remote-worker arrivals.

There is, however, an important difference between the shocks these papers study and the one studied here. Import competition and automation are shocks to local production: they reduce the demand for local workers in tradable sectors, and their effects on non-tradables arise indirectly through lost local income. Remote-worker arrivals are a shock to local income that does not pass through local production at all. The local labor market literature has developed a rich understanding of negative production shocks; it has had less occasion to study pure income shocks of the kind that remote work generates.

### 2.3.3 Export bases and local multipliers

The idea that a local economy can be divided into a tradable export base and a non-tradable sector that serves local demand has a long history in regional economics. Its modern empirical form is the literature on local multipliers. {{moretti2010|n}} estimates long-run local employment multipliers across US cities, asking how many additional non-tradable jobs are created in a city when it gains tradable jobs. He finds that each additional tradable job in manufacturing is associated with more than one additional non-tradable job in the same city. The mechanism is local spending: tradable workers spend part of their income on local goods and services, raising local labor demand in the non-tradable sector. The framework also suggests why the multiplier should depend on local labor and housing supply: when a local demand shock raises wages and rents, the cost of producing non-tradables rises, and the employment response is dampened.

{{moretti2012|n}} develops the broader argument in a book-length treatment. He argues that the US economy has seen a growing divergence among cities, driven by the concentration of innovation-sector employment in a small number of hubs. A key element of the argument is that high-skill jobs generate especially large local multipliers, because high earners spend more on local services and because their presence supports higher wages for less-skilled workers in the same city. On this view, the location of high-skill workers matters not only for their own productivity but for the employment and earnings of everyone around them.

The connection to remote work is direct. If high-skill tradable jobs create local service jobs through their workers' spending, then high-skill remote workers who relocate to a new market should create local service jobs there, because they bring their spending with them. Remote workers are, in this sense, an export base: they produce tradable output, they are paid by employers outside the local economy, and their income enters the local economy through spending. But they differ from the conventional export base in a way that makes them analytically useful. A conventional tradable job brings with it a local employer, whose own purchases of local inputs and whose effects on local productivity also contribute to the multiplier. A remote worker brings only her spending. The remote-worker multiplier therefore isolates the household-spending component of the local multiplier from the components that operate through local firms.

This observation generates a testable expectation. Because remote workers bring only the spending channel, their multiplier should be smaller than the conventional multiplier for high-skill tradable jobs, all else equal. The estimate in Chapter 5, of 0.6 local non-tradable jobs per remote worker, should be read in this light. It is not directly comparable to conventional estimates, which include channels that remote workers lack and are estimated in different settings, but its relative size is consistent with the export-base interpretation.

### 2.3.4 Spatial equilibrium and the incidence of local shocks

The multiplier literature focuses on quantities. To understand who gains from a local demand shock, one also needs to understand prices. The natural framework is spatial equilibrium, in which mobile workers choose where to live by comparing wages, housing costs and amenities across locations, and in which these prices adjust until no worker wishes to move. In this framework, a local demand shock raises local wages and rents, and the division of the shock between the two depends on the elasticities of labor and housing supply.

Spatial-equilibrium reasoning has a clear implication for incidence. Where housing supply is elastic, a local demand shock is accommodated largely by new construction, rents rise little and the shock's benefits accrue mainly to workers through higher employment and wages. Where housing supply is inelastic, the shock is capitalized into land values, rents rise substantially and the benefits accrue mainly to landowners. This is the mechanism behind the finding of {{gupta2022|n}} that within-metro price changes were larger where housing supply was less elastic, and it is the mechanism behind Propositions 3.1 and 3.2 in Chapter 3.

Two features of remote-worker arrivals make the incidence question especially pointed. First, remote workers raise housing demand without raising labor demand for themselves; their wage is set elsewhere. They therefore compete with incumbent residents for housing while not competing for local jobs. Second, as Section 2.1 showed, the incumbent residents most exposed to rising rents, renters working in local services, are disproportionately lower paid {{mongey2021}}. If the wage gains from expanded service demand are smaller than the rent increases, the arrival of remote workers transfers income from incumbent renters to incumbent owners and to arriving workers. Whether this happens is an empirical question, and Chapter 6 addresses it.

## 2.4 Agglomeration economies

### 2.4.1 Mechanisms

A long tradition in urban economics holds that density raises productivity. {{duranton2004|n}} organize the theoretical micro-foundations of these agglomeration economies into three mechanisms: sharing, in which firms in a dense location share indivisible facilities, a wide variety of input suppliers and a deep pool of workers; matching, in which a larger market improves the quality or probability of matches between workers and firms; and learning, in which proximity facilitates the generation, diffusion and accumulation of knowledge. They develop core models for each mechanism and compare their aggregate and welfare implications.

{{rosenthal2004|n}} survey the empirical evidence. They show that agglomeration economies operate across several dimensions, including industrial scope, geographic scope and temporal scope, and that they attenuate with distance. They also review the evidence on the classic Marshallian sources, labor-market pooling, input sharing and knowledge spillovers, and find support for each. A recurring theme is that agglomeration benefits depend on interaction among workers and firms, which is facilitated by physical proximity.

### 2.4.2 Learning and selection

Two further contributions refine the picture. {{glaeser2001|n}} examine the urban wage premium and find that much of it accrues over time spent in a city rather than immediately on arrival. They interpret this as evidence that cities speed up the accumulation of human capital, consistent with learning-based agglomeration economies. {{combes2012|n}} address the concern that the productivity advantages of large cities might reflect the selection of more productive firms into them rather than agglomeration per se. Nesting a model of firm selection within a model of agglomeration, they show that selection should truncate the lower tail of the productivity distribution in large cities while agglomeration should shift and dilate the entire distribution. Applying a quantile approach to French establishment data, they find that the productivity advantages of large cities are explained mainly by agglomeration rather than selection.

### 2.4.3 Why remote workers may not bring agglomeration benefits

The agglomeration literature matters for this dissertation primarily as a reason for a modeling choice. If agglomeration economies are generated by interaction among workers and firms in close proximity, then a remote worker who lives in a market but works for an employer elsewhere is unlikely to contribute much to them. She does not share local inputs with local firms, she does not enter the local job-matching market, and while she may exchange ideas with her neighbors, her professional interactions take place largely with colleagues elsewhere. If anything, the learning mechanism emphasized by {{glaeser2001|n}} suggests that remote workers forgo some of the human-capital benefits of the cities where their employers are located, a concern that echoes the promotion penalty found by {{bloom2015|n}}.

The model in Chapter 3 therefore abstracts from agglomeration economies in the receiving market. This is not a claim that agglomeration economies are unimportant in general, which would contradict the evidence reviewed above. It is a claim that the specific shock studied here, the arrival of workers whose production takes place elsewhere, is unlikely to generate them locally to a significant degree. The claim is testable. If remote workers did generate local agglomeration benefits, local tradable employment and productivity should rise after their arrival. The model predicts that tradable employment should respond little, and Chapter 6 reports a tradable employment effect close to zero, which supports the abstraction.

There is a further reason to be cautious about agglomeration in this context. The receiving markets in the panel are, on average, smaller than the large metros from which remote workers originate; the median treated market has 61,000 jobs. The agglomeration literature suggests that agglomeration economies scale with density and market size {{combes2012}}, so that even if remote workers did participate in local interactions, the gains in smaller markets might be modest. The empirical focus on non-tradables, wages and housing is therefore not an omission but a reflection of where the main effects of remote-worker arrivals are expected to lie.

## 2.5 The econometrics of staggered difference-in-differences

### 2.5.1 From the canonical design to two-way fixed effects

The canonical difference-in-differences design compares the change in outcomes over time for a treated group with the change for an untreated group. With two groups and two periods, and under the assumption that the groups would have followed parallel paths in the absence of treatment, the difference in their changes identifies the average treatment effect on the treated. The design's appeal lies in its transparency and in the mildness of its identifying assumption, which allows the groups to differ in levels so long as their trends would have been the same.

Many applications, including this one, have more than two periods and units that are treated at different times. The conventional approach to such settings has been the two-way fixed-effects regression

$$
y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta\,D_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \tag{?}
$$

where $y_{it}$ is the outcome for unit $i$ in period $t$, $\alpha_i$ and $\lambda_t$ are unit and period fixed effects, $D_{it}$ is an indicator equal to one if unit $i$ is treated in period $t$, and $\beta$ is interpreted as the treatment effect. A dynamic version replaces $D_{it}$ with a set of indicators for periods relative to treatment, producing an event-study regression whose leads are used to assess pre-trends and whose lags trace out the dynamics of the effect. Chapter 4 sets out both forms of the regression as applied to the panel.

For many years, Equation (2.1) and its event-study counterpart were treated as straightforward generalizations of the two-by-two design. A cluster of papers published around 2020 and 2021 showed that this interpretation is correct only under restrictive conditions. When treatment effects are the same for all units and constant over time, $\beta$ recovers that common effect. When effects vary across cohorts or over time since treatment, $\beta$ can be a poor summary of them, and in some cases can have the wrong sign.

### 2.5.2 Decompositions and negative weights

{{goodmanbacon2021|n}} shows that, with staggered treatment timing, the two-way fixed-effects estimator equals a weighted average of all possible two-group, two-period difference-in-differences comparisons that can be formed from the data. Some of these comparisons are unproblematic: they compare a treated cohort with never-treated units, or a cohort treated earlier with one not yet treated. Others use units that have already been treated as controls for units treated later. These later-versus-earlier comparisons are valid only if the treatment effect for the earlier cohort is constant over time. If the effect for the earlier cohort is growing, its outcomes continue to rise after its treatment, and using it as a control subtracts part of its own treatment effect from the estimate for the later cohort. The weights in the decomposition depend on the size of each group and the variance of treatment within each comparison, so that cohorts treated near the middle of the panel receive disproportionate weight. Goodman-Bacon provides a diagnostic decomposition that reports each comparison's estimate and weight, allowing researchers to see where the two-way fixed-effects coefficient comes from.

{{dechaisemartin2020|n}} reach a related conclusion from a different angle. They show that the two-way fixed-effects coefficient estimates a weighted sum of the average treatment effects in each group and period, and that some of the weights can be negative. When effects are heterogeneous, the negative weights mean that the coefficient can lie outside the range of the underlying effects and can even have the opposite sign from all of them. They propose computing the weights as a diagnostic and offer an alternative estimator that is robust to heterogeneous effects.

{{sun2021|n}} extend the analysis to event-study regressions. They show that, with heterogeneous effects across cohorts, the coefficient on a given lead or lag in a two-way fixed-effects event study is contaminated by effects from other relative periods. A particularly troubling implication is that apparent pre-trends can arise purely from treatment-effect heterogeneity, so that the usual practice of inspecting leads for evidence of differential trends can mislead even when parallel trends holds.

These results have a common structure that can be stated compactly. Let $\tau_{gt}$ denote the average treatment effect in period $t$ for the cohort first treated in period $g$. The two-way fixed-effects coefficient can be written as

$$
\beta^{TWFE} = \sum_{g}\sum_{t \geq g} \omega_{gt}\,\tau_{gt}, \qquad \sum_{g}\sum_{t \geq g} \omega_{gt} = 1 \tag{?}
$$

where the weights $\omega_{gt}$ are determined by the design rather than by the researcher's choice of estimand and need not all be positive. Equation (2.2) makes clear why the estimator is reliable when $\tau_{gt}$ is constant and unreliable otherwise. In the setting of this dissertation, effects are expected to grow with time since treatment, as local businesses open and housing markets adjust, and to differ across cohorts, which makes the problems identified in this literature directly relevant. Chapter 5 reports both the Goodman-Bacon decomposition and the share of negative weights for the employment estimates.

### 2.5.3 Heterogeneity-robust estimators

The diagnosis was quickly followed by remedies. The common idea is to separate the estimation of treatment effects for each cohort and period from the aggregation of those effects into summary measures, so that the aggregation weights are chosen by the researcher rather than imposed by the regression, and to ensure that already-treated units are never used as controls.

{{callaway2021|n}} develop methods for settings with multiple periods, staggered treatment timing and parallel trends that may hold only conditional on covariates. Their building block is the group-time average treatment effect, the average effect in period $t$ for units first treated in period $g$:

$$
ATT(g,t) = \mathbb{E}\left[\,Y_t(g) - Y_t(0) \mid G = g\,\right] \tag{?}
$$

where $Y_t(g)$ is the potential outcome in period $t$ if first treated in $g$, $Y_t(0)$ is the untreated potential outcome, and $G$ denotes a unit's treatment cohort. Under parallel trends and no anticipation, each $ATT(g,t)$ in Equation (2.3) is identified by a simple comparison of the change in outcomes from period $g-1$ to period $t$ for cohort $g$ with the corresponding change for a comparison group of never-treated or not-yet-treated units. The group-time effects can then be aggregated into an overall average, into averages by cohort, or into event-study averages by time since treatment, and the authors provide bootstrap-based procedures for simultaneous inference. Chapter 4 specifies the version used in this dissertation and its aggregation.

{{sun2021|n}} propose an interaction-weighted estimator for event studies. It estimates cohort-specific effects at each relative period by interacting relative-period indicators with cohort indicators, and then averages them across cohorts with non-negative weights. The resulting estimates are convex averages of cohort-specific effects and are free of the contamination that afflicts conventional event-study coefficients.

{{borusyak2024|n}} take an imputation approach. They show that conventional regression-based event-study estimators are biased absent strong homogeneity restrictions and derive an efficient estimator that proceeds in three steps: fit unit and period fixed effects using only untreated observations, use the fitted model to impute the untreated potential outcome for each treated observation, and average the differences between observed and imputed outcomes with researcher-chosen weights. Under their assumptions the estimator is efficient, and it accommodates time-varying covariates and more complex designs.

The three estimators rest on similar identifying assumptions but differ in their choice of comparison group, their use of pre-treatment periods and their efficiency. Callaway and Sant'Anna use only the period immediately before treatment as the base, which makes the estimator robust to some violations of parallel trends in earlier periods but potentially less efficient. Borusyak, Jaravel and Spiess use all untreated periods, which improves efficiency but places more weight on parallel trends across the whole pre-period. Sun and Abraham sit between the two. Reporting all three, as Chapter 5 does, therefore provides a form of robustness check: if the estimates agree, the conclusions do not depend on these choices. In the present application they do agree closely.

### 2.5.4 Assessing and relaxing parallel trends

Even heterogeneity-robust estimators rely on parallel trends. The conventional way to assess the assumption is to test whether pre-treatment event-study coefficients are jointly zero. {{roth2023|n}} review the limitations of this practice. Pre-trend tests may have low power against economically meaningful violations, so that a failure to reject is weak evidence for parallel trends; and conditioning the analysis on passing a pre-test can distort subsequent inference. Their review organizes the recent literature around relaxations of the canonical model, covering staggered timing, potential violations of parallel trends and alternative frameworks for inference, and offers practical recommendations that inform the empirical approach of this dissertation.

{{rambachan2023|n}} propose a more credible approach. Instead of assuming that parallel trends holds exactly, they bound how much post-treatment violations can differ from the violations observed before treatment. One class of restrictions, relative magnitudes, assumes that the post-treatment violation in any period is no larger than $\bar{M}$ times the largest pre-treatment violation. Under such restrictions the treatment effect is partially identified, and the authors derive confidence sets that are uniformly valid. The researcher can then report the largest value of $\bar{M}$ for which the effect remains significantly different from zero, a breakdown value that summarizes how robust the conclusion is to departures from parallel trends. Chapter 5 reports this analysis for the employment effect four quarters after treatment.

### 2.5.5 Inference

Difference-in-differences designs raise distinctive inference problems, because outcomes are typically serially correlated within units and treatment is assigned at the level of units over many periods. {{bertrand2004|n}} demonstrated the severity of the problem using placebo laws assigned at random to US states in wage data. Because conventional standard errors ignore serial correlation, they badly overstate precision, and placebo laws were found to have significant effects far more often than nominal test sizes would allow. The authors evaluated several remedies, including collapsing the data into pre- and post-treatment periods and allowing for an arbitrary covariance structure within states.

{{cameron2015|n}} provide a practical guide to cluster-robust inference. They cover cluster-robust variance estimation, its interaction with fixed effects, corrections for settings with few clusters, including the wild cluster bootstrap, and multiway clustering. Their guidance informs two choices in this dissertation: standard errors are clustered at the level of the labor market, the level at which treatment is assigned, and wild cluster bootstrap inference is used for estimates based on individual cohorts, where the number of treated clusters is smaller.

{{abadie2023|n}} reframe the question of when to cluster. They argue that clustering is a matter of design, motivated by clustered sampling or by clustered assignment of treatment, rather than merely by the presence of correlated residuals within clusters, and they propose variance estimators for cases in which conventional cluster-robust standard errors are overly conservative. In the present design, treatment is assigned at the level of the labor market, which justifies clustering at that level on design grounds. Their argument also implies that market-clustered standard errors may in some cases be conservative; the dissertation nonetheless reports them as the default, on the grounds that conservative inference is preferable in a first study of this question.

### 2.5.6 Lessons for the empirical design

The econometric literature reviewed in this section translates into five concrete choices in the chapters that follow. First, the conventional two-way fixed-effects estimate is reported, but as a benchmark to be diagnosed rather than as the headline result. Its divergence from the robust estimates is itself informative, because it reveals whether treatment effects in this setting are dynamic and heterogeneous enough to matter. Second, the headline estimates come from the Callaway–Sant'Anna estimator, with the Sun–Abraham and Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess estimators reported alongside it, so that the conclusions do not rest on a single choice of comparison group or base period. Third, both not-yet-treated and never-treated markets are used as controls in the main specification, and Appendix B shows the effect of restricting the comparison group to one or the other. The choice matters because markets treated late in the panel may differ from those never treated, and the not-yet-treated comparison uses only the former.

Fourth, pre-trends are examined in the event study, but the conclusions are not allowed to rest on a failure to reject. Following {{roth2023|n}}, the dissertation reports the sensitivity analysis of {{rambachan2023|n}} alongside the conventional joint test, so that the reader can judge how large a departure from parallel trends would be needed to overturn the main findings. Fifth, inference is clustered at the level at which treatment is assigned, and estimates based on a small number of treated markets, such as the cohort-specific effects, are accompanied by bootstrap inference.

One further lesson concerns anticipation. All of the robust estimators assume that units do not change their behavior before treatment in anticipation of it. In the present setting, anticipation could arise if, for example, local businesses expanded in expectation of arrivals that had been publicized in advance, or if remote workers who had decided to move began spending locally before their recorded move. The treatment definition in Chapter 4, which requires two consecutive quarters of net in-migration above the threshold, dates treatment from the first of those quarters, which limits the scope for anticipation to arise from the definition itself. Whether anticipation is present in the data can be judged from the event-study coefficients immediately before treatment, which Chapter 5 reports.

## 2.6 Synthesis and the gap

The five literatures reviewed in this chapter have each been developed with different questions in mind, but together they frame the question this dissertation asks.

The remote-work literature establishes that a substantial share of jobs, concentrated among higher earners, can be done remotely; that remote work rose sharply in 2020 and has persisted; and that remote workers' earnings are largely independent of where they live {{dingel2020;barrero2021stick;barrero2023evolution;bloom2015;brynjolfsson2020}}. The geography literature shows that remote work moves spending and housing demand, that the consequences for local service workers can be severe {{althoff2022}}, and that price responses depend on housing supply {{gupta2022}}; but it has focused on origin cities and on reorganization within metropolitan areas {{ramani2021;delventhal2022}}. The local labor market literature provides the unit of analysis {{tolbert1996;fowler2016}}, the evidence that local shocks have persistent local effects {{autor2013china;autor2013services}} and the concept of the local multiplier {{moretti2010;moretti2012}}; but it has mostly studied shocks to local production rather than pure shocks to local income. The agglomeration literature explains why conventional export-base shocks may have effects beyond spending {{duranton2004;rosenthal2004;glaeser2001;combes2012}}, and why remote-worker arrivals may not. And the econometric literature shows that the conventional estimator for staggered designs can mislead when effects are heterogeneous, and supplies robust alternatives and diagnostics {{goodmanbacon2021;dechaisemartin2020;sun2021;callaway2021;borusyak2024;roth2023;rambachan2023}}.

The gap that emerges is the receiving side of between-market remote-worker migration. No study in this reading list estimates, for the labor markets that gain remote workers, the effects of their arrival on local non-tradable employment, the implied local multiplier, the responses of wages, rents and house prices, or the incidence of these changes across renters and owners. Filling this gap requires a framework that treats remote workers as an export base, a panel of local labor markets with staggered arrivals, and an estimation strategy robust to the heterogeneous and dynamic effects that such a setting is likely to produce. The dissertation provides all three.

Three expectations follow from the review and are formalized in the next chapter. First, if remote workers act as an export base, their arrival should raise local non-tradable employment, with a multiplier smaller than that associated with conventional high-skill tradable jobs because only the spending channel is present. Second, because remote workers raise housing demand without raising local labor demand for themselves, part of the shock should be capitalized into rents and house prices, more so where housing supply is inelastic. Third, the incidence of these changes should favor owners over renters, with the balance for renters working in local services depending on the relative size of wage and rent increases.

Chapter 3 now builds a spatial-equilibrium model in which these expectations can be derived precisely, stated as testable propositions and connected to the estimands of the empirical chapters.

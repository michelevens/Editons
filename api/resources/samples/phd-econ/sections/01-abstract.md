# Abstract

When workers can do their jobs from anywhere, the place where income is earned and the place where it is spent come apart. This dissertation studies what happens to a local labor market when remote-capable workers move in, bringing earnings generated by tradable work performed for employers elsewhere. I argue that such workers act as a new kind of export base: their income enters the local economy much as the revenue of an exporting plant does, but without any local production of the exported good.

I develop a spatial-equilibrium model with tradable and non-tradable sectors, local workers and remote workers whose wage is set nationally. The model predicts that remote-worker arrivals raise non-tradable employment and housing rents, that the employment response is weaker and the rent response stronger where housing supply is inelastic, and that the gains are unevenly shared between renters and owners.

I test these predictions on a quarterly panel of 600 labor markets modelled on US commuting zones, 2017Q1–2024Q4, in which 306 markets become treated in four cohorts between 2020Q3 and 2022Q1. Because treatment timing is staggered and effects grow over time, I rely on heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences estimators. Non-tradable service employment rises by 2.1 percent on average after treatment (Callaway–Sant'Anna; SE 0.6), building to 3.0 percent eight quarters on, with no evidence of pre-trends. A conventional two-way fixed-effects regression understates the effect at 1.3 percent, because 38 percent of its weight falls on comparisons that use earlier-treated markets as controls. Each additional remote worker is associated with 0.6 additional local non-tradable jobs. Non-tradable wages rise by 1.2 percent, rents by 4.6 percent and house prices by 6.3 percent, while tradable employment is unchanged. Rent effects are more than twice as large in markets with inelastic housing supply.

The incidence of these changes is regressive. Incumbent homeowners gain; renters working in local services lose whenever rent exceeds about a quarter of their income. The results suggest that the local benefits of attracting remote workers depend heavily on whether housing supply can respond.

# Chapter 4: Data, Definitions and Empirical Strategy

Chapter 3 derived a set of predictions about what should happen to a local labor market when remote-capable workers arrive: non-tradable employment should rise, by less where housing is inelastically supplied; rents and non-tradable wages should rise, by more where housing is inelastically supplied; tradable employment should barely move; and the effects should build over time as the inflow accumulates. This chapter describes how those predictions are taken to data. It does four things. It describes the panel of local labor markets on which all the estimates rest. It defines the treatment, the arrival of a sustained inflow of remote-capable workers, and the outcomes. It presents the cohort structure and descriptive statistics that any reader needs before interpreting a staggered difference-in-differences design. And it sets out the estimating equations, the identifying assumptions and the approach to inference that Chapters 5 and 6 apply.

The design problem is easy to state. Remote-worker arrivals were not randomly assigned. They began at different dates in different markets, and they probably had effects that differed across markets and grew with time since arrival. The first feature means that identification must rest on a comparison of trends rather than levels, and that the comparison must be defended. The second and third features mean that the most familiar estimator for staggered designs, the two-way fixed-effects regression, is not reliable, because it can assign negative weight to some treatment effects and can use already-treated markets as controls {{goodmanbacon2021;dechaisemartin2020}}. The strategy adopted here therefore treats the conventional estimator as a benchmark and relies for inference on estimators built for staggered adoption with heterogeneous effects {{callaway2021;sun2021;borusyak2024}}, following the recommendations synthesized by {{roth2023|n}}.

The chapter is organized as follows. Section 4.1 describes the panel. Section 4.2 defines remote-capable workers and the treatment. Section 4.3 presents the treatment cohorts in Table 4.1 and Figure 4.1. Section 4.4 defines the outcomes and covariates. Section 4.5 reports the descriptive statistics in Table 4.2. Section 4.6 sets out the estimating equations, Equations (4.1) to (4.4). Section 4.7 discusses identification and Section 4.8 inference. Section 4.9 summarizes.

## 4.1 The Labor-Market Panel

### 4.1.1 Geographic units

The unit of analysis is the local labor market. The theoretical object of Chapter 3 is a market within which residents both live and spend, so that the non-tradable demand generated by a remote worker's income is met by firms and workers located in the same market. The empirical counterpart must therefore be large enough to contain most commuting and most local consumption, and small enough that markets differ in their exposure to remote-worker inflows. In US empirical work the standard choice is the commuting zone, a cluster of counties grouped by the strength of the commuting flows between them so that most residents of a zone also work in it {{tolbert1996}}. Commuting zones have been the unit of analysis in influential studies of local labor-market adjustment to trade shocks {{autor2013china}} and to the automation of routine tasks {{autor2013services}}, and their use here places the estimates within the same tradition.

Commuting zones are not uniquely defined. Their boundaries depend on the clustering method and on the cutoffs used to decide when two counties belong together, and different vintages can produce noticeably different zones {{fowler2016}}. The panel fixes a single set of 600 market boundaries for the whole period, so that no change in an outcome can arise from a change in geography. The 600 markets are modeled on US commuting zones in their structure and in the right-skewed distribution of their sizes, but they are not actual commuting zones.

### 4.1.2 Period and frequency

The panel is quarterly and runs from 2017Q1 to 2024Q4, a total of 32 quarters. With 600 markets it contains 19,200 market-quarter observations, and it is balanced: every market is observed in every quarter for every variable. Three features of this window matter for the design. The twelve quarters from 2017Q1 to 2019Q4 precede any possible treatment and provide the pre-period against which parallel trends can be assessed. The window begins early enough that every treatment cohort is observed for at least fourteen quarters before onset. And it ends late enough that every cohort is observed for at least twelve quarters after onset, so that the event-study profile from eight quarters before to eight quarters after onset is estimated from the same set of cohorts at every horizon.

The quarterly frequency is a compromise. Annual data would smooth over the timing of arrivals, which in the panel are concentrated in a two-year window, and would make it impossible to distinguish an effect that emerges within a year of onset from one that takes two. Monthly data would add noise without adding economically meaningful variation in the slow-moving outcomes studied here.

> **Sample note.** The data reported in this thesis are synthetic, created to illustrate how such a study is reported. No participants were recruited.

The panel, its construction and the consistency checks applied before estimation are documented in Appendix A. Because the panel is synthetic, none of the numbers reported in this or later chapters describes the actual United States. Facts about the real economy enter the dissertation only through the cited literature: for example, that a large share of US jobs could plausibly be performed entirely at home {{dingel2020}}, that the shift to remote work during the pandemic was large and fast {{brynjolfsson2020}}, and that a substantial share of working days continued to be worked from home well after the pandemic {{barrero2021stick;barrero2023evolution}}.

## 4.2 Remote-Capable Workers and the Treatment

### 4.2.1 Classifying remote-capable workers

The treatment is defined by the migration of workers who are able to work remotely. Following {{dingel2020|n}}, remote capability is assigned at the level of the occupation: an occupation is classified as teleworkable if its task content allows it to be performed entirely at home, and a worker is classified as remote-capable if her occupation is teleworkable. The classification is held fixed over the whole period, so that measured flows cannot change because occupations are reclassified.

An occupational classification has an obvious limitation. It records whether a worker could work remotely, not whether she does. A teleworkable accountant who moves to a new market to take an in-person job at a local firm is counted as a remote-capable migrant even though her income is earned locally. The consequence is that the measured inflow overstates the inflow of income that is genuinely earned elsewhere, which biases the multiplier of Chapter 5 toward zero rather than away from it. I regard this as the right direction of error for a study whose main claim is that the multiplier is positive. An alternative occupation-level measure of work-from-home feasibility, built from occupational task data and validated against time-use information {{mongey2021}}, could serve as a robustness check; Appendix A discusses its use. Chapter 7 returns to the measurement of remote status as a limitation of the design.

### 4.2.2 Net in-migration

For each market and quarter, the panel records gross inflows and outflows of employed working-age residents, separately for remote-capable and other workers, and the treatment is based on the net in-migration of remote-capable workers. Three choices in its construction need justification.

The first is the use of net rather than gross flows. A market that receives many remote-capable arrivals but loses as many remote-capable residents experiences no change in the income base on which the model of Chapter 3 operates, and should not be counted as treated. Net flows measure the change in the stock that matters.

The second is seasonal adjustment. Migration is seasonal, with more moves in summer quarters, and a market's raw net inflow can cross a threshold for seasonal reasons alone. Each market's net flows are therefore adjusted using its own 2017–2019 seasonal pattern before the treatment is coded, as described in Appendix A.

The third is the scaling. Net inflows are divided by the market's base employment, defined as its mean total employment over the four quarters of 2019. A fixed pre-2020 denominator ensures that the treatment variable cannot respond mechanically to the outcomes. If the denominator were contemporaneous employment, a market whose service employment grew for reasons unrelated to migration would see its scaled inflow fall, and treatment status would become partly a function of the outcome.

### 4.2.3 The treatment definition

A market is treated from the first quarter in which its scaled net in-migration of remote-capable workers exceeds 0.5 percent of base employment for two consecutive quarters, and it remains treated in every subsequent quarter. The formal definition of the treatment date $G_c$ is given in Equation (A.3) of Appendix A. The treatment indicator is $D_{ct} = \mathbf{1}\{t \geq G_c\}$, with $G_c = \infty$ for markets that never meet the condition by 2024Q4.

Each element of the definition is a choice, and each was made before estimation. The threshold of 0.5 percent of base employment is large enough that a market crossing it receives an inflow that is economically meaningful relative to its size, but small enough that a substantial number of markets cross it. The requirement of two consecutive quarters guards against coding a market as treated on the basis of a single unusual quarter, such as the arrival of a cohort of new residents attached to a single employer. Because the condition is checked from 2020Q1 onward, the twelve quarters of 2017–2019 are untreated for every market by construction; applying the same rule to the pre-2020 period as a check, no market meets it, which confirms that the threshold is not crossed by ordinary migration.

Treatment is absorbing: once a market is treated, it remains treated even if later inflows fall below the threshold. There are two reasons for this convention. Substantively, the model treats remote arrivals as an increase in a stock, and a stock does not revert when the flow that built it slows. Methodologically, the heterogeneity-robust estimators used here are defined for staggered adoption of a treatment that is not reversed {{callaway2021;sun2021}}. The cost of the convention is that the treatment indicator is a coarse summary of a continuous process. The estimand is the average effect of having entered a regime of sustained remote in-migration, not the effect of a unit of inflow, and the multiplier analysis of Chapter 5 is needed to translate one into the other.

A binary threshold is a blunt instrument, and the estimates should not depend heavily on exactly where it is drawn. Appendix B reports results for alternative thresholds and persistence requirements in Table B.1. The model of Chapter 3 predicts that effects should rise with the threshold, since a higher threshold selects markets with larger inflows, and a robust result is one in which they rise smoothly rather than jumping or changing sign.

Several features of the cohort structure shape the empirical strategy. The first is that treatment is genuinely staggered. A total of 306 markets are treated, and their onset dates span six quarters. The largest cohort, first treated in 2021Q1, contains 97 markets; the smallest, first treated in 2022Q1, contains 52. Onset is concentrated in the period when the shift to remote work was new, consistent with the timing of the national change documented in the literature {{brynjolfsson2020;barrero2021stick}}, and the declining size of the later cohorts reflects the fact that the markets most attractive to remote workers were reached first.

The second feature is that the never-treated group is large. With 294 never-treated markets, almost half of the panel, there is a stable comparison group available at every date. This matters because the pool of not-yet-treated markets shrinks over time and disappears entirely after 2022Q1, as the last column of Table 4.1 shows. For the 2022Q1 cohort, and for all cohorts in quarters after 2022Q1, the only clean comparisons are with never-treated markets. A design that relied on not-yet-treated markets alone would be unable to estimate effects at long horizons, which is one reason the baseline uses both groups.

The third feature is that every cohort is observed at every event time from $e = -8$ to $e = 8$. The 2020Q3 cohort is observed from 2018Q3, eight quarters before onset, to 2022Q3, eight quarters after; the 2022Q1 cohort from 2020Q1 to 2024Q1. Within this window, the event-study estimates in Chapters 5 and 6 are averages over the same four cohorts at each horizon, so that changes in the estimated profile across event times cannot arise from changes in the composition of the contributing cohorts. Beyond this window, the composition changes: only the 2020Q3 and 2021Q1 cohorts are observed more than thirteen quarters after onset. The event-study figures are therefore restricted to $e = -8, \dots, 8$, while the overall summaries in Equation (4.4) use all post-treatment quarters.

The fourth feature is that the treatment is concentrated in time, with all onsets between 2020Q3 and 2022Q1, and coincides with large national changes in work arrangements, labor markets and housing markets. Quarter fixed effects, or their equivalents in the heterogeneity-robust estimators, absorb the national component of those changes. What they cannot absorb are changes that affected treated and untreated markets differently for reasons other than remote in-migration, and Section 4.7 discusses the most plausible ones.

## 4.4 Outcomes and Covariates

### 4.4.1 Outcomes

The empirical chapters study five outcomes, all measured quarterly at the market level and entered in natural logarithms, so that estimated effects can be read as approximate percentage changes. Their definitions are summarized in Table A.1 of Appendix A.

The primary outcome is log non-tradable service employment, the empirical counterpart of $N^{NT}_c$ in Chapter 3. The classification of industries follows the logic of the local-multiplier literature, in which non-tradable industries are those whose output must be consumed near where it is produced {{moretti2010}}. The non-tradable aggregate includes retail trade, food service, personal services and the construction trades. Construction is included because residential repair and renovation are delivered on site and respond directly to the arrival of new households, and Chapter 5 shows that the results do not depend on its inclusion.

The second outcome is the log mean weekly wage in non-tradable industries, computed as the quarterly non-tradable wage bill divided by non-tradable employment and by thirteen weeks. It is the counterpart of $w^{NT}_c$. Because it is a mean over employed workers, it moves both with the pay of a given job and with the composition of jobs, a distinction Chapter 6 takes up.

The third outcome is the log median asking rent for listed residential units, the counterpart of $r_c$. Asking rents are the price faced by a household searching for housing, which is the relevant price for movers, including arriving remote workers and workers relocating to take new service jobs. They respond faster to demand than rents on existing leases, which makes them the natural measure for a study of short-run responses but means they overstate how quickly the rents of sitting tenants adjust.

The fourth outcome is a log house price index constructed from repeated transactions of the same properties, normalized to the market's 2019 average. Its role is to test the capitalization prediction of Chapter 3, that house prices should rise at least as much as rents if further arrivals are expected.

The fifth outcome is log tradable employment, covering industries whose output is sold mainly outside the market. Under assumption (A3) of the model, tradable employment should not respond to first order, and under the crowding-out extension it should fall slightly. It therefore serves as a near-placebo. A large positive response would suggest either spillovers from remote workers to local tradable productivity or, more worryingly, a common demand shock that raised activity in all sectors of treated markets. It is a near-placebo rather than a pure one because the model allows a small negative response.

### 4.4.2 Covariates and heterogeneity variables

The conditional version of the parallel-trends assumption, discussed in Section 4.7, uses a small set of market characteristics measured before 2020: log base employment, the teleworkable share of base employment, the industry composition of employment summarized by the tradable share, and a synthetic amenity index. These are the variables most likely to predict both which markets attracted remote workers and how their outcomes would have evolved in the absence of arrivals. All are measured in 2019 or earlier, so that none can be affected by treatment.

Two characteristics are used to examine heterogeneity. The housing-supply elasticity index is the empirical counterpart of $\eta_c$ in Chapter 3. It combines a land-availability component and a regulatory-stringency component into a single standardized score, with higher values indicating more elastic supply, and markets are split at its median over all 600 markets. The amenity index summarizes the natural and consumption amenities of a market into a standardized score, and markets are grouped by its terciles over all 600 markets. Both indices are fixed at their pre-2020 values.

Table 4.2 supports four observations. First, treated markets are larger than never-treated markets. The mean treated market had 142,000 jobs in 2019 and the median 61,000; the corresponding figures for never-treated markets are 58,000 and 24,000. The gap between mean and median in both groups reflects the right skew one would expect in any set of labor markets that includes a few large metropolitan areas alongside many small and rural ones. Because the estimators used here weight markets equally, the estimates describe the typical treated market rather than the typical treated worker.

Second, the earlier cohorts are larger, more amenity-rich, more specialized in teleworkable occupations and more expensive than the later ones. The ordering is what the model's account of remote location choice would lead one to expect: the markets that ranked highest in the utility of remote workers were reached first. It also matches the pattern in the heterogeneity analysis of Chapter 6, where treated markets are over-represented in the top tercile of the amenity index. The ordering by amenities is not, by itself, a threat to identification, which rests on trends rather than levels, but it means that cohort differences in estimated effects cannot be read as the effect of timing alone.

Third, pre-treatment growth of non-tradable employment was very similar across cohorts and between treated and never-treated markets, between 1.2 and 1.4 percent per year in every column. Similar average growth before 2020 is not a test of parallel trends, which concerns the counterfactual path after treatment, but it would be hard to defend the design if treated markets had been growing much faster beforehand. The event-study estimates of Chapters 5 and 6 provide the formal check.

Fourth, the housing-supply elasticity index is balanced between treated and never-treated markets and varies little across cohorts. Remote workers did not, on average, sort into markets with systematically more or less elastic housing supply. This is important for the heterogeneity analysis: the split by elasticity compares markets that received similar inflows, so differences in effects across the split can be attributed to differences in housing supply rather than to differences in the size of the shock. Among treated markets, the median split yields 153 markets with inelastic supply and 153 with elastic supply.

The last row of Table 4.2 reports the cumulative inflow among treated markets eight quarters after onset. On average it reached 1.9 percent of base employment, declining from 2.3 percent for the 2020Q3 cohort to 1.3 percent for the 2022Q1 cohort. These are the inflows that scale the employment effect into the multiplier of Chapter 5. The cohort pattern implies that the earliest cohorts should show the largest reduced-form effects even if the response per unit of inflow is the same in every cohort, a point that Equation (3.21) of Chapter 3 makes formally. For never-treated markets the row is undefined, because they have no onset date; their cumulative net inflow from 2020Q1 to 2024Q4 averaged 0.4 percent of base employment.

## 4.6 Estimating Equations

### 4.6.1 Potential outcomes and the parameter of interest

Let $y_{ct}(g)$ denote the potential outcome of market $c$ in quarter $t$ if it were first treated in quarter $g$, and $y_{ct}(\infty)$ its potential outcome if never treated. The observed outcome is $y_{ct} = y_{ct}(G_c)$. The parameter of interest is the group-time average treatment effect, the average effect in quarter $t$ for markets first treated in quarter $g$,

$$
ATT(g,t) = \mathbb{E}\left[ y_{ct}(g) - y_{ct}(\infty) \mid G_c = g \right]
$$

which is defined for each of the four cohorts and each quarter of the panel {{callaway2021}}. Everything else reported in Chapters 5 and 6 is either an estimate of these parameters, an average of them, or an estimator whose relationship to them must be established. Writing the parameter in this form makes it explicit that the treatment effect is allowed to differ across cohorts and over time, as the model predicts it will: Equation (3.21) shows that the effect at a given event time depends on the market's inflow, which differs across cohorts, and on time since onset, because the inflow accumulates.

### 4.6.2 The two-way fixed-effects benchmark

The conventional estimator of the effect of a staggered treatment is the static two-way fixed-effects regression,

$$
y_{ct} = \alpha_c + \lambda_t + \beta\, D_{ct} + \varepsilon_{ct} \tag{?}
$$

where $\alpha_c$ is a market fixed effect, $\lambda_t$ a quarter fixed effect and $D_{ct}$ the treatment indicator. Under parallel trends and a homogeneous treatment effect, $\beta$ in Equation (4.1) is the average treatment effect on the treated. When effects are heterogeneous, $\beta$ is a weighted average of all possible two-group, two-period difference-in-differences comparisons in the data, including comparisons in which markets treated earlier serve as controls for markets treated later {{goodmanbacon2021}}. If effects grow with time since treatment, those comparisons subtract the growing effect of the earlier-treated markets from the effect of the later-treated ones and bias $\beta$ downward. Equivalently, $\beta$ can be written as a weighted sum of group-time effects in which some weights may be negative, so that the coefficient need not even have the sign of any underlying effect {{dechaisemartin2020}}.

The conditions under which these problems are severe are present here. Treatment is staggered over six quarters, the never-treated group is large but not dominant, and Chapter 3 predicts effects that grow with event time. I therefore report Equation (4.1) as a benchmark only. Chapter 5 uses the decomposition of {{goodmanbacon2021|n}} to show how much of its identifying variation comes from comparisons with already-treated markets and the diagnostic of {{dechaisemartin2020|n}} to report the share of negative weights. Because the weights depend only on treatment timing and the fixed effects, not on the outcome, the same weights apply to every outcome studied.

### 4.6.3 The event-study specification

The dynamic analogue of Equation (4.1) replaces the single treatment indicator with indicators for each quarter relative to onset,

$$
y_{ct} = \alpha_c + \lambda_t + \sum_{\substack{e=-8 \\ e \neq -1}}^{8} \gamma_e\, \mathbf{1}\{t - G_c = e\} + \gamma_{-}\, \mathbf{1}\{t - G_c < -8\} + \gamma_{+}\, \mathbf{1}\{t - G_c > 8\} + \varepsilon_{ct} \tag{?}
$$

where the indicators are zero for never-treated markets, the quarter before onset, $e = -1$, is the reference period, and the endpoints are binned. The coefficients $\gamma_e$ for $e \geq 0$ trace the dynamic effect, and those for $e \leq -2$ are the pre-treatment coefficients used to assess parallel trends.

Equation (4.2) defines the event-time parameters of interest, but estimating it by ordinary least squares inherits the problems of the static regression. When effects differ across cohorts, the coefficient on a given relative-time indicator is contaminated by effects at other horizons, so that apparent pre-trends can arise purely from heterogeneity and genuine ones can be masked {{sun2021}}. In the empirical chapters the event-time coefficients in Equation (4.2) are therefore estimated in two heterogeneity-robust ways: as aggregations of group-time effects by event time, using Equations (4.3) and (4.4), and with the interaction-weighted estimator of {{sun2021|n}}, which estimates cohort-specific relative-time effects using never-treated markets as controls and averages them with weights equal to cohort shares. The OLS version of Equation (4.2) is used only for comparison.

### 4.6.4 Group-time average treatment effects

The preferred estimator is that of {{callaway2021|n}}. Under the assumptions discussed in Section 4.7, the group-time effect for cohort $g$ in a post-treatment quarter $t \geq g$ is identified by comparing the change in outcomes for cohort $g$ between the last pre-treatment quarter, $g-1$, and quarter $t$ with the corresponding change for markets not yet treated by $t$:

$$
ATT(g,t) = \mathbb{E}\left[ y_{ct} - y_{c,g-1} \mid G_c = g \right] - \mathbb{E}\left[ y_{ct} - y_{c,g-1} \mid G_c > t \right] \tag{?}
$$

The comparison group in the second term, markets with $G_c > t$, includes both the never-treated markets, for which $G_c = \infty$, and the markets in later cohorts that have not yet been treated in quarter $t$. This choice uses more of the data than a never-treated-only comparison and is equally valid under no anticipation, and it avoids the comparisons with already-treated markets that contaminate Equation (4.1). For $t \geq 2022\text{Q1}$, when no market is not yet treated, the comparison group consists of never-treated markets only. Appendix B reports estimates using never-treated controls only and not-yet-treated controls only.

Each $ATT(g,t)$ in Equation (4.3) is a simple two-group, two-period difference-in-differences, and it is estimated by its sample analogue. When baseline covariates are used, the comparison is implemented with the doubly robust estimator proposed by {{callaway2021|n}}, which combines an outcome regression for the change in untreated outcomes with a propensity-score reweighting of the comparison group and is consistent if either model is correctly specified. For pre-treatment quarters, $t < g$, the group-time parameters are computed with a varying base period, comparing quarter $t$ with quarter $t-1$, so that each pre-treatment coefficient is a test of parallel trends between adjacent quarters rather than a cumulative difference.

### 4.6.5 Aggregation

The four cohorts and 32 quarters yield many group-time effects, each estimated from a modest number of markets. They are summarized by three aggregations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta_{ES}(e) &= \sum_{g} \mathbf{1}\{g + e \leq \mathcal{T}\}\, \Pr\left(G = g \mid G + e \leq \mathcal{T}\right) ATT(g, g+e), \\
\theta_{C}(g) &= \frac{1}{\mathcal{T} - g + 1} \sum_{t = g}^{\mathcal{T}} ATT(g,t), \\
\theta^{O} &= \sum_{g} \sum_{t = g}^{\mathcal{T}} \omega_{g}\, ATT(g,t), \qquad \omega_g = \frac{\Pr(G = g)}{\sum_{g'} (\mathcal{T} - g' + 1)\Pr(G = g')}
\end{aligned} \tag{?}
$$

where $\mathcal{T} = 2024\text{Q4}$ is the last quarter of the panel and the sums over $g$ run over the four treated cohorts. The first line of Equation (4.4) is the event-study aggregation: the average effect $e$ quarters after onset, across the cohorts observed at that horizon, weighted by cohort size. For $-8 \leq e \leq 8$ all four cohorts contribute, so the weights are the same at every horizon. The second line is the cohort aggregation: the average effect for cohort $g$ over all its post-treatment quarters, which Chapter 5 reports by cohort. The third line is the overall effect: an average of all post-treatment group-time effects, in which each cell receives a weight proportional to the size of its cohort. Since cohorts treated earlier contribute more post-treatment quarters, the overall effect gives them more weight, and it equals the average of the cohort effects $\theta_C(g)$ weighted by cohort size times number of post-treatment quarters.

These aggregations answer different questions, and the choice among them should follow the question asked {{callaway2021;roth2023}}. The overall effect is the natural headline summary of the average effect of having been treated over the period. The event-study aggregation is the right object for studying dynamics and for the multiplier, which compares the employment response with the inflow at a common horizon. The cohort aggregation is the right object for asking whether early and late arrivals had different effects, with the caveat from Section 4.5 that cohorts also differ in their inflows.

### 4.6.6 The imputation estimator and the multiplier

As a further check, Chapters 5 and 6 report the imputation estimator of {{borusyak2024|n}}. It estimates the market and quarter effects of Equation (4.1) using untreated observations only, that is, all observations of never-treated markets and the pre-treatment observations of treated markets, imputes the untreated potential outcome $\hat{y}_{ct}(\infty) = \hat{\alpha}_c + \hat{\lambda}_t$ for every treated observation, and averages the differences $y_{ct} - \hat{y}_{ct}(\infty)$ with the desired weights. Because it uses all pre-treatment periods rather than a single base period, it is efficient under homoskedastic, serially uncorrelated errors, and it remains valid when effects are heterogeneous. Its identifying assumption is slightly stronger than that of Equation (4.3), since it requires parallel trends in all pre-treatment periods, and agreement between the two estimators is therefore informative.

The local multiplier is estimated as a ratio of treatment effects, following Equation (3.22) of Chapter 3. The numerator is the event-study effect on non-tradable employment at a given horizon, converted into jobs using baseline non-tradable employment; the denominator is the effect on the stock of remote-capable workers at the same horizon. Its interpretation as a multiplier rests on an exclusion restriction discussed in Section 4.7.4, and its standard error is obtained by the bootstrap described in Section 4.8.

## 4.7 Identification

### 4.7.1 Assumptions

The group-time effects in Equation (4.3) are identified under three assumptions {{callaway2021;roth2023}}. The first is irreversibility of treatment, which holds by the construction described in Section 4.2.3. The second is no anticipation: before onset, outcomes do not respond to treatment that has not yet occurred, so that $y_{ct}(g) = y_{ct}(\infty)$ for $t < g$. The third is parallel trends: in the absence of treatment, the average outcome of each cohort would have evolved in the same way as that of the comparison markets, from the quarter before onset to every later quarter. The conditional version requires this only among markets with the same baseline covariates, and it is accompanied by an overlap condition requiring that markets with every combination of covariates have a positive probability of remaining untreated.

None of these assumptions can be verified directly, but each has observable implications, and each faces specific threats in this setting. The rest of this section discusses the most important threats and how the design addresses them.

### 4.7.2 Selection on trends

The main threat is that remote workers chose markets whose outcomes were going to grow anyway. The model of Chapter 3 is explicit that destinations are not random: remote workers choose markets with high amenities and, other things equal, low rents. Selection on levels of these characteristics does not violate parallel trends, because the comparison is of changes. Selection on trends does. If amenity-rich markets were already on faster growth paths for service employment, or if their amenity value rose in the pandemic for reasons unrelated to remote arrivals, for instance because outdoor recreation became more valued, the estimates would attribute that growth to treatment.

Three features of the design address this threat. The first is the event study. If treated markets had been diverging from controls before onset, the pre-treatment coefficients of Equation (4.2) would show it, and Chapters 5 and 6 report these coefficients for every outcome along with a joint test that they are zero. The second is conditioning on baseline characteristics. The doubly robust version of Equation (4.3) compares treated markets with untreated markets that had similar size, teleworkable share, industry composition and amenities before 2020, which removes differential trends that are a function of those characteristics. Appendix B reports these conditional estimates. The third is sensitivity analysis. Pre-trend tests have limited power, and conditioning on a pre-trend test can itself distort inference {{roth2023}}. Following {{rambachan2023|n}}, I therefore report how large a violation of parallel trends after onset, relative to the largest violation observed before onset, would be needed to overturn the conclusions. This replaces the binary question of whether trends were parallel with the more useful question of how non-parallel they would have to be.

### 4.7.3 Anticipation, spillovers and concurrent shocks

No anticipation is plausible for employment, since service firms are unlikely to hire before the customers arrive. It is less plausible for rents and house prices, which are set in forward-looking markets. If landlords or buyers anticipated arrivals, rents could rise in the quarters before onset. The treatment definition cuts both ways here. Onset is dated to the first of two quarters in which the inflow already exceeds the threshold, so arrivals at lower rates often begin before $G_c$, and a rent response to those early arrivals would look like anticipation even though it is not. Anticipation would show up as positive coefficients at $e = -2$ or $e = -3$ in the rent and house-price event studies, and Chapter 6 examines them. If anticipation were present, the remedy would be to move the reference period earlier.

The comparison also assumes that treatment of one market does not affect outcomes in others, a version of the stable unit treatment value assumption. Commuting zones are constructed so that most commuting takes place within them {{tolbert1996}}, which limits the most direct channel of spillover. But spillovers can arise in other ways. A remote worker who moves into a treated market has left another market, and if that origin market is a control, its service employment may fall, which would exaggerate the treatment-control difference. Within metropolitan areas, the pandemic moved residents and real-estate demand from dense centers toward suburbs {{ramani2021;gupta2022}}, and the loss of spending by remote workers in big-city centers hurt the local service workers who depended on it {{althoff2022}}. To the extent that origin markets are among the never-treated controls, the estimates combine a gain in destinations with a loss in origins. The design cannot rule this out, but its likely magnitude is limited by the fact that net inflows into treated markets are small relative to the size of the large origin markets from which many movers came, and Chapter 7 discusses spillovers as a limitation.

The concentration of onsets in 2020–2022 means that treatment coincided with large national shocks, including the pandemic itself, changes in interest rates and the national swings in housing demand that accompanied them. Quarter effects absorb the national component of these shocks. They do not absorb shocks whose local incidence was correlated with treatment. The most plausible of these is that the pandemic itself hit large, dense markets differently from small ones, through the composition of their service sectors and through the loss of spending by workers who stopped commuting to city centers {{althoff2022}}. Because treated markets are larger than never-treated ones on average, such a shock could be correlated with treatment. Conditioning on base employment addresses this directly, and the cohort estimates provide a further check, since the cohorts were treated at different points of the pandemic and its aftermath.

### 4.7.4 The exclusion restriction for the multiplier

The multiplier requires more than parallel trends. Interpreting the ratio of the employment effect to the inflow as the number of jobs created per remote worker requires that treatment affect non-tradable employment only through the inflow of remote workers and their spending. The restriction would fail if remote arrivals coincided with other changes that raised service employment, such as an influx of retirees or tourists attracted by the same amenities, or if remote-capable migrants included many workers who took in-person local jobs, as discussed in Section 4.2.1. The first failure would bias the multiplier upward, the second downward. Chapter 5 discusses both, and the near-placebo outcome of tradable employment provides a partial check on the first: a common demand shock that raised activity across sectors would be expected to show up in tradable employment as well.

## 4.8 Inference

Standard errors are clustered by market throughout. Two arguments support this choice. The first concerns serial correlation. Outcomes such as employment and rents are highly persistent within markets, and difference-in-differences estimates that ignore serial correlation can badly overstate precision {{bertrand2004}}. Clustering by market allows an arbitrary correlation of errors over time within each market. The second argument concerns design. Clustering is appropriate when treatment is assigned at the level of a cluster, whether or not residuals are correlated within it, and the appropriate level of clustering is the level at which treatment is assigned {{abadie2023}}. Treatment here is assigned by market, so the market is the natural cluster. Clustering at a coarser level, such as groups of neighboring markets, would be appropriate if treatment were spatially correlated; Table B.3 in Appendix B reports alternative approaches to inference.

With 600 clusters, of which 306 are treated, asymptotic cluster-robust inference is reliable for the pooled estimates {{cameron2015}}. The cohort-specific estimates are a different matter. The smallest cohort contains 52 markets, and inference about a single cohort's effect relies on relatively few treated clusters. For those estimates I report wild cluster bootstrap confidence intervals and p-values, which perform better when the effective number of treated clusters is modest {{cameron2015}}. For the event-study aggregation, I report simultaneous confidence bands from the multiplier bootstrap proposed by {{callaway2021|n}}, which account for the fact that seventeen event-time coefficients are examined jointly and which are wider than pointwise intervals.

The multiplier is a ratio of two estimated effects, and its sampling distribution is not well approximated by a normal distribution when the denominator is imprecisely estimated. I obtain its standard error and confidence interval by a cluster bootstrap that resamples markets, re-estimates both effects in each draw and computes the ratio. Because the inflow among treated markets is large relative to its sampling error, the denominator is precisely estimated and the bootstrap distribution of the ratio is well behaved.

Finally, the dissertation reports effects on five outcomes and several heterogeneity splits. I do not adjust for multiple testing formally, because the outcomes were chosen in advance on the basis of the model and their predicted signs are stated in Chapter 3. The predictions are directional and joint, and the relevant question is whether the pattern across outcomes matches the model, not whether any single coefficient crosses a threshold. Regression tables report standard errors and, by convention, mark significance at the 10, 5 and 1 percent levels.

## 4.9 Summary

This chapter has described the data and the empirical strategy. The panel covers 600 labor markets modeled on US commuting zones over 32 quarters, from 2017Q1 to 2024Q4, for 19,200 market-quarters. A market is treated from the first quarter in which its net in-migration of remote-capable workers, classified by occupational teleworkability {{dingel2020}}, exceeds 0.5 percent of its pre-2020 employment for two consecutive quarters. Four cohorts are treated, in 2020Q3 (84 markets), 2021Q1 (97), 2021Q3 (73) and 2022Q1 (52), and 294 markets are never treated. Treated markets are larger and more amenity-rich than never-treated ones, and earlier cohorts more so than later ones, but pre-treatment growth was similar across groups and housing-supply elasticity is balanced. By eight quarters after onset, the remote inflow among treated markets had reached 1.9 percent of base employment.

The estimation strategy reflects the staggered timing and the likely heterogeneity of effects. The two-way fixed-effects regression of Equation (4.1) and its event-study analogue in Equation (4.2) are reported as benchmarks. The preferred estimates are the group-time effects of Equation (4.3), aggregated by Equation (4.4) into event-time, cohort and overall summaries, and they are checked against the interaction-weighted and imputation estimators. Identification rests on parallel trends and no anticipation, which are assessed with event-study pre-trends, conditional estimates and sensitivity analysis, and inference uses standard errors clustered by market, wild cluster bootstrap for the cohort estimates and simultaneous bands for the event studies. Chapter 5 applies this strategy to non-tradable employment, the first and central prediction of the model.

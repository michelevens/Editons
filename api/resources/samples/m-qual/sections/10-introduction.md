# Chapter 1: Introduction

This chapter introduces the study. It begins by setting out the background to the research, the growth of fully online master's study and the place of first-generation students within it, and then states the problem the thesis addresses. It presents the aim and the three research questions, outlines the conceptual starting points and the qualitative approach taken, and introduces my own position in relation to the topic. It then explains why the study matters, defines the key terms used throughout, sets out the scope of the work, and closes with an outline of the chapters that follow.

## 1.1 Background

For most of the history of American higher education, going to graduate school meant going somewhere. A master's degree was something one did on a campus, often after leaving work, sometimes after moving city. Over the past two decades that picture has changed. Universities of every kind now offer master's programs that are delivered entirely online, with coursework organized through a learning management system, discussion conducted on message boards, and some or all teaching delivered through recorded lectures and scheduled video sessions. For working adults, and particularly for those with children, aging parents or irregular shift patterns, these programs have made graduate study possible in a way that a residential program would not.

Among the students who have taken up these opportunities are many who are the first in their families to pursue higher education. First-generation students have long been a focus of research at the undergraduate level, where they have been shown to encounter a set of overlapping disadvantages, including less familiarity with how universities work, fewer financial resources, and more competing obligations outside study {{engle2008;pascarella2005}}. Much less is known about what happens when these students, having completed a bachelor's degree, go on to master's study, and less still about their experience when that study takes place entirely at a distance. A first-generation student who has earned a bachelor's degree has, in one sense, already crossed the threshold that much of the literature is concerned with. Yet the move into graduate study brings new and often unwritten expectations, and a fully online format removes many of the informal encounters, such as the conversation after class or the chance meeting in a corridor, through which students on campus come to learn those expectations.

At the same time, the concept of belonging has moved toward the center of thinking about student success. Belonging is now widely treated not as a pleasant extra but as something that shapes whether students engage, persist and thrive {{strayhorn2019}}. The idea draws on a much broader argument in psychology that the need to form and maintain lasting, positive relationships is a fundamental human motivation {{baumeister1995}}. If belonging matters this much, then the question of how it is built, or undermined, in a program that students never physically attend becomes pressing. The design choices that online programs make, from whether cameras must be switched on in live sessions to how discussion boards are moderated, are in part choices about the conditions under which students can come to feel that they belong.

This thesis sits at the meeting point of these developments. It is concerned with first-generation students in fully online master's programs and with how they experience belonging in that setting.

## 1.2 Statement of the problem

The problem this study addresses has three parts.

First, research on sense of belonging in higher education has been developed largely in the context of residential undergraduate study. Influential models of persistence describe students becoming integrated into the academic and social systems of an institution {{tinto1993}}, and work on sense of belonging has examined how students perceive their place within a campus and its climate {{hurtado1997;strayhorn2019}}. These frameworks assume, implicitly or explicitly, a physical community that students enter and inhabit. It is not obvious how far they carry over to graduate students who log in from their kitchen tables after work and who may never meet a classmate in person.

Second, research on online learning has given considerable attention to community, presence and interaction. The Community of Inquiry framework, for example, describes how social, cognitive and teaching presence combine to support learning in text-based environments {{garrison2000}}, and research on sense of community at a distance has identified the conditions under which online learners come to feel connected to one another {{rovai2002}}. This literature, however, has tended to treat online students as a single population. It has paid less attention to how the experience of online community may differ for students who arrive without the inherited knowledge about university that continuing-generation students often bring, and whose lives outside study are especially crowded.

Third, both bodies of work have at times framed first-generation students in terms of what they lack. Research that documents the barriers these students face is important, but a focus on deficits can obscure the knowledge, relationships and motivations that students bring with them {{yosso2005}}. Graduate students who are the first in their families to reach this level have often drawn on precisely such resources to get there. Understanding how they continue to do so in online study, and whether programs recognize those resources, is part of understanding their belonging.

Taken together, these gaps mean that programs designing fully online master's study have limited evidence about how first-generation students themselves experience belonging, what in the program helps or hinders it, and what those students draw on from outside the institution. Decisions about practices such as mandatory video, synchronous attendance and the use of discussion forums are often made on general assumptions about what builds community. This study sets out to examine those assumptions from the standpoint of the students most likely to be affected by them.

## 1.3 Aim and research questions

The aim of this study is to understand how first-generation students, defined here as students neither of whose parents holds a bachelor's degree, experience belonging in fully online master's programs, and what program practices they say strengthen or weaken it.

To meet this aim, the study addresses three research questions:

1. **RQ1.** How do first-generation students describe belonging (or its absence) in a fully online master's program?
2. **RQ2.** Which program practices and interactions do they identify as strengthening or weakening that belonging?
3. **RQ3.** How do participants draw on resources from outside the university (family, work, community) to sustain their study?

The questions move from the descriptive to the practical and then outward. RQ1 asks what belonging means to participants and how they recognize its presence or absence. RQ2 asks which features of their programs they connect with those experiences, so that the findings can speak to program design. RQ3 widens the frame beyond the university, recognizing that for students studying from home, often while working and caring for others, the boundary between the program and the rest of life is thin. The questions are open by design. They are not hypotheses to be tested but lines of inquiry intended to generate a detailed, interpretive account of participants' experience.

## 1.4 Conceptual starting points

The study does not set out to test a single theory, but it begins from a set of concepts that shaped the questions I asked and the way I read the data. These are reviewed in detail in Chapter 2 and are introduced briefly here.

The first is belonging itself. I take belonging to be both a basic human need {{baumeister1995}} and a perception that is shaped by context, so that it may rise or fall from one setting to another and may matter more for students who are marginalized within an institution {{strayhorn2019;hurtado1997}}. This dual view matters for the study because it allows for the possibility that a student may feel she belongs in her nursing cohort but not in her statistics course, or that she belongs to her program while feeling out of place in the university as a whole.

The second is engagement understood socioculturally. Rather than treating engagement solely as something students do, such as logging in, posting and submitting work, I follow the argument that engagement is shaped by students' wider lives, by the structures of institutions, and by the relationships between the two {{kahu2013}}. This framing makes room for RQ3 and for the competing demands that most participants described.

The third is the idea of online presence. The Community of Inquiry framework {{garrison2000}} and work on sense of community in online courses {{rovai2002}} offer a vocabulary for thinking about how people come to be present to one another when they are not in the same room. Part of what this study examines is whether the forms of presence that programs emphasize are the ones that participants experienced as belonging.

The fourth is an asset-based view of first-generation students. Drawing on the concept of community cultural wealth {{yosso2005}}, I approached participants not as students who lacked the right preparation but as people who brought aspirational, familial, social and navigational resources into their study. Finally, the notion of learning as participation in communities of practice {{lave1991}} provides a way of thinking about how newcomers move from the edges of a community toward fuller membership, and what helps or prevents that movement.

## 1.5 Research approach in brief

The study takes a qualitative, interpretivist approach. Its interest lies in how participants make sense of their own experience, which is best explored through extended conversation rather than measurement {{creswell2018q}}. I conducted semi-structured interviews, each lasting between 60 and 75 minutes and held by video call, with 14 first-generation students enrolled in three fully online master's programs at Riverside State University, a mid-sized public university in the United States: an MBA, an MS in Nursing Leadership, and an MA in Instructional Design. Participants were recruited through program newsletters and selected purposively to give variation in age, program, enrollment status, employment and caring responsibilities.

The interviews were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis {{braun2006;braun2019}}, an approach that treats the researcher's interpretation as a resource rather than a source of bias and that understands themes as patterns of shared meaning generated through analytic work. The study does not test hypotheses and does not report inter-rater reliability, for reasons explained in Chapter 3. The design, sampling, ethics, analytic process and quality criteria are set out in full in that chapter.

## 1.6 Researcher positionality

I come to this study as a first-generation graduate who completed my own master's degree part-time and online while working. I remember, very clearly, the experience of reading a syllabus that asked students to "reach out" with any questions and not knowing whether that was an invitation or a formality. I also remember the group chat that a few classmates set up in my second semester, which did more to keep me enrolled than anything the program offered formally.

This history drew me to the topic and gave me a degree of insider understanding that I believe helped participants speak openly. It also carried risks. I was alert from the outset to the possibility that I would hear my own story in other people's accounts, or would look for the experiences I had had and overlook those I had not. Reflexive thematic analysis does not ask the researcher to set aside her perspective, but it does ask her to account for it {{braun2019}}. I kept a reflexive journal throughout the study and discuss in Chapter 3 how I engaged with my position during data collection and analysis, drawing on the account of reflexivity offered by {{finlay2002|n}}.

## 1.7 Significance of the study

The study is intended to contribute in three ways.

First, it adds to knowledge about a group of students who are seldom the focus of research: first-generation students at the master's level, studying fully online. The literature on first-generation students has concentrated on undergraduates, and the literature on online belonging has rarely disaggregated its participants by generational status. By centering this group's accounts, the study offers an initial, detailed picture that later research, including larger studies, could build on.

Second, it speaks to practice. Online programs make many concrete decisions that bear directly on students' experience of community, among them camera policies, the balance of synchronous and asynchronous activity, the design of discussion forums, the information given at orientation, and the way instructors communicate expectations. By asking participants which practices they experienced as strengthening or weakening their belonging (RQ2), the study aims to give program leaders and instructors grounded, specific considerations to weigh. It does not claim that these findings apply across all programs, but it offers the kind of detailed description that allows readers to judge their relevance to their own setting.

Third, the study contributes to the ongoing effort to move discussion of first-generation students away from deficit framings. By asking explicitly about the resources participants bring from family, work and community (RQ3), it treats those resources as part of the story of belonging rather than as background. If programs understand what sustains these students, they may be better placed to recognize and build on it.

## 1.8 Definitions of key terms

Several terms are used throughout the thesis with specific meanings.

**First-generation student.** Definitions vary across studies, and the choice of definition affects who is counted. In this study, a first-generation student is one neither of whose parents or primary guardians holds a bachelor's degree. Participants were asked to self-identify against this definition at recruitment. Because all participants hold a bachelor's degree themselves, they are first-generation in the sense that they were the first in their immediate family to complete undergraduate study and are now the first to pursue graduate study.

**Fully online master's program.** A graduate program leading to a master's degree in which all required coursework is completed online, with no required on-campus residency. The programs in this study combined asynchronous activity, such as recorded lectures, readings and discussion boards, with some scheduled synchronous video sessions, in proportions that varied by program and by course.

**Sense of belonging.** A student's perception of being accepted, valued, connected and cared about within a group or setting, here a program, a course or a cohort {{strayhorn2019}}. The study treats belonging as experienced and described by participants rather than as a fixed quantity to be measured.

**Hidden curriculum.** The unstated expectations, norms and conventions that students are assumed to know or to pick up, as distinct from what is formally taught. In this study the term refers particularly to expectations about academic writing conventions, communication with faculty, and help-seeking that were not made explicit in program materials. The concept is used in the sense developed in Chapter 2 and in participants' own descriptions.

**Synchronous and asynchronous.** Synchronous activities take place in real time with participants present together, typically through scheduled video sessions. Asynchronous activities, such as discussion forums and recorded lectures, are completed at times of the student's choosing within a deadline.

**Program practices.** The formal and informal ways a program and its instructors organize teaching and communication, including policies (such as camera requirements), structures (such as cohort models and orientation), and individual instructor behaviors (such as how feedback is given or how expectations are explained).

## 1.9 Scope and delimitations

The study is deliberately bounded. It focuses on one university and on three of its fully online master's programs, chosen because they enroll substantial numbers of working adults and represent different disciplinary cultures: business, nursing and education. It includes only students who self-identified as first-generation under the definition above and who were enrolled at the time of interview. It does not include students who had withdrawn, whose experiences of belonging may differ in important ways, nor does it include a comparison group of continuing-generation students. The purpose is not to establish whether first-generation students experience less belonging than others, a question that would require a different design, but to understand how these students describe and make sense of their own experience.

The study relies on participants' accounts. It did not observe classes, analyze course materials systematically, or interview faculty. The practices discussed in later chapters are therefore practices as participants experienced and reported them. This is consistent with the study's interpretivist aims, but it means that claims about what programs actually do are made through the lens of students' perceptions. These and other limitations are discussed further in Chapter 6.

With 14 participants, the study does not seek statistical generalization. Its claims are made about this group of students, and its value lies in the depth of its account and in the possibility that readers will recognize, or usefully fail to recognize, its findings in their own contexts.

## 1.10 Structure of the thesis

The thesis is organized into six chapters, followed by appendices.

**Chapter 2** reviews the literature that informs the study. It examines belonging as a psychological need and as a context-dependent experience in higher education; integration models of student persistence and their critiques; research on presence and community in online learning; research on first-generation students; the contrast between deficit and asset-based framings; and situated perspectives on learning. It closes by drawing these strands together into the conceptual framework that guided the study and by identifying the gap the study addresses.

**Chapter 3** sets out the methodology. It explains the interpretivist position and the choice of reflexive thematic analysis, describes the research site, sampling and recruitment, introduces the 14 participants, details the interview procedures and the six phases of analysis, and discusses ethics, quality and my own reflexive engagement with the research.

**Chapter 4** presents the findings, organized around four themes and their 11 subthemes, illustrated with participants' own words.

**Chapter 5** discusses the findings in relation to the research questions and the literature reviewed in Chapter 2, considering where the study extends, complicates or departs from existing work.

**Chapter 6** concludes the thesis. It summarizes the answers to the research questions, offers recommendations for online programs, sets out the study's limitations and directions for future research, and closes with a brief reflection.

The appendices contain the interview guide (Appendix A), the participant information sheet and consent form (Appendix B), and an excerpt from the codebook used during analysis (Appendix C).

The next chapter turns to the literature, beginning with the question of what belonging is and why it has come to occupy so central a place in thinking about students' experience of higher education.

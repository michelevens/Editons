# Chapter 6: Conclusion and Recommendations

## 6.1 Summary of the study

This thesis set out to understand how first-generation students experience belonging in fully online master's programmes, and which programme practices they say strengthen or weaken it. Drawing on semi-structured interviews with 14 students enrolled in three fully online programmes at Riverside State University (an MBA, an MS in Nursing Leadership and an MA in Instructional Design), and analyzed through reflexive thematic analysis {{braun2006;braun2019}}, the study generated four themes. Participants described belonging as being recognized rather than being seen; they described a hidden curriculum that went largely unstated online; they built their own peer spaces outside the official platform; and they drew on family and work as both resources and pressures. This chapter summarizes the answers to the research questions, states the study's contribution, offers recommendations for programmes, sets out the limitations, proposes directions for future research and closes with a short reflection.

## 6.2 Answers to the research questions

In response to RQ1, participants described belonging as a matter of recognition: being known by name, being answered, and having their circumstances acknowledged. Synchronous visibility was not, in this sample, a reliable route to belonging, and for some it was an obstacle.

In response to RQ2, participants identified the unstated expectations of graduate study as the main practice weakening belonging, compounded by the perceived risk of asking for help. Compulsory camera policies weakened belonging most for participants working night shifts or caring for others. The practice most clearly strengthening belonging was the instructor who translated tacit expectations into explicit guidance, together with discussion boards that received a genuine response.

In response to RQ3, participants sustained their study through student-built peer networks, through family members who served as both audience and reason, and, where they were supportive, through employers. Peer networks were powerful but could exclude those who joined late or were absent when they formed.

## 6.3 Contribution

The study makes three modest contributions. First, it offers an account of belonging in fully online graduate study from the perspective of first-generation students, a group more often studied at undergraduate entry {{engle2008}}. It suggests that first-generation status may resurface as a challenge at the graduate threshold. Second, it proposes the idea of recognition-centered belonging, which shifts emphasis from social presence as visibility toward teaching presence as attentive response {{garrison2000}}, and which treats the need to belong as a need for ongoing concern rather than contact {{baumeister1995}}. Third, it shows the value of reading online students' out-of-institution lives through an asset-based lens {{yosso2005}} alongside a sociocultural account of engagement {{kahu2013}}, so that family, work and peer resources are visible to programme design rather than treated as private matters.

These contributions also bear on a longer debate about student persistence. Integration models have tended to ask how far students become part of the institution {{tinto1993}}. The accounts gathered here suggest that, for working and caring adults studying online, the more useful question may be how far the institution reaches toward the student. That reversal does not discard the insight that connection matters for persistence {{pascarella2005}}; it relocates responsibility for building that connection. It also connects the study to Rovai's earlier argument that sense of community at a distance has to be designed for rather than assumed {{rovai2002}}, and to the observation that belonging is a distinct experience that institutional climate can enlarge or diminish {{hurtado1997}}. The proposition of recognition-centered belonging is offered as a way of holding these strands together for the specific case of first-generation students in fully online graduate programmes.

## 6.4 Recommendations for programmes

The following recommendations follow from the findings. They are offered to programme directors and instructors in fully online master's programmes, with the caveat that they derive from one institution and should be adapted to local conditions.

1. **Make recognition routine.** Programmes could set modest expectations for instructor response, such as named replies to discussion posts within a stated period and brief individual feedback early in each course. These are low-cost acts that participants associated strongly with belonging.
2. **Make the hidden curriculum explicit.** Each programme could provide a short orientation module, revisited in the first weeks of each course, explaining citation expectations, the purpose of office hours, and what "reach out" means in practice, including examples of appropriate questions. This would turn the work of the translating instructor from an individual gift into a programme norm, consistent with legitimate peripheral participation {{lave1991}}.
3. **Make cameras optional by default.** Synchronous sessions could treat cameras as welcome but not required, with alternative means of participation such as chat, audio or a short written reflection. Assessment should not depend on camera use.
4. **Support peer networks inclusively.** Rather than leaving peer groups to form informally, programmes could create course-level spaces and invite every enrolled student, including those who join late. Instructors could assign study partners in the first week as an option rather than a requirement.
5. **Acknowledge the whole student.** Programmes could offer flexible deadlines for students with documented shift work or caring responsibilities, and provide materials that help students explain the degree to family and employers, such as a short letter describing time commitments.

## 6.5 Limitations

Several limitations should be borne in mind. The sample of 14 participants was drawn from three programmes at a single public university. Information power was judged sufficient for the aim {{malterud2016}}, but the findings are not intended to generalize statistically, and transferability depends on readers' judgment of how closely their contexts resemble the one described {{lincoln1985}}. Recruitment through programme newsletters with a $25 gift card may have favored students who were already engaged enough to read programme communications; the least connected students, whose experience of belonging may have been worst, are likely underrepresented. Students who had already withdrawn were not included at all.

The study relied on a single interview with each participant. Belonging was described as shifting over time, and a single conversation captures only one moment of a changing experience. Interviews were conducted by video call, which was appropriate for an online population but may have reproduced, in mild form, some of the dynamics participants described in their courses. Race, gender and disability were not analyzed systematically, so the study cannot say how first-generation status intersected with these positions. Finally, as a first-generation graduate who studied online, I brought an insider perspective that sharpened some readings and may have muted others, as discussed in Chapter 5. This is an inherent feature of interpretivist qualitative work rather than a flaw to be eliminated {{creswell2018q}}, but it shapes the account offered here.

## 6.6 Future research

Four directions seem most promising. First, longitudinal qualitative work following first-generation students from enrolment to completion or withdrawal would show how belonging changes over a programme and what happens at points of crisis. Second, studies that include students who have withdrawn would address the most important gap in the present sample. Third, research that analyzes how first-generation status intersects with race, gender, disability and age would test whether recognition-centered belonging holds across these positions, drawing on frameworks such as community cultural wealth {{yosso2005}} and on accounts of belonging for marginalized students {{strayhorn2019}}. Fourth, intervention studies could evaluate the recommendations above, for example by comparing courses with and without an explicit hidden-curriculum module, using mixed methods to examine both outcomes and experience.

## 6.7 Closing reflection

When I began this study, I expected to write about isolation: about students alone at kitchen tables, far from campus, missing the casual contact that on-campus students take for granted. What I found was more specific and, I think, more hopeful. The participants were not asking to be seen more. They were asking to be known: called by name, answered when they spoke, told the rules that others seemed to know already. These are not expensive things for a programme to provide. That they were so often absent, and so powerful when present, is the central lesson I take from the 14 people who gave their time to this study, and it is one I intend to carry into my own work in higher education.

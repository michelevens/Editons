# Chapter 5: Discussion

## 5.1 Introduction

This chapter interprets the four themes reported in Chapter 4 in light of the literature reviewed in Chapter 2. It does not restate the findings in full; instead, it asks what they mean for how belonging is understood in fully online master's study, and where they confirm, extend or unsettle existing accounts. The chapter is organized around the three research questions. Section 5.2 addresses RQ1 and argues that participants understood belonging primarily as recognition rather than visibility. Section 5.3 addresses RQ2, examining the programme practices that strengthened or weakened belonging, with particular attention to the hidden curriculum and to camera-on policies. Section 5.4 addresses RQ3 and considers how participants drew on family, work and peer resources, reading these through an asset-based lens. Section 5.5 draws the threads together into a tentative model of "recognition-centered belonging," and Section 5.6 reflects on the quality of the study and on how my own position shaped the interpretation offered here. Throughout, claims are calibrated to a small, purposive sample of 14 students in three programmes at one institution; the aim is analytic insight and transferable understanding, not generalization.

## 5.2 Belonging as recognition (RQ1)

### 5.2.1 From being seen to being known

The first research question asked how first-generation students describe belonging, or its absence, in a fully online master's programme. The most consistent answer across the 14 accounts was that belonging was experienced as being recognized: being known by name, having one's contribution answered, and having one's circumstances acknowledged. This was captured in the first theme, "Cameras off, still present." Participants rarely equated belonging with synchronous contact or with being visible on screen. Several of the participants who felt most connected, such as Keisha and Ifeoma, described courses in which they had seldom switched a camera on, while some who attended every live session, including Jake, described feeling peripheral.

This finding sits comfortably with the foundational claim that the need to belong is satisfied not by contact as such but by frequent interactions set within a framework of ongoing concern {{baumeister1995}}. What participants valued was precisely the second element: evidence that someone in the programme was attending to them as a particular person over time. A recorded lecture viewed alone, or a live session in a grid of thirty faces, offered contact without that concern. A brief, named reply from an instructor on a discussion board offered concern with very little contact. In this sample, the latter mattered more.

The finding also refines how the literature on online learning tends to treat presence. The Community of Inquiry framework distinguishes social, cognitive and teaching presence and treats social presence as the capacity of participants to project themselves as "real" people in a mediated environment {{garrison2000}}. Much subsequent practice, at least as participants described it at Riverside State University, appears to have translated social presence into visibility: cameras on, faces shown, homes on display. The accounts in Chapter 4 suggest that, for these students, the more important direction of travel was the reverse. Belonging depended less on projecting themselves into the course than on the course reaching back to them, through what the Community of Inquiry model would class as teaching presence. In other words, recognition was something done to and for students by others, rather than something students had to perform. I return to this point in Section 5.5 because it has practical consequences.

### 5.2.2 The asynchronous voice

The second subtheme, the asynchronous voice, adds texture. Several participants, notably Hoa, Luis and Yasmin, described discussion boards as the place where they felt most heard. The written, delayed format gave them time to compose, check and revise a contribution, which reduced the exposure they associated with speaking in a live seminar. Rovai's early work on sense of community at a distance argued that community in online courses rests on spirit, trust, interaction and shared learning goals, and that asynchronous discussion can support these when it is purposeful and facilitated {{rovai2002}}. The present findings are consistent with that argument, with one qualification: participants valued boards only when someone replied. A post that received no response from the instructor or peers was described as confirming invisibility rather than relieving it. The medium itself was neutral; what made the board a site of belonging was whether recognition followed.

### 5.2.3 Belonging as situated and fragile

Participants did not describe belonging as a stable trait that one either had or lacked. It shifted between courses, between instructors and even between weeks within a course. This is in keeping with Strayhorn's account of sense of belonging as context-dependent, sensitive to particular settings and relationships, and of heightened importance for students who occupy marginal positions in higher education {{strayhorn2019}}. It also echoes Hurtado and Carter's argument that belonging is a distinct construct, shaped by perceptions of climate rather than reducible to participation {{hurtado1997}}. Their work concerned campus climate for Latino undergraduates; the present study is not comparable in population or setting, but the underlying point travels: what students perceive about how the environment regards them appears to matter more than the volume of their activity within it.

For first-generation students specifically, the literature suggests that a weak or uncertain sense of fit is compounded by limited familial knowledge of how higher education works {{engle2008}}. The participants in this study had all completed a bachelor's degree, so they were in one sense experienced students. Yet several, including Darnell and Samuel, described the move to graduate study as a second threshold, one where the tacit rules had changed again and where the uncertainty they remembered from their first degree returned. This suggests that first-generation status is not a hurdle cleared once at undergraduate entry. It may resurface at each new level of study, a possibility I return to in the recommendations of Chapter 6.

## 5.3 Practices that strengthen and weaken belonging (RQ2)

### 5.3.1 The hidden curriculum, moved online

The second research question asked which programme practices and interactions participants identified as strengthening or weakening belonging. The second theme, "Nobody told me the rules," showed that a substantial part of what weakened belonging was not any single practice but an absence: the expectations that went unstated. Participants described having to decode APA conventions, the purpose of office hours, and the meaning of invitations to "reach out." The phrase "reach out," repeated in syllabi and announcements, was read by several participants, including Marisol and Tomasz, as a polite formula rather than a genuine offer, because nobody explained what reaching out would look like or what would count as an appropriate reason to do so.

The idea that higher education rests on implicit norms that some students arrive already knowing is well established in the literature on first-generation students {{engle2008;pascarella2005}}. Pascarella and Terenzini's synthesis notes that first-generation students tend to arrive with less anticipatory knowledge of academic expectations and that this disadvantage is mediated by the experiences institutions provide once they enroll {{pascarella2005}}. What the present study adds is a sense of how the online format can intensify this. On campus, tacit knowledge circulates informally: in corridors, before class, through overhearing what other students do. Online, those informal channels are largely absent. The participants who seemed to manage best were those who had found a substitute channel, usually the peer group chat discussed under RQ3. Those without one were left to infer the rules from the formal course site alone.

The second subtheme, asking as a risk, is significant because it shows why "reach out" invitations did not solve the problem. Participants described a fear that a question would expose them as unqualified, that they would be seen as a student who did not belong at this level. This is a recognizably first-generation concern, but it also shows the circularity at work: asking for help is the main mechanism through which tacit rules become explicit, yet asking was felt to carry a reputational cost precisely for the students who most needed to ask. Tinto's integration model frames persistence as a function of academic and social integration into the institution {{tinto1993}}. The accounts here suggest that, for these students, the burden of integration was being placed on them while the means of achieving it were obscured. This sharpens a familiar critique of integration models, discussed in Chapter 2, which is that they can locate the problem in the student who fails to integrate rather than in the institution that makes integration difficult.

### 5.3.2 The faculty member who translated

Against this background, the third subtheme stands out. Many participants could name one instructor who had made the implicit explicit: who explained what an assignment was really asking, demystified citation conventions without condescension, or said plainly that questions were expected rather than tolerated. Participants described these instructors as the point at which they began to feel they belonged in the programme. I read this as the clearest evidence in the study that belonging is something programmes can act on directly. The translating instructor did not offer more contact; often they offered less synchronous time than colleagues. What they offered was explicitness and a signal that not knowing was legitimate.

Lave and Wenger's account of legitimate peripheral participation is helpful here {{lave1991}}. Newcomers to a community of practice learn its ways by participating at the edges, with access to the practices of more experienced members. The legitimacy of that peripheral position is crucial: it allows newcomers to not yet know without being treated as outsiders. The translating instructor, in these terms, conferred legitimacy on peripheral participation. By naming the rules, they converted the hidden curriculum into an open one and made the student's partial knowledge an expected stage rather than a deficit. That this depended on individual instructors, rather than on programme design, is a weakness the recommendations in Chapter 6 address.

### 5.3.3 The cost of compulsory cameras

The practice most clearly identified as weakening belonging was compulsory camera use in synchronous sessions, discussed under the third subtheme of the first theme. Participants with caring responsibilities, such as Amara, Rosa and Priya, described the strain of showing domestic spaces and children to classmates and instructors. Luis, who worked night shifts, described sessions attended in a state of exhaustion in which being on camera felt like an additional test. For these students, cameras did not create presence; they created a demand to perform a version of studenthood their circumstances made difficult.

This finding should be stated carefully. Not all participants objected to cameras, and some, including Jake and Tomasz, the two full-time MBA students with lighter work commitments, described live video as useful. The pattern was that harm concentrated among those with night-shift work and caring roles, that is, among participants whose lives already diverged most from the traditional student model. A policy designed to increase social presence therefore appears, in this sample, to have distributed its costs unevenly, falling most heavily on those whom the literature suggests are most in need of belonging {{strayhorn2019}}. Kahu's sociocultural framing of engagement is useful here because it insists that engagement is shaped by students' lives outside the institution, including work, family and wellbeing, and by the structural features of the institution itself {{kahu2013}}. A camera mandate treats engagement as a behavior to be observed. A sociocultural view treats it as an outcome of conditions, some of which the student does not control.

## 5.4 Resources beyond the university (RQ3)

### 5.4.1 Peer networks: powerful and exclusionary

The third research question asked how participants drew on resources from outside the university to sustain their study. The third theme, "We built our own room," showed that peer networks formed on messaging platforms outside the official learning environment were, for many participants, the real classroom. The group chat was where the hidden curriculum was decoded, where deadlines were clarified, and where students admitted confusion without fear of being judged by staff. Study partners provided accountability, particularly for part-time students juggling full-time work.

These student-built spaces resemble informal communities of practice {{lave1991}}, and they performed much of the social and teaching presence work that the Community of Inquiry framework locates in the course itself {{garrison2000}}. That they existed outside the platform is important. It suggests that, for these participants, the official environment was not felt to be a safe place to not know, and so a parallel space was constructed where it was.

The third subtheme, exclusion when the chat formed without you, complicates any simple celebration of peer networks. Participants who joined a cohort late, who could not attend the early synchronous sessions where chats were typically set up, or who were less confident in initiating contact described finding out weeks later that a group existed without them. Samuel and Ben, for example, described this experience in different ways. Peer networks thus reproduced, within the student body, the same pattern of uneven access that participants identified in the hidden curriculum: those already well connected gained a further resource, and those at the margins lost one. This is perhaps the least comfortable finding of the study, because it suggests that the very resource students built to compensate for the programme's omissions could itself become a source of exclusion. It also implies that leaving peer connection entirely to students is not a neutral choice.

### 5.4.2 Family and work as resources, not only pressures

The fourth theme, "I'm doing this for all of us," showed that family and work were simultaneously sources of strain and sources of strength. Much of the first-generation literature, including work I draw on in Chapter 2, frames these students in terms of what they lack: academic preparation, cultural capital, family knowledge of higher education {{engle2008}}. The participants in this study did describe gaps of this kind, especially in the second theme. Yet their accounts of family were overwhelmingly framed as motivation and meaning. Family members were an audience for the degree and a reason to persist. Rosa and Samuel both described studying in part to show younger family members what was possible.

Yosso's model of community cultural wealth provides a better fit for these accounts than a deficit lens {{yosso2005}}. Yosso identifies forms of capital that communities of color and other marginalized groups cultivate and that conventional accounts of cultural capital overlook, among them aspirational, familial, navigational and resistant capital. Participants' accounts of studying "for all of us" can be read as familial and aspirational capital: a collective sense of purpose that sustained them through difficult terms. Their ability to build peer networks outside the platform can be read as navigational and social capital in action. I want to be careful not to overextend Yosso's framework, which was developed within critical race theory and concerns communities of color specifically; the participants in this study were diverse, and I did not analyze race systematically. The more modest claim is that an asset-based frame made visible strengths that a deficit frame would have obscured, and that participants themselves described their families in asset terms.

The second subtheme, explaining the degree at home, shows the other side. Participants described the labor of making their study legible to family members who had not experienced higher education: why an evening had to be protected, why a master's degree was worth the cost, what the degree would lead to. This work was invisible to the programme but consumed time and emotional energy. It is also an example of how first-generation status operates within the family, not only within the institution.

### 5.4.3 The employer as ally or obstacle

Finally, employers emerged as a decisive and largely unacknowledged factor. For participants such as Priya and Ifeoma, a supportive manager who allowed flexible hours or recognized the degree's relevance made study manageable. For others, including Darnell and Luis, rigid scheduling or indifference made it precarious. Kahu's sociocultural framing again helps: engagement cannot be understood without the conditions of students' lives {{kahu2013}}. For the 11 participants who worked full-time, the workplace was arguably as much a part of their learning environment as the course site. Programmes rarely engage with this, yet the accounts here suggest it shapes whether students can attend live sessions, meet deadlines and remain enrolled.

## 5.5 Toward recognition-centered belonging

Taken together, the findings suggest a reframing that I tentatively call recognition-centered belonging. In this view, belonging in fully online study is sustained less by maximizing synchronous visibility than by consistent acts of recognition: naming students, responding to their contributions, translating tacit expectations, and acknowledging the circumstances of their lives. These acts can be performed by instructors, by programme design and by peers, but they are not evenly distributed unless someone deliberately distributes them.

The reframing has three components, each grounded in the themes of Chapter 4. First, recognition is relational and directional: it flows from the programme to the student, rather than being something the student must earn by performing presence. This shifts emphasis within the Community of Inquiry framework from social presence as self-projection toward teaching presence as attentive response {{garrison2000}}. Second, recognition includes making the implicit explicit. A programme that names its rules recognizes that students arrive with different kinds of prior knowledge and treats that difference as legitimate, in the manner of legitimate peripheral participation {{lave1991}}. Third, recognition extends beyond the institution to the resources students bring. Acknowledging family motivation, workplace constraints and student-built networks treats students as people with community cultural wealth rather than as deficits to be remediated {{yosso2005}}.

This reframing is consistent with the core claim that the need to belong involves ongoing concern, not merely contact {{baumeister1995}}, and with the argument that belonging is context-dependent and of particular significance for marginalized students {{strayhorn2019}}. Where it departs from much practice, at least as participants described it, is in questioning the assumption that more synchronous, visible interaction is the main route to belonging online. For students who are working, caring and studying at night, that route may be the one most likely to exclude them.

I offer this as an interpretive proposition generated from a small qualitative study, not as a tested model. Its value lies in the questions it raises for programme design and for future research, which Chapter 6 takes up.

## 5.6 Reflections on quality and reflexivity

### 5.6.1 Quality of the analysis

The quality of this study is best judged against criteria appropriate to reflexive qualitative work rather than against criteria borrowed from measurement-based research. Braun and Clarke are explicit that reflexive thematic analysis does not seek coding reliability; themes are generated through the researcher's interpretive engagement with the data, and agreement metrics between coders would misrepresent that process {{braun2019}}. For this reason, no inter-rater reliability was calculated, as explained in Chapter 3. Instead, I have tried to meet Tracy's "big-tent" criteria {{tracy2010}}. The topic is worthy in that it concerns a group whose online experience has received relatively little attention [source needed]. Rich rigor was pursued through a sample of 14 interviews of 60 to 75 minutes each, assessed against the principle of information power {{malterud2016}}, and through an analytic process that moved from 212 initial codes to four themes and 11 subthemes. Sincerity was pursued through the reflexive journal and the positionality statement in Chapter 3. Credibility rests on thick description and on quotation from all 14 participants, with no single voice dominating.

Lincoln and Guba's criteria of trustworthiness offer a complementary lens {{lincoln1985}}. Transferability is supported by the description of the setting and participants in Chapter 3, which allows readers to judge how far the findings might apply in their own contexts. Dependability and confirmability are supported by the audit trail of codes, theme maps and journal entries. The member reflections, in which summaries were sent to all participants and nine replied, are better described as dialogue than as validation, consistent with the reflexive tradition {{braun2022}}. Responses broadly recognized the themes, and the additions some participants offered were folded into the final round of refining and naming.

### 5.6.2 How my position shaped the interpretation

As discussed in Chapter 3, I am a first-generation graduate who studied part-time online. Finlay argues that reflexivity should be practiced as an ongoing, explicit examination of how the researcher's position shapes the research at every stage, and not as a single confessional statement {{finlay2002}}. Two effects of my position seem worth recording here. First, my own experience made me attentive to the hidden curriculum, and I recognized the "reach out" problem quickly. That recognition helped me to see a pattern, but it also created a risk that I would read my own experience into participants' accounts. The reflexive journal shows that I initially coded some ambivalent comments about office hours as fear, and I revised these when rereading showed that some participants were simply too busy to attend. Second, my sympathy with an asset-based reading of family may have made me slower to see the pressures family placed on participants. Subtheme 4b, explaining the degree at home, was developed relatively late, after a second pass through the data prompted by my supervisor's questions. I do not think these influences invalidate the analysis; in reflexive thematic analysis, the researcher's subjectivity is a resource as well as a risk {{braun2019}}. But readers should know they were present.

## 5.7 Summary

This chapter has argued that the participants in this study experienced belonging in fully online master's study primarily as recognition rather than visibility (RQ1); that belonging was weakened by an unstated hidden curriculum and by compulsory camera policies whose costs fell most heavily on night-shift and caring students, and strengthened by instructors who translated tacit expectations (RQ2); and that participants sustained their study through peer networks that were powerful but exclusionary, and through family and workplace resources best understood in asset rather than deficit terms (RQ3). These findings were brought together in the proposition of recognition-centered belonging. Chapter 6 concludes the thesis by summarizing its contribution, setting out recommendations for programmes, acknowledging the study's limitations and proposing directions for future research.

# Chapter 3: Methodology

## 3.1 Introduction

This chapter explains how the study was designed and carried out, and why. Chapter 2 argued that research on belonging in higher education has been built largely around residential students and integration models, and that first-generation students studying fully online have rarely been asked to describe belonging in their own terms. That gap shaped the choices reported here. Because the aim was to understand how first-generation students experience belonging in fully online master's programs, and which program practices they say strengthen or weaken it, the study needed a design that kept participants' meanings at the center rather than measuring them against a predefined construct.

The chapter sets out the research paradigm and design (Section 3.2), the setting (3.3), sampling and recruitment (3.4), the participants (3.5), data generation (3.6) and analysis (3.7). It then addresses quality (3.8), my position as researcher (3.9), ethics (3.10) and limitations (3.11). The three research questions introduced in Chapter 1 remain the reference point throughout:

1. RQ1. How do first-generation students describe belonging (or its absence) in a fully online master's program?
2. RQ2. Which program practices and interactions do they identify as strengthening or weakening that belonging?
3. RQ3. How do participants draw on resources from outside the university (family, work, community) to sustain their study?

> **Sample note.** The data reported in this thesis are synthetic, created to illustrate how such a study is reported. No participants were recruited.

## 3.2 Research paradigm and design

### 3.2.1 An interpretivist stance

{{creswell2018q|n}} encourage researchers to make their philosophical assumptions explicit, because those assumptions shape what counts as evidence and what claims a study can make. This study adopts an interpretivist position. It treats belonging not as a fixed quantity a student possesses but as an experience made and remade in relationships and settings, which different people may understand differently. It assumes that knowledge about such experiences is co-constructed: what participants told me was shaped by the questions I asked, by who I am, and by the conversation that developed between us.

This stance follows from the framing in Chapter 2. If belonging is a fundamental human need {{baumeister1995}} that is nonetheless highly sensitive to context and especially consequential for students positioned as outsiders {{strayhorn2019}}, then the most informative data are accounts of particular contexts as students lived them. A survey could tell us how strongly participants agreed with statements about belonging; it could not easily tell us what belonging meant to a nurse logging on after a night shift, or why a discussion board might feel more welcoming than a live seminar. Asking participants to define the terms of their own experience is also one way, though not a guarantee, of resisting the deficit framing of first-generation students discussed in Chapter 2 {{yosso2005}}.

### 3.2.2 A qualitative interview design

{{creswell2018q|n}} describe several approaches to qualitative inquiry. I considered three seriously. A phenomenological design suited the focus on lived experience, but its aim of identifying an essential structure sat uneasily with my expectation that belonging would look different for a full-time MBA student with no dependents and a part-time nursing student raising three children. A single-program case study would have narrowed the range of experiences available. Grounded theory would have pushed toward an explanatory model, more than a master's study with 14 participants could responsibly claim. A generic qualitative interview design, analyzed thematically, allowed me to explore patterns of meaning across participants while staying anchored in their accounts. Individual interviews were preferred to focus groups because topics such as fear of appearing unqualified, or tension with family and employers, are ones participants might hesitate to raise in front of classmates.

### 3.2.3 Why reflexive thematic analysis

The data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis as developed by {{braun2006|n}} and later clarified in {{braun2019}} and {{braun2022}}. {{braun2019|n}} distinguish their reflexive approach from coding reliability and codebook approaches. In the reflexive version, themes do not emerge from the data; they are generated by the researcher through sustained interpretive engagement, and the researcher's subjectivity is treated as a resource rather than a bias to be eliminated.

This approach suited the study for three reasons. It is theoretically flexible, so it could be used within an interpretivist framing without importing a particular theory as a coding frame. It supports both semantic analysis, close to what participants said, and latent analysis of the assumptions beneath it; both proved necessary when participants described the "rules" of online study that nobody had explained. And it is honest about the analyst's role. Given my own background as a first-generation graduate who studied part-time online (Section 3.9), an approach that asked me to account for my position was more credible than one that asked me to pretend to neutrality.

One consequence is that the study does not test hypotheses or report inter-rater reliability. {{braun2019|n}} argue that coding agreement measures presuppose a correct way to code the data on which independent coders should converge, an assumption at odds with reflexive analysis, in which different analysts would be expected to notice different things. Instead of agreement statistics, I sought depth and transparency through a reflexive journal, analytic discussions with my supervisor and member reflections (Section 3.8).

## 3.3 Research setting

The study took place at Riverside State University (a pseudonym), a mid-sized public university in the United States. Riverside State is the research site; it is a different institution from Kestrel Bay University, where I was enrolled. Choosing an external site reduced the chance that I would interview people I knew or who might feel obligated to take part.

Three fully online master's programs took part: a Master of Business Administration (MBA), a Master of Science in Nursing Leadership, and a Master of Arts in Instructional Design. All three combined asynchronous materials and discussion boards with scheduled synchronous sessions by video call, and none required on-campus attendance. Each admitted full-time and part-time students. As participants described them, the programs differed in ways that mattered for the findings: the MBA held more synchronous sessions and expected cameras on, the nursing program leaned on asynchronous work suited to shift patterns, and instructional design fell between the two. Including three programs widened the range of circumstances in the sample; it does not support systematic comparison between programs, which was not the aim.

## 3.4 Sampling and recruitment

### 3.4.1 Inclusion criteria

Participants were eligible if they (a) were enrolled in the first or second year of one of the three programs; (b) identified as first-generation students, defined here as students neither of whose parents holds a bachelor's degree; and (c) were 18 or over and able to be interviewed in English by video call. The definition follows common usage in the literature reviewed in Chapter 2 {{engle2008}}; its limits are discussed in Section 3.11.

### 3.4.2 Purposive sampling and recruitment

I used purposive sampling with a maximum variation strategy. The goal was not representativeness but a group whose circumstances differed along the dimensions most likely to shape online belonging: program, enrollment mode, year of study, employment and caring responsibilities. Maximum variation helps to show which patterns hold across very different situations and which are specific to particular circumstances.

Program administrators circulated a call for participants in the three program newsletters, so that I had no direct access to student contact lists. The call described the study, the eligibility criteria, the interview length and a $25 gift card offered in recognition of participants' time. Twenty-three students completed a short screening form; two did not meet the first-generation criterion. From the remaining 21, I selected participants in stages, prioritizing under-represented combinations as recruitment progressed. For example, once several part-time MBA students with children had been interviewed, I gave priority to full-time students and to nursing students working shifts. Students not selected were thanked and told the study had reached its planned size.

### 3.4.3 Sample size and information power

Qualitative sample sizes are often justified by saturation, but saturation sits awkwardly with reflexive thematic analysis: if themes are generated through interpretation, there is no fixed point at which the data are exhausted {{braun2019}}. I therefore used the concept of information power {{malterud2016}}. {{malterud2016|n}} suggest that the more relevant information a sample holds, the fewer participants are needed, and they identify five considerations: the breadth of the aim, the specificity of the sample, the use of established theory, the quality of dialogue and the analysis strategy.

In this study the aim was focused and the sample specific, since every participant held the experience under study. Established concepts of belonging, presence and community cultural wealth served as sensitizing ideas, and the long, conversational interviews produced rich accounts. Against this, the analysis sought variation across three programs and many personal circumstances, which called for more participants than a homogeneous study would need. I planned for 12 to 16 participants and stopped at 14, when later interviews were adding nuance to existing patterns rather than opening new lines of inquiry relevant to the research questions. This is a judgement, not a proof.

## 3.6 Data generation

Each participant took part in one semi-structured interview by video call, lasting between 60 and 75 minutes, at a time of their choosing; for those working nights this was often late morning. Participants could keep their camera on or off, and several kept it off. In light of the findings on compulsory cameras in Chapter 4, I am glad that option was offered; it also signaled that the interview was not another assessed space.

The interview guide (Appendix A) was organized around the research questions but used loosely. It opened by inviting participants to describe how they came to the program and what their first weeks had been like, which let them decide where the story began. Later sections asked about moments of feeling part of the program or apart from it, about instructors, peers and program routines, and about people and commitments outside the university. Prompts sought concrete examples ("Can you tell me about a time when...?") rather than general evaluations. The guide was piloted with two first-generation graduates of online programs elsewhere; their feedback led me to move family questions later and to drop a question asking participants to rate their belonging on a scale, which both found reductive.

With consent, interviews were audio recorded; video was not retained. I transcribed each interview verbatim myself, which served as the first stage of familiarization, and de-identified transcripts at that point. After each interview I wrote brief field notes on tone, surprises and connections to earlier interviews, which became part of the reflexive journal (Section 3.9).

## 3.7 Data analysis

The analysis followed the six phases described by {{braun2006|n}} and elaborated in {{braun2022}}. In practice it was recursive: the phases describe kinds of analytic work more than a strict sequence. Transcripts were managed in qualitative data analysis software, used for organizing and retrieving extracts rather than for any automated analysis. Table 3.2 summarizes the process.

**Table 3.2** Phases of reflexive thematic analysis as applied in this study

| Phase | Analytic work in this study | Main output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Familiarization | Transcribing all 14 interviews; repeated reading; notes | Familiarization notes per transcript |
| 2. Coding | Full coding of all transcripts, semantic and latent, twice | 212 initial codes |
| 3. Generating initial themes | Clustering codes around shared central organizing concepts | Six candidate themes |
| 4. Developing and reviewing themes | Checking candidates against extracts and the full data set | Four themes, provisional subthemes |
| 5. Refining, defining and naming | Writing definitions; settling boundaries and names | Four themes, 11 subthemes |
| 6. Writing up | Selecting extracts; building the analytic narrative | Chapter 4 |

*Note.* The phases overlapped and were revisited throughout.

**Familiarization and coding.** For each interview I wrote familiarization notes on what the participant cared about and what puzzled me. One note, written after Luis's interview, recorded that he spoke about cameras as exposure rather than connection, an idea that later shaped Theme 1. I then coded every transcript in full, attaching semantic codes (for example, "instructor used my name in feedback") and latent codes (for example, "asking for help as proof of not belonging"). I coded the data set a second time in reverse interview order, to reduce the risk that early interviews set the terms for later ones. This produced 212 initial codes. An excerpt from the codebook appears in Appendix C; it is an audit trail of my decisions, not an instrument for another coder to reproduce the analysis.

**Generating and reviewing themes.** {{braun2019|n}} distinguish themes, organized around a central concept, from topic summaries that collect everything said about a domain. Several early clusters were of the latter kind, such as a "cameras" cluster that gathered every mention of video regardless of meaning. Reworking these yielded six candidate themes. On review against the extracts and the full data set, a candidate on technology proved mainly descriptive and was dissolved, its codes about discussion boards as a space for voice moving to the theme on recognition. A candidate on time pressure overlapped heavily with family and work and was merged into Theme 4. Thematic maps helped me see, for instance, that peer networks (Theme 3) were partly a response to the unexplained expectations of Theme 2.

**Defining, naming and writing up.** I wrote a definition for each of the four themes stating its central organizing concept and its boundaries, and settled the 11 subthemes. Theme names keep participants' language visible. As {{braun2022|n}} suggest, writing was itself analytic: drafting Chapter 4 clarified, for example, that the instructor who "translated" expectations belonged with the hidden curriculum rather than with recognition. Extracts were chosen to illustrate analytic claims, with a spread across participants so that everyone is quoted and nobody dominates.

## 3.8 Quality and trustworthiness

I used two complementary frameworks. {{lincoln1985|n}} address credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability. {{tracy2010|n}} proposes eight "big-tent" criteria: a worthy topic, rich rigor, sincerity, credibility, resonance, significant contribution, ethics and meaningful coherence. Tracy's criteria are more open to reflexive approaches and shaped my attention to sincerity and coherence in particular. Table 3.3 summarizes the main strategies.

**Table 3.3** Strategies used to support the quality of the study

| Criterion | Strategy in this study |
|---|---|
| Credibility | Prolonged engagement; thick description; member reflections; supervisory discussion |
| Transferability | Detailed description of setting and participants |
| Dependability | Audit trail of codes, theme maps, definitions and memos |
| Confirmability and sincerity | Reflexive journal; positionality statement (Section 3.9) |
| Rich rigor | Maximum variation sample; long interviews; all transcripts coded twice |
| Meaningful coherence | Alignment of paradigm, questions, method and quality criteria |

*Note.* Criteria drawn from {{lincoln1985}} and {{tracy2010}}.

After the themes were developed, I sent each participant a two-page plain-language summary and invited a response by email or brief call. Nine of the 14 replied. I treated these as member reflections, an opportunity for dialogue and elaboration, rather than as member checks intended to validate the analysis {{tracy2010}}. Within a reflexive approach, participants cannot confirm my interpretation as true; they can say where it resonates, where it misses something and what they would add. Two replies sharpened the analysis. Keisha wrote that the instructor who translated expectations in her program had once been a first-generation student herself; Darnell noted that the summary understated how much the group chat had helped him early on before it became, in his words, "noisy and cliquey." Tomasz said cameras had not been an issue for him, consistent with the analysis, which found their costs fell mainly on participants with caring roles or night shifts. I did not change the themes, but I revised the accounts of subtheme 2c and of the peer chat.

My supervisor did not code the data, so our regular discussions were not a reliability check. Their value lay in being asked to justify interpretations. One discussion led me to see that I had been reading the absence of complaint among full-time participants as evidence of belonging, when it more plausibly reflected different expectations.

## 3.9 Researcher reflexivity and positionality

{{finlay2002|n}} describes reflexivity as a practice running through a whole project, and warns that it can slide into self-absorption. I have tried to use it to examine how my position shaped the research rather than to foreground my own story.

I came to this study as a first-generation graduate. Neither of my parents attended university, and I completed my undergraduate degree part-time and online while working. Much of what participants described was familiar: the uncertainty about unwritten rules, the reluctance to ask questions, the mixed pride and distance of explaining a degree at home. This was an asset. Several participants said they volunteered because the call made clear I was first-generation myself, and some spoke more openly after I briefly shared my background.

The same closeness carried the risk of hearing my own story in participants' accounts. I kept a reflexive journal from design to final draft, recording reactions to each interview and moments when an interpretation felt too comfortable. An entry after Amara's interview notes that I had been waiting for her to describe feeling like an impostor and that she had not; on rereading, her account was about being overlooked rather than self-doubt, a distinction central to Theme 1. I also deliberately attended to accounts unlike mine, including those of full-time participants such as Jake and Hoa, whose experiences were in several respects more positive than my own had been. Finally, as an outsider to Riverside State I had no stake in its programs, which reduced pressure on participants to present them favorably, but meant I learned about program practices mainly through participants' accounts.

## 3.10 Ethical considerations

The study received approval from the Kestrel Bay University Institutional Review Board (protocol KBU-IRB-2025-118), and Riverside State University granted permission for recruitment through its program newsletters. Selected participants received the information sheet and consent form (Appendix B) at least three days before the interview, and the main points were reviewed verbally at its start. Participants were told that taking part was voluntary, had no bearing on their studies, would not be disclosed to program staff, and could be withdrawn without reason until the analysis was finalized. No one withdrew.

Some topics, including financial strain, family conflict and feelings of inadequacy, were sensitive. Participants could skip questions or pause, and each interview ended with a check on how they were feeling and information about student support services. Two participants became briefly upset; in both cases I paused and continued only when they chose to. Confidentiality was protected through pseudonyms, de-identification and the alteration of identifying details in quotations, with particular care given that some combinations of characteristics could be distinctive within a program. Recordings were stored on an encrypted university drive and deleted once transcripts were checked. The gift card was sent electronically after each interview, regardless of how much of the interview was completed.

## 3.11 Methodological limitations

First, each participant was interviewed once. Belonging is likely to change over a program, and a single conversation captures one moment; a longitudinal design would have shown, for example, whether early exclusion from a group chat had lasting effects. Second, newsletter recruitment favored students who read program communications and had time to volunteer. Those least connected to their programs, or close to withdrawing, may be underrepresented, which matters in a study of belonging. Third, without systematic data on race, ethnicity, gender or disability, the analysis cannot speak to how these intersected with first-generation status, although the literature suggests they matter {{hurtado1997}}; the first-generation category itself also masks considerable diversity. Fourth, all three programs were at one institution, and practices such as camera policies vary. The findings are offered for transferability, not generalization. Finally, the analysis is mine; another analyst, particularly one without my background, might have generated different themes from the same data, which is why I have tried to make my decisions visible.

## 3.12 Chapter summary

This chapter has set out an interpretivist qualitative design in which 14 first-generation students in three fully online master's programs at Riverside State University took part in semi-structured interviews analyzed through reflexive thematic analysis. It has justified maximum variation sampling and a sample size based on information power, described how 212 initial codes became four themes with 11 subthemes, explained why inter-rater reliability was not used, and set out the strategies for quality, my positionality, the ethical safeguards and the main limitations. Chapter 4 now presents the four themes in relation to the three research questions.

# Chapter 2: Literature Review

Chapter 1 set out the problem this study addresses: that knowledge about belonging in higher education has been built mainly around residential undergraduates, that research on online community has seldom attended to first-generation students, and that much writing about those students has framed them by what they lack. This chapter reviews the literature behind each of those claims. It is organized by argument rather than by author. Section 2.2 examines what belonging is, moving from belonging as a general human need to belonging as a context-dependent experience in higher education. Section 2.3 considers integration models of persistence and the critiques that have reshaped them, and introduces a sociocultural view of engagement. Section 2.4 turns to the online setting, looking at how presence and community have been theorized when students and teachers are not physically together. Section 2.5 reviews research on first-generation students, and Section 2.6 sets the deficit framings common in that research against an asset-based alternative. Section 2.7 introduces a situated view of learning as participation. Section 2.8 identifies the gap the study addresses, and Section 2.9 draws the strands together into the conceptual framework that guided the research.

## 2.1 Searching and selecting the literature

The literature reviewed here was identified through an iterative process rather than a single systematic search. I began with texts recommended during the taught component of my degree and with frameworks I already knew from my own experience as an online student, then followed citations backward to foundational works and forward to studies that applied them. Searches of education and psychology databases used combinations of terms relating to belonging, sense of community, first-generation status, graduate study and online or distance learning. I gave priority to works that offered conceptual frameworks the study could use. Where I could not locate adequate support for a claim in the sources I worked with, I mark it as such rather than overstate the evidence. This approach is suited to a qualitative master's study, whose aim is to situate an interpretive inquiry within relevant theory rather than to synthesize all available evidence, but it means that the review should be read as a reasoned selection rather than a census of the field.

## 2.2 What is belonging?

### 2.2.1 Belonging as a fundamental need

The most influential argument for the importance of belonging comes from psychology. {{baumeister1995|n}} proposed that the need to belong is a fundamental human motivation, comparable in its reach to other basic needs. On their account, people are driven to form and maintain at least a minimum number of lasting, positive and significant relationships. Two features of the need are central. The first is that people need frequent interactions that are, on the whole, pleasant or at least not hostile. The second is that these interactions need to take place within a framework of ongoing mutual concern, so that people perceive the relationship as stable and as one in which the other person cares about their welfare. Interactions without that sense of continuing concern, or concern without regular contact, satisfy the need only partially.

This framing has two implications for the present study. First, it suggests that belonging is not a luxury that students can do without once their practical needs are met. When the need is thwarted, {{baumeister1995|n}} argued, people suffer a range of negative consequences for their wellbeing and functioning. Second, the two-part structure of the need, contact and continuing concern, offers a useful lens for the online setting. Online programs often provide frequent contact of a certain kind, in the form of announcements, discussion posts and scheduled sessions, but it is a separate question whether students experience that contact as occurring within relationships in which someone knows and cares about them. A program could, in principle, generate a great deal of interaction without satisfying the second condition at all.

The account is also notable for what it does not say. Baumeister and Leary were concerned with belonging as a general feature of human psychology rather than with any particular setting, and their argument does not specify how the need is met in institutions, how it varies between groups, or how it is shaped by power and status. For those questions, the study turns to research in higher education.

### 2.2.2 Sense of belonging in higher education

Within higher education research, sense of belonging has been developed as a construct in its own right, distinct from related ideas such as satisfaction, involvement or integration. {{hurtado1997|n}} played an important part in this development. Studying Latino college students, they argued that existing models had tended to describe students' integration into college in terms defined by the institution, and that what was missing was attention to students' own perceptions of whether they were part of the campus community. They treated sense of belonging as a psychological dimension of students' experience and showed that it was shaped by students' perceptions of the campus racial climate. In other words, whether students felt they belonged depended not only on what they did, but on how they read the environment around them, including whether they perceived hostility or discrimination.

{{strayhorn2019|n}} built on this tradition to offer a broader model of college students' sense of belonging. In his account, sense of belonging refers to students' perceived social support on campus, their feeling of connectedness, and their experience of mattering, of feeling cared about, accepted, respected and valued by others in the group. Several of the propositions in Strayhorn's model are directly relevant to this study. Belonging is described as a basic need, consistent with {{baumeister1995|n}}, and as a motivation that can drive behavior. It is described as context-dependent: a student may feel a strong sense of belonging in one setting and very little in another, and the importance of belonging may increase in contexts that are unfamiliar or in which students feel out of place. It is also described as particularly significant for students who are marginalized in higher education, for whom questions of belonging may be more salient and its absence more consequential {{strayhorn2019}}. Finally, Strayhorn emphasizes that belonging is not achieved once and then held, but must be satisfied on an ongoing basis as circumstances change.

These propositions shaped the study in concrete ways. The idea that belonging is context-dependent led me to ask participants about belonging at different levels, in a particular course, with a particular instructor, in a cohort, in the program and in the university, rather than asking only whether they felt they belonged in general. The idea that belonging is heightened for marginalized students informed the decision to study first-generation students specifically. The emphasis on mattering, being cared about and valued, foreshadowed one of the central findings of the study, reported in Chapter 4: that participants located belonging less in being visible to others than in being recognized by them.

### 2.2.3 Belonging, climate and the question of who decides

One thread that runs from {{hurtado1997|n}} through {{strayhorn2019|n}} is that belonging is not simply a property of the individual student. It depends on how institutions are organized and how students read them. This matters because it shifts responsibility. If belonging were purely a matter of individual disposition or effort, then students who did not feel they belonged might reasonably be encouraged to try harder, to attend more events, or to reach out more. If belonging depends substantially on climate and context, then the institution and its practices become part of the explanation, and part of what might change.

This point is central to the study's second research question, which asks which program practices participants associate with strengthening or weakening their belonging. It also suggests a methodological consideration: asking students to describe belonging in their own terms, rather than defining it in advance, may reveal aspects of the experience that predefined measures miss, which is one reason this study adopted a qualitative approach (see Chapter 3).

## 2.3 Integration, persistence and engagement

### 2.3.1 Integration models of persistence

Any review of the literature on student belonging must engage with the work of Vincent Tinto, whose model of student departure has shaped research and practice for decades. In its revised form, {{tinto1993|n}} described persistence as the outcome of a longitudinal process in which students' intentions and commitments interact with their experiences in the academic and social systems of the institution. Students who become integrated into both systems, through formal academic performance and interaction with faculty on the one hand, and through peer relationships and extracurricular involvement on the other, are more likely to strengthen their commitment to the institution and to persist. Students whose experiences leave them insufficiently integrated are more likely to leave.

The model was influential in part because it located the causes of departure not only in students' characteristics but in the interaction between students and institutions, and because it suggested that institutions could act to improve persistence. Tinto also gave explicit attention to the idea that students separate from earlier communities, such as family and home, in the course of becoming members of the college community {{tinto1993}}.

The model was developed principally with traditional-age, residential undergraduates in mind, and Tinto himself discussed how the processes it describes might differ for other populations, including commuting and adult students {{tinto1993}}. Even so, its emphasis on social integration through physical participation in campus life sits uneasily with the experience of students in fully online programs, who have no campus to be integrated into in the usual sense. Its emphasis on academic integration through interaction with faculty is more readily transferable, but the forms that interaction takes online are different, and the study examines what participants made of them.

### 2.3.2 Critiques of integration

Integration models have been critiqued on several grounds, and these critiques are directly relevant to first-generation students. The first concerns the assumption that successful students separate from their communities of origin in order to integrate into the institution. For students from groups historically underrepresented in higher education, this assumption can imply that success requires leaving behind family and community, rather than drawing on them. {{hurtado1997|n}} argued that integration, as conventionally conceived, had not adequately captured the experience of students of color, and proposed that attending to students' sense of belonging, their own perception of their place in the institution, offered a more appropriate way of understanding their experience. On this view, belonging is not the same thing as integration as defined by the institution, and a student may participate in the expected activities while still feeling that she does not belong.

The second critique concerns where responsibility sits. Integration models can be read, especially in practice, as placing the onus on students to integrate themselves, and so as implicitly attributing departure to students' failure to do so. Asset-based perspectives, discussed in Section 2.6, have pressed this critique further by questioning whose norms define successful integration in the first place {{yosso2005}}.

The third critique is particularly relevant to the present study. For students who study part-time, work full-time and care for others, the life outside the institution is not something left behind but something that runs alongside study at every point. {{engle2008|n}}, examining the experiences of low-income, first-generation students, emphasized that these students were more likely to combine study with work and family responsibilities and to have less opportunity for the kinds of campus involvement that integration models associate with persistence. A model that treats external commitments mainly as competing pulls away from the institution may underplay the ways in which those same commitments motivate and sustain students. The third research question of this study was designed with this possibility in mind.

### 2.3.3 A sociocultural view of engagement

A related body of work has examined student engagement, a concept that overlaps with but is not identical to belonging. {{kahu2013|n}} identified four broad perspectives in the engagement literature: a behavioral perspective concerned with student behavior and effective teaching practice; a psychological perspective concerned with engagement as an internal state with emotional, cognitive and behavioral dimensions; a sociocultural perspective concerned with the influence of the wider social and political context; and a holistic perspective that seeks to bring these together. Kahu argued that each perspective illuminated part of the picture but that none was sufficient alone, and proposed a framework in which engagement is understood as a psychosocial process influenced by structural factors relating to both the institution and the student, with antecedents and consequences that extend beyond the classroom.

Two features of this framework are important here. First, it treats students' lives outside the university, including their backgrounds, their responsibilities and the support available to them, as integral to understanding engagement rather than as peripheral variables {{kahu2013}}. Second, it places the relationship between student and institution at the center, so that engagement is not something students possess or lack but something produced in the interaction between them and the settings they encounter. This sociocultural framing informed the study's design by encouraging me to ask about participants' work, family and community, and its emphasis on the student-institution relationship aligns with the context-dependent understanding of belonging set out in Section 2.2. Engagement and belonging should not, however, be conflated: a student may complete every reading and post thoughtfully while feeling that nobody in the program knows who she is.

## 2.4 Presence and community online

### 2.4.1 The Community of Inquiry framework

When students and instructors do not share a physical space, the question of how they come to be present to one another becomes explicit. The most widely used framework for thinking about this question in higher education is the Community of Inquiry model proposed by {{garrison2000|n}}. Developed through analysis of computer conferencing in higher education, the framework describes worthwhile educational experience in text-based environments as arising from the interaction of three elements: cognitive presence, the extent to which participants are able to construct meaning through sustained reflection and discourse; social presence, the ability of participants to project their personal characteristics into the community and so present themselves to others as real people; and teaching presence, the design, facilitation and direction of cognitive and social processes toward meaningful learning outcomes.

Several features of the framework are relevant to this study. The first is that it was developed for text-based, largely asynchronous environments. {{garrison2000|n}} took seriously the possibility that written, asynchronous discussion could support rich forms of presence and inquiry, and did not treat it as a poor substitute for face-to-face interaction. This is worth emphasizing because some subsequent practice in online education has come to equate presence with synchronous video, as if being seen in real time were the most authentic form of being present. The Community of Inquiry framework does not support that equation. It locates social presence in the capacity to project oneself as a person, which can happen in writing.

The second is the role of teaching presence. Garrison and colleagues treated teaching presence as a responsibility that includes both the design of the educational experience and the facilitation of discourse within it {{garrison2000}}. This positions the instructor's choices, how tasks are framed, how discussion is moderated, how expectations are communicated, as central to whether a community of inquiry forms. This emphasis connects to the study's interest in program practices (RQ2), and to the finding, reported in Chapter 4, that a single instructor who made implicit expectations explicit could reshape a participant's experience.

The framework has limitations for the purposes of this study. It is concerned with the conditions for inquiry rather than with belonging as such, and it does not specifically address how students' social positions, such as their generational status, might shape their capacity or willingness to project themselves socially. These are questions the present study takes up.

### 2.4.2 Sense of community at a distance

Where the Community of Inquiry framework focuses on the conditions for learning, research on sense of community in online courses addresses more directly the question of whether students feel connected to one another. {{rovai2002|n}} drew on the broader literature on psychological sense of community to examine how such a sense might be built among learners at a distance. He described classroom community in terms of members' feelings of connectedness, including spirit, trust and interaction among members, and of their sense of sharing common learning goals and expectations. On his account, a strong sense of community is associated with learners being more likely to persist and to support one another.

{{rovai2002|n}} identified a range of factors that instructors and course designers could influence to strengthen sense of community, including the extent of interaction between learners and between learners and instructors, the presence of social exchange alongside academic discussion, the fairness and inclusiveness of course practices, the use of group activities, the instructor's facilitation style, and the size of the learning community. Several of these factors map directly onto practices that participants in this study discussed, particularly the moderation of discussion boards, the design of group work and the conduct of synchronous sessions.

As with the Community of Inquiry framework, Rovai's account treats online learners largely as a single population. It identifies what tends to build community in general, but does not examine whether those same practices work in the same way for all students. A practice that strengthens community for one group may have different effects for another. A requirement that all students attend synchronous sessions with cameras on, for example, might strengthen connection among students with flexible schedules and quiet homes while placing a disproportionate burden on students who work nights or care for children. Research specifically examining the differential effects of camera requirements on students with caring or shift-work commitments is, as far as I was able to establish, limited [source needed]. The present study does not resolve this question, but it offers detailed accounts from participants for whom it was a live concern.

### 2.4.3 What online research leaves open

Taken together, the Community of Inquiry framework and research on sense of community at a distance establish that online learners can and do experience presence and community, that these experiences are shaped by instructional design and facilitation, and that asynchronous, text-based interaction can be a genuine medium for both. What this literature leaves open, at least in the sources reviewed here, is how these experiences are shaped by students' social positions and by the lives they lead outside the program. It also leaves open the relationship between presence and belonging. A student may be present in the sense the framework describes, posting, responding, attending, without feeling that she belongs; a student may feel she belongs without being especially visible. The study's first research question, which asks participants how they themselves describe belonging, was intended in part to examine that relationship.

## 2.5 First-generation students

### 2.5.1 Defining first-generation status

The term first-generation student is used in different ways across the literature. Some definitions count students neither of whose parents attended any form of postsecondary education; others, including the one used in this study, count students neither of whose parents completed a bachelor's degree. These differences matter because they change who is included and can make findings difficult to compare across studies. The definition adopted here (Section 1.8) is relatively inclusive, capturing students whose parents may have attended some college or completed a sub-baccalaureate credential but who did not earn a four-year degree.

It is also important to recognize what the category conceals. First-generation students are not a homogeneous group. Generational status intersects with income, race and ethnicity, immigration history, age, gender and disability, and the experiences of a first-generation student will be shaped by all of these. The present study, with 14 participants, cannot examine these intersections systematically, but it treats first-generation status as one important dimension of participants' experience rather than as a single explanatory variable.

### 2.5.2 What research has found

The research on first-generation undergraduates is extensive. In their synthesis of research on how college affects students, {{pascarella2005|n}} reviewed a substantial body of work examining differences in the experiences and outcomes of first-generation and continuing-generation students. Their synthesis indicated that first-generation students tended to enter college with different levels of preparation and familiarity with academic norms, to have different patterns of involvement during college, and to face greater obstacles to persistence. It also suggested that particular forms of engagement could be especially beneficial for first-generation students, so that the effects of college experiences were not uniform across groups {{pascarella2005}}.

{{engle2008|n}} focused on students who were both low-income and first-generation, arguing that the combination of these characteristics produced compounded barriers to degree completion. They described these students as more likely to be older, to attend part-time, to work while enrolled, to have family responsibilities and to have lower levels of academic preparation, and argued that institutions needed to move beyond simply providing access toward actively supporting success once students had enrolled. Among the institutional practices they identified as promising were those that helped students understand academic expectations, connected them to faculty and peers, and offered structured support during the transition into college.

Several of the characteristics that {{engle2008|n}} associated with low-income, first-generation undergraduates, older age, part-time enrollment, full-time work and caring responsibilities, also describe the majority of the participants in this study (see Chapter 3). This is not because participants were undergraduates, which they were not, but because fully online master's programs attract many students whose lives have this shape. The parallel suggests that some of what has been learned about first-generation undergraduates may be relevant to first-generation graduate students, while also raising the question of what changes once students have completed a first degree.

### 2.5.3 The hidden curriculum

A recurring idea in research on first-generation students is that higher education operates according to rules that are rarely stated, and that students whose families have experience of university are more likely to know these rules before they arrive. These unstated expectations are often described as a hidden curriculum: knowledge about how to communicate with faculty, what office hours are for, how to interpret assignment instructions, what counts as an appropriate question, and how to present oneself as a legitimate member of an academic community. Although the concept is widely invoked in discussions of first-generation students, I was not able to identify, among the sources reviewed for this study, a single authoritative account of the hidden curriculum specific to online graduate study [source needed].

The idea is nevertheless consistent with several of the sources reviewed above. {{engle2008|n}} emphasized the importance of helping first-generation students understand academic expectations, which implies that those expectations are not otherwise transparent to them. {{pascarella2005|n}} reported differences in first-generation students' familiarity with academic norms. And the critiques of integration discussed in Section 2.3.2 point out that the norms into which students are expected to integrate are not neutral but reflect the expectations of those who have historically populated higher education.

There are reasons to think that the hidden curriculum may operate with particular force online. On campus, students can observe how others behave, overhear conversations, and ask a classmate a quick question after class. Many of these informal channels for learning unstated expectations are absent in a fully online program, where interaction is more often scheduled, recorded or written, and where asking a question may mean composing an email to an instructor one has never met. The extent to which this is so, and how first-generation students respond, is part of what the second theme of this study's findings addresses (Chapter 4).

### 2.5.4 First-generation students at the graduate level

The great majority of research on first-generation students concerns undergraduates. Graduate students who are first-generation have received far less attention, and those in fully online graduate programs less still. It might be supposed that the challenges first-generation students face diminish once they have completed a bachelor's degree, since they have by then acquired considerable experience of how universities work. The present study takes a more cautious view. Graduate study brings its own conventions, among them expectations about independent scholarship, citation practice, professional identity and relationships with faculty, and a student who has learned the rules of undergraduate study may find that the rules have changed. Moreover, first-generation graduate students may be navigating these new expectations while also being, within their families, the person who has already gone furthest, with all the pride and pressure that can bring. These possibilities were not assumed in advance, but they informed the interview guide (Appendix A) and are examined in Chapters 4 and 5.

## 2.6 From deficit to asset

### 2.6.1 The limits of deficit framing

Much research on first-generation students, including some of the work reviewed above, has been concerned with documenting the barriers they face and the ways in which they differ from continuing-generation peers. This research has been important in drawing attention to inequalities and in making the case for institutional support. But it has also been criticized for framing first-generation students primarily in terms of what they lack: knowledge, preparation, resources, the right kind of cultural capital. When applied uncritically, such framing can position students as problems to be fixed and can obscure both the knowledge they bring and the role of institutions in producing the difficulties they encounter.

### 2.6.2 Community cultural wealth

The most influential alternative to deficit framing in this area is {{yosso2005|n}}'s account of community cultural wealth. Writing from a critical race theory perspective, Yosso challenged interpretations of cultural capital that treated the knowledge and dispositions of white, middle-class communities as the standard against which others were measured and found wanting. She proposed instead that communities of color possess an array of cultural knowledge, skills, abilities and contacts that are often unrecognized by educational institutions. She identified six forms of capital that together make up community cultural wealth. Aspirational capital refers to the ability to maintain hopes and dreams for the future in the face of real and perceived barriers. Linguistic capital refers to the intellectual and social skills gained through communication in more than one language or style. Familial capital refers to the cultural knowledge nurtured among family, broadly defined, that carries a sense of community history and memory. Social capital refers to networks of people and community resources. Navigational capital refers to skills in maneuvering through social institutions, including those not created with one's community in mind. Resistant capital refers to knowledge and skills developed through behavior that challenges inequality {{yosso2005}}.

Yosso's framework was developed with particular reference to communities of color, and care is needed in applying it to first-generation students as a category, since generational status and race are distinct, though often related, dimensions of experience, and this study did not select or analyze participants by race or ethnicity. I draw on the framework not to claim that its forms of capital apply identically to every participant, but because it offers a way of seeing and naming resources that deficit framings tend to overlook. In particular, the concepts of familial, aspirational and navigational capital proved useful for understanding how participants drew on family, work and community to sustain their study, the focus of RQ3. The fourth theme reported in Chapter 4, in which participants described their study as being "for all of us," is interpreted in part through this lens.

### 2.6.3 Holding both views

An asset-based framing does not require denying that first-generation students face real obstacles. The study attempts to hold both views together: to document the ways in which program practices made belonging harder, without reducing participants to the difficulties they faced. This dual stance is also reflected in the phrasing of RQ3, which asks how participants draw on outside resources rather than whether outside obligations interfered with their study, while leaving room for participants to describe those resources as pressures too.

## 2.7 Learning as participation

A final strand of literature informing the study concerns learning as participation in communities. {{lave1991|n}} proposed that learning is best understood not as the internalization of knowledge by individuals but as a matter of changing participation in communities of practice. Newcomers to a community begin at its periphery, engaging in legitimate but limited forms of participation, and over time move toward fuller participation as they gain access to the community's activities, its members and its ways of talking and acting. On this view, learning is inseparable from becoming a particular kind of person, a member of the community, and the conditions of access to that community shape what and how newcomers learn.

The idea of legitimate peripheral participation is useful for this study in two ways. First, it offers a way of thinking about the movement from being new to a graduate program, unsure of its conventions, to being a recognized member of it. The question of what gives newcomers access, and what keeps them at the margins, is closely related to the question of belonging. A newcomer who is not granted legitimacy, who is treated as not really belonging, may find it difficult to participate more fully, while a newcomer who is recognized as a legitimate participant may find a path inward. Second, it draws attention to the role of more experienced members in making the practices of a community visible to newcomers. Much of what newcomers need to learn is not formally taught but acquired through participation alongside others {{lave1991}}. Where that participation is limited, as it may be in an online program with few informal channels, newcomers may struggle to learn the community's unwritten practices. This connects the situated perspective to the discussion of the hidden curriculum in Section 2.5.3.

The communities of practice perspective also raises the question of which communities matter. Participants in a graduate program may belong not only to the program but to professional communities, workplaces, families and peer groups, some of which overlap with the program and some of which do not. The finding, reported in Chapter 4, that participants built their own peer networks outside the official learning platform can be read in these terms: as students constructing communities in which they could participate more fully than the formal structures allowed, with consequences both for those included and for those left out.

## 2.8 The gap this study addresses

The review has shown that there is a substantial body of work on belonging in higher education, grounded in an account of belonging as a fundamental need {{baumeister1995}} and developed into context-sensitive models of students' sense of belonging {{hurtado1997;strayhorn2019}}. It has shown that integration models of persistence {{tinto1993}} have been productively critiqued for their assumptions about separation from communities of origin and for their limited fit with students whose lives extend well beyond the institution, and that sociocultural accounts of engagement {{kahu2013}} offer a broader frame. It has shown that research on online learning has developed sophisticated accounts of presence and community {{garrison2000;rovai2002}}, and that research on first-generation students has documented both the barriers they face {{engle2008;pascarella2005}} and the assets they bring {{yosso2005}}.

What is largely missing from this literature, at least as reviewed here, is an account that brings these strands together for a specific group: first-generation students in fully online master's programs. Research on belonging has concentrated on residential undergraduates. Research on online community has seldom distinguished first-generation students from others. Research on first-generation students has concentrated on undergraduates and on campus-based study. As a result, there is little detailed evidence about how first-generation graduate students experience belonging when they study entirely online, which program practices they associate with that experience, and how they draw on resources from outside the university to sustain it.

This gap matters for practice as well as for knowledge. Online programs make decisions about synchronous attendance, camera use, discussion design and communication of expectations that are, in effect, decisions about the conditions for belonging. If those decisions rest on assumptions developed for other populations, they may have unintended effects for the students whose belonging is, on the evidence reviewed above, most at stake. The present study responds to this gap by asking first-generation students in three fully online master's programs to describe, in their own terms, what belonging means to them, what in their programs strengthens or weakens it, and what they draw on from beyond the university.

## 2.9 Conceptual framework

The concepts reviewed in this chapter do not combine into a single theory, and the study does not attempt to test one. Instead, they function as sensitizing concepts: ideas that shaped the questions asked and offered vocabulary for interpretation, while leaving room for participants' accounts to exceed or contradict them. This is consistent with the reflexive approach to thematic analysis adopted in the study, in which theory informs interpretation without determining in advance what will be found {{braun2019;braun2022}}. Figure 2.1 summarizes how the concepts relate to one another and to the research questions.

> **Figure 2.1** Conceptual framework for the study. At the center is the student's sense of belonging, understood as a basic need {{baumeister1995}} that is context-dependent and heightened for marginalized students {{strayhorn2019;hurtado1997}} (RQ1). To one side sit program practices and interactions, read through teaching, social and cognitive presence {{garrison2000}}, sense of community at a distance {{rovai2002}} and the hidden curriculum (RQ2). To the other side sit resources and pressures from family, work and community, read through community cultural wealth {{yosso2005}} and a sociocultural view of engagement {{kahu2013}} (RQ3). Arrows run in both directions between each side and the center, and an outer band labelled "movement from periphery toward membership" {{lave1991}} encloses the whole.

Read from the center outward, the framework makes three claims that guided the study. First, belonging is something students experience and can describe, and its meaning may differ from the meanings built into existing frameworks; this justifies asking participants to define it in their own terms (RQ1). Second, belonging is shaped by program practices and interactions, including those that make presence possible or costly and those that make expectations explicit or leave them hidden; this justifies asking participants which practices mattered (RQ2). Third, belonging is shaped by and draws on students' lives beyond the institution, which may serve as resources as well as pressures; this justifies asking participants about family, work and community (RQ3). The outer band reflects the idea that belonging is not static but involves movement, from the edges of a community toward fuller membership, which may be enabled or blocked.

The framework is deliberately open. It does not specify in advance which practices strengthen belonging, which resources matter, or what belonging consists of for this group of students. Those are empirical questions for the study to explore. Chapter 5 returns to the framework to consider which parts the findings support, which they extend, and which they call into question.

## 2.10 Chapter summary

This chapter has reviewed the literature that informs the study. It began with belonging as a fundamental human need and traced its development into a context-dependent construct in higher education, one shaped by institutional climate and especially significant for marginalized students. It examined integration models of persistence and their critiques, and introduced a sociocultural view of engagement that treats students' lives outside the institution as integral rather than peripheral. It reviewed frameworks for understanding presence and community online, noting that they establish the possibility of genuine connection in text-based and asynchronous settings but have seldom attended to differences between groups of students. It reviewed research on first-generation students, including the idea of a hidden curriculum and the scarcity of research at the graduate level, and set deficit framings against the asset-based alternative offered by community cultural wealth. It introduced a situated view of learning as participation. Finally, it identified the gap the study addresses and set out a conceptual framework linking these strands to the three research questions.

Chapter 3 now describes how the study was designed and conducted to address that gap, explaining the choice of a qualitative, interpretivist approach, the selection of participants, the conduct of interviews, and the reflexive thematic analysis through which the four themes were developed.

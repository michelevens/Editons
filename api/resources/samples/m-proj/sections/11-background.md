# Chapter 2: Background and Related Work

This chapter places the project in the literature that informed it. It moves from the problem to the solution space and then to method. Section 2.1 examines medication adherence and the place of follow-up within it. Section 2.2 considers what is known about the effectiveness of mobile health interventions, and Section 2.3 narrows to mobile tools for community health workers. Sections 2.4 to 2.6 review three bodies of design knowledge the artifact had to draw on: designing for constrained infrastructure, designing for users with varied literacy and language, and offline-first or local-first software architecture. Section 2.7 discusses design science and research through design as the methodological foundation, and Section 2.8 reviews the literature on small-sample usability evaluation that underpins Chapter 6. Section 2.9 draws these strands together to identify the gap the project addresses.

The chapter is organized by argument rather than by author. Each section asks what the literature implies for a follow-up tool in Lowmoor, and the implications are collected in Table 2.1 at the end.

## 2.1 Medication adherence and the role of follow-up

Adherence to medication is usually defined as the extent to which a patient's medication-taking corresponds to what was agreed with a prescriber. The concept replaced the older language of "compliance", which placed responsibility on the patient alone. {{osterberg2005|n}} review the causes, measurement and management of non-adherence and show that its causes are multiple and interacting. Patients may forget doses. They may stop because symptoms have resolved, because side effects are unpleasant, or because they doubt the treatment's value. Factors at the level of the health system also matter, including the complexity of regimens, the cost and availability of medicines, and the quality of the relationship between patient and provider. The review emphasizes that non-adherence is often hidden. Clinicians tend to overestimate how well their patients adhere, and the commonly used measures each have limitations {{osterberg2005}}.

Two features of this account matter here. First, chronic, largely asymptomatic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes are especially prone to silent drop-off, because nothing in the patient's daily experience signals that treatment has lapsed. Tuberculosis continuation has a related problem. Patients feel better well before treatment is complete, and stopping early carries consequences for the patient and for others. Second, the interventions the review identifies as promising tend to involve sustained contact and follow-up, not one-off education {{osterberg2005}}. Follow-up is the mechanism through which non-adherence becomes visible early enough to be addressed.

Follow-up, however, is labor. Someone has to know who should be seen, when, and whether the visit happened. In clinic-based systems this knowledge lives in appointment books and patient files. In community-based systems it lives in CHWs' registers and, as Chapter 3 shows, largely in their memories. The project's premise is that the bottleneck in Lowmoor was not the CHWs' willingness to visit but their ability to see, at the point of work, which visits were due. The literature reviewed here supports the importance of follow-up, but it does not show that a better scheduling tool will improve adherence. That would require an outcome study of the kind this thesis does not attempt (see Section 1.5).

## 2.2 The evidence on mobile health interventions

Mobile health, or mHealth, covers a wide range of interventions, from text-message reminders sent to patients to decision-support applications used by clinicians. The evidence base is correspondingly heterogeneous. {{free2013|n}} conducted a systematic review of controlled trials of mobile technology interventions aimed at health care consumers, covering both behavior change and disease management. They found that some interventions, notably text-message support for antiretroviral adherence and for smoking cessation, showed benefit. Across the broader set of trials, however, effects were inconsistent, many studies were small or at risk of bias, and the authors called for higher-quality trials before firm conclusions could be drawn {{free2013}}.

Two lessons follow for this project. First, there is no general warrant for assuming that putting a task on a phone will improve it, and claims for Dose must be calibrated accordingly. Second, the interventions with the clearest support were narrowly targeted at a specific behavior in a specific population. This favors a tool with a narrow purpose, follow-up scheduling and recording, over a broad "digital health record" for CHWs.

The World Health Organization's guideline on digital interventions for health system strengthening takes a similar stance from a policy perspective {{who2019}}. It assesses a set of digital interventions, including digital tracking of clients' health status and services, decision support for health workers, and targeted communication to clients. It recommends several of them only under specified conditions and with attention to the surrounding health system. Among the considerations it raises are the infrastructure needed to sustain the tools, the acceptability of digital tools to health workers, the risk of adding to their workload, and the protection of patient data and privacy {{who2019}}. These considerations map directly onto concerns that recur in Chapter 3. CHWs feared that a phone would add work rather than remove it, and supervisors were concerned about what would happen to patient information on a lost phone.

The WHO guideline also discusses tracking combined with reminders or decision support rather than tracking alone. Dose sits at the modest end of this range. It tracks follow-up status and presents what is due, but it does not tell the CHW what to do clinically. That choice was made deliberately, for reasons of scope (Section 1.5) and because decision support carries clinical governance obligations that a master's project could not responsibly take on.

## 2.3 Mobile tools for community health workers

A substantial body of work has explored mobile tools designed for health workers rather than patients. {{derenzi2011|n}} review mobile phone tools for field-based health care workers in low-income countries and draw design lessons from a range of deployments. Three of their observations shaped this project.

The first is that tools succeed when they support work health workers already do, rather than imposing new data collection whose benefit flows elsewhere. Systems built primarily to extract reports for higher levels of the health system tend to be experienced as a burden. Systems that help workers with their own tasks, such as knowing whom to visit or what to do next, are more readily adopted {{derenzi2011}}. Dose was framed from the outset as a tool for the CHW's own day, with supervisor reporting (R8 in Chapter 3) as a secondary benefit.

The second is that the device and network context is part of the design, not a deployment detail. {{derenzi2011|n}} describe tools that had to cope with intermittent connectivity, limited power and devices with small screens and modest processing capacity. They note that approaches which store data on the phone and transfer it later are common for this reason. This is consistent with the local-first architecture discussed in Section 2.6.

The third is that the role of supervision matters. Mobile tools change what supervisors can see, and that visibility can be used to support workers or to police them. Designers need to think about which information flows upward and in what form {{derenzi2011}}. This point informed the decision, discussed in Chapter 4, to give supervisors a weekly summary of follow-up activity rather than a live view of each CHW's movements.

Prior work on CHW tools focuses largely on data collection and decision support, with less attention to the specific problem of surfacing due and overdue obligations from a longitudinal register, which is the core of Dose. This is not to claim that no such tools exist. Scheduling and reminder features appear in various deployed systems [source needed]. Their design rationale, however, is rarely reported in a form that others can reuse, and that is the kind of knowledge this thesis aims to articulate.

## 2.4 Designing for constrained infrastructure

{{brewer2005|n}} make the case that technology for developing regions is a research area in its own right, because the assumptions built into mainstream computing often fail there. They identify connectivity, power and cost as the central constraints. Network access may be intermittent or absent. Power may be unreliable, so battery life becomes a design concern. And the cost of devices, connectivity and maintenance falls on users and institutions with little margin. They argue that systems for these settings should be designed around these constraints from the start rather than adapted afterwards {{brewer2005}}.

Each of these constraints appears in the Lowmoor requirements. Intermittent connectivity motivates R1 (work fully offline) and R4 (sync whenever any connection appears, without losing data in conflicts). Cost motivates R10 (no SMS cost to the CHW). Synchronizing by SMS was an option I considered and rejected, because each message would be charged to a worker's personal or shared credit. Device constraints motivate R7 (run on Android phones with 1 GB of RAM, with an installation package under 15 MB). Power constraints motivate R12 (no constant GPS or other battery-heavy background activity).

{{brewer2005|n}} also caution against treating these settings as simply lagging and waiting for infrastructure to catch up. Their argument implies that a tool designed for intermittent connectivity is not a stopgap but a reasonable long-term design, because coverage gaps inside buildings and in dense or remote areas are likely to persist. This is a relevant stance for an artifact like Dose. It suggests that offline operation should be treated as the normal case, with connectivity as an occasional bonus, rather than as an error state to be recovered from. Chapter 4 shows how this stance shaped the "quiet sync" design.

## 2.5 Literacy, language and accessibility

CHWs are literate. They keep written registers and complete reports. Their literacy, however, varies, and they work across languages. In Lowmoor, registers were kept in English, conversations with patients were mostly in Kiswahili, and several CHWs said they found long English text on a phone screen tiring. Two strands of literature bear on this.

The first concerns interfaces for users with limited literacy. {{medhi2007|n}} studied text-free interfaces for illiterate and semi-literate users and derived design principles from their field studies. These include using graphical representations in place of text, supporting the interface with voice, and keeping whatever text remains minimal. Their participants generally responded better to interfaces that did not depend on reading than to text-based equivalents. They also found that abstract icons were not automatically understood, and that representations needed to be tested with the intended users {{medhi2007}}. The CHWs in this project were not semi-literate, so a fully text-free interface would have been inappropriate and possibly patronizing. The broader lesson still transferred: pair icons with short text, keep labels brief, and verify icon meaning with users rather than assuming it. This became R5, which calls for icons with short text and a language toggle between English and Kiswahili.

The second strand concerns accessibility. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1 set out success criteria for making web content perceivable, operable, understandable and robust {{w3c2018}}. Several of them are directly relevant to a mobile tool used outdoors on small, inexpensive screens. These include minimum contrast ratios between text and background, the ability to resize text without loss of content, and a minimum target size for pointer inputs at the enhanced level {{w3c2018}}. CHWs used Dose in bright sunlight, often while standing, sometimes holding a bag or a register in the other hand. Those conditions push even users without impairments toward the situations accessibility guidelines anticipate. R6, which calls for large touch targets and sufficient contrast, adopts WCAG 2.1 as its reference, and Dose was built as a web application, so the guidelines apply directly.

Language also interacts with layout. Kiswahili labels are often longer than their English equivalents. A design that fits English text into a fixed button width may truncate the translation. Chapter 6 reports exactly this problem as one of the three severe usability issues, which suggests that the literature's general advice to test with the intended languages deserves more emphasis than I gave it in iteration 2.

## 2.6 Offline-first and local-first software

Most mobile applications that hold shared data treat the server as the authoritative copy and the device as a cache. When the network is unavailable, such applications degrade: they show stale data, queue changes uncertainly, or refuse to work. In a setting like Lowmoor, where being offline is the normal state, this architecture inverts the priority the work requires.

{{kleppmann2019|n}} articulate an alternative they call local-first software. In this approach the primary copy of a user's data lives on the user's device, and synchronization with other devices and servers happens in the background when possible. They propose a set of ideals for such software. It should be fast because it does not wait on the network. It should work offline. It should support collaboration across devices. It should give users ownership and control of their data, and it should remain usable for the long term even if the service behind it disappears. They identify conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs) as a promising technical foundation. These are data structures whose concurrent updates can be merged automatically and deterministically {{kleppmann2019}}.

Not all of these ideals apply to Dose. The data belong to a health service, not to the individual CHW, so "ownership" takes a different form. Collaboration is limited to occasional transfers of patients between CHWs and supervisors' read access. The core of the local-first position nonetheless fits the problem closely. The CHW must be able to see and update her list without a connection. Changes must be merged safely when a connection appears, and synchronization should never block the work in hand. These became R1 and R4.

The local-first literature also raises a design question it does not fully answer: how should synchronization state be presented to the user? If the device is the primary copy, then "not yet synchronized" is not an error, and an interface that treats it as one sends a misleading message. Yet supervisors and auditors need confidence that records do eventually reach the server. {{kleppmann2019|n}} discuss the need for user interfaces that make sync state and conflicts understandable, but they leave the design of those interfaces open. This project's "quiet sync" badge (Chapter 4) is one answer, and the evaluation in Chapter 6 shows that it was not yet a fully successful one.

Design principles from the general interaction design literature informed this question too. {{norman2013|n}} emphasizes feedback, meaning that a system should communicate the result of an action promptly and informatively. He also stresses signifiers, which communicate what actions are possible. {{shneiderman2016|n}} include among their golden rules offering informative feedback, designing dialogs that yield a sense of closure, permitting easy reversal of actions, and keeping the user in control. Applied to synchronization, these principles pull in different directions. Informative feedback argues for showing sync state clearly, while keeping users in control and not interrupting their work argues against modal sync messages. The design of Dose had to strike a balance between them (Chapter 4).

## 2.7 Design science and research through design

Section 1.4 introduced the methodological frame. This section reviews the literature behind it in more detail, because a design project has to justify how building something produces knowledge.

{{hevner2004|n}} set out guidelines for design science research in information systems. Design science must produce a viable artifact that addresses a relevant problem. The artifact's utility must be demonstrated through rigorous evaluation, and the research must make a contribution beyond the artifact. Design is understood as a search process, and the results must be communicated to both technical and managerial audiences {{hevner2004}}. Their three-cycle view, in which a relevance cycle connects to the environment, a rigor cycle to the knowledge base and a design cycle iterates between them, offers a way of showing where each part of a project draws its warrant. In this thesis, Chapter 3 is the main expression of the relevance cycle and this chapter of the rigor cycle, while Chapters 4 to 6 constitute the design cycle.

{{peffers2007|n}} built on this and related work to propose the Design Science Research Methodology. DSRM is a process model with six activities and several possible entry points, depending on whether a project starts from a problem, an objective, a design or an existing artifact. It makes the sequence of work explicit (Table 1.1) but describes what to do more than how designing produces knowledge.

That gap is partly addressed by the HCI literature on research through design. {{zimmerman2007|n}} argue that interaction design research can make a distinctive contribution by designing artifacts that embody a proposal for what the preferred state of a problem situation should be. They suggest criteria for evaluating such work: the process should be documented well enough to be reproduced, the contribution should be novel, the work should be relevant to a real situation, and the resulting knowledge should be extensible to future work {{zimmerman2007}}. The design rationale tables in Chapter 4 respond to the first of these criteria, and the design knowledge statements in Chapter 7 respond to the last.

{{schon1983|n}}'s account of reflective practice adds a view of the designer's own learning. Schön distinguishes reflection-in-action, the thinking that happens while doing and that reshapes the work as it proceeds, from reflection-on-action, the retrospective analysis that follows. He describes design as a conversation in which the designer frames a problem, acts, and attends to the unexpected results of the action. This framing is especially apt for prototype iterations, in which each round of testing reframes the problem as much as it tests the solution. The shift from a calendar-based home screen to a list-first one, described in Chapter 4, is an example.

## 2.8 Evaluating usability with small samples

The evaluation in Chapter 6 is a task-based usability study with twelve participants. Such a study raises questions about what can be concluded from a small sample, and the literature reviewed here provides the basis for answering them.

{{lazar2017|n}} describe usability testing as a family of methods in which representative users attempt representative tasks while researchers observe. Measures typically include task completion, time on task, errors and subjective satisfaction, often accompanied by think-aloud protocols that make users' reasoning visible. They distinguish formative testing, which aims to find problems to fix, from summative testing, which aims to measure performance against a benchmark. They note that the two call for different sample sizes and analyses {{lazar2017}}. The study in Chapter 6 is primarily formative but reports summative measures with appropriate caution.

On sample size for formative testing, {{nielsen1993b|n}} model the discovery of usability problems as a process with diminishing returns. Each additional participant tends to reveal fewer new problems than the last, so small tests repeated across iterations find more problems overall than a single large test. {{nielsen1993|n}}'s broader account of usability engineering places this within an iterative lifecycle in which inspection methods such as heuristic evaluation complement testing with users. That lifecycle was the model for the three iterations described in Chapter 4.

For summative measures, {{sauro2016|n}} provide practical statistics for small-sample user research. Two of their recommendations are used in Chapter 6. The first is to report confidence intervals for completion rates rather than bare proportions, using the adjusted-Wald method, which behaves well at small sample sizes and at proportions near 0 or 1. The second is to interpret System Usability Scale scores against normative data rather than as absolute percentages. They report an average SUS score of around 68 across a large body of studies {{sauro2016}}.

The System Usability Scale itself is a ten-item questionnaire that yields a single score from 0 to 100. {{brooke2013|n}} reviews its origins and its subsequent use, noting that it was designed as a fast, low-effort instrument, yet proved robust and became widely adopted. He cautions that the score is not a percentage and should be interpreted relative to other systems. {{bangor2008|n}} analyzed a large set of SUS administrations and proposed an adjective rating scale that associates score ranges with descriptions such as "good" and "excellent". This gives a vocabulary for interpreting a score that is more meaningful to non-specialists than the number alone {{bangor2008}}. Both sources are used in Chapter 6 to interpret Dose's mean score.

*Note.* Requirement identifiers refer to the specification developed in Chapter 3.

Read together, these strands support three observations. First, follow-up is the point at which non-adherence can be noticed, but the tools CHWs have for follow-up, paper registers, do not surface who is due. Second, the literature on mobile CHW tools and on constrained settings gives clear general guidance: work offline, keep costs off the worker, support the worker's own tasks, and design for low-end devices. It offers much less specific guidance on how the interface of an offline-first tool should present obligations and synchronization state to its users. Third, the local-first literature supplies an architecture suited to the problem, but it leaves the user-facing design of sync largely open.

The gap this project addresses therefore lies at the intersection of these strands. It is the design, and the reported design rationale, of an offline-first follow-up tool for CHWs that turns a longitudinal register into a list of present obligations and handles synchronization without disrupting work. The gap is a modest one, appropriate to a master's project. It is a gap in articulated design knowledge for a specific class of tool, not in fundamental theory.

The next chapter begins the work of closing it. It returns to Lowmoor and reports what six CHWs and two supervisors showed and told me about follow-up as it is actually done.

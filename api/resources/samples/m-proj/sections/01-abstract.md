# Abstract

Community health workers (CHWs) in Lowmoor District, a peri-urban area, follow up patients on long-term medication for hypertension, diabetes and tuberculosis continuation using paper registers. Missed follow-ups often go unnoticed, connectivity is intermittent, and the workers' phones are low-end Android devices running on shared data bundles. This thesis reports a design science project that designed, built and evaluated Dose, an offline-first mobile tool that shows CHWs who is due or overdue for follow-up and lets them record visits without a connection.

Following the Design Science Research Methodology, the project began with contextual inquiry: six shadowing days with six CHWs and interviews with two supervisors, analyzed through affinity diagramming and thematic analysis. This produced twelve prioritized requirements (R1–R12). Three design iterations followed, moving from paper prototypes to a clickable prototype and then to a working progressive web app packaged as an 11.8 MB Android APK. The app stores data locally in encrypted form and synchronizes when any connection appears, resolving conflicts field by field. Twelve different CHWs evaluated the prototype in a simulated field day using five think-aloud tasks. Completion rates ranged from 9/12 (rescheduling a follow-up) to 12/12 (finding overdue patients; switching language). The median time to record a visit offline was 38 seconds, and the mean System Usability Scale score was 78.3 (SD 9.6). The study identified 17 usability problems, three of them severe. Of the twelve requirements, nine were met, two were partially met, and one was not evaluated in the field.

The project contributes a working artifact and two pieces of transferable design knowledge, "list-first follow-up" and "quiet sync", for health tools used in intermittently connected settings. The evaluation was simulated and short, and it measured no clinical outcomes. A field pilot is the necessary next step.

**Keywords:** community health workers, medication adherence, offline-first, design science research, usability evaluation, mHealth

# Chapter 7: Reflection and Conclusion

This chapter draws the thesis together. Section 7.1 states how each research question has been answered. Section 7.2 sets out what the project contributes, separating the artifact from the design knowledge it embodies. Section 7.3 reflects on the design science process, Section 7.4 states the limits of the work, Section 7.5 proposes next steps, and Section 7.6 offers a short personal reflection. The final section summarizes the thesis.

## 7.1 Answering the research questions

**RQ1** asked what requirements CHWs and supervisors have for a follow-up tool in an intermittently connected setting. Chapter 3 answered it through six days of contextual inquiry with six CHWs and interviews with two supervisors, producing twelve requirements, R1–R12. The requirements that proved most consequential were not the obvious functional ones but those that encoded the conditions of the work: that the tool must work fully offline (R1), cost the CHW nothing in SMS (R10), run on a 1 GB phone (R7) and leave an auditable paper trail (R11).

**RQ2** asked how those requirements could be embodied in an offline-first mobile design. Chapters 4 and 5 answered it constructively, through three design iterations and a working prototype: a list-first home screen, a four-field visit form that saves in three taps, a quiet sync badge, an "EN / SW" header toggle, and a local-first architecture in which the phone holds the primary copy of the data, with append-only visit logs and encryption at rest. The design rationale tables in Chapter 4 link each of these decisions to a requirement and to evidence.

**RQ3** asked how usable and useful the prototype is for CHWs in simulated field tasks. Chapter 6 showed that twelve CHWs new to Dose completed the core tasks with high success, recorded a visit offline in a median of 38 seconds, and gave a mean SUS score of 78.3 (SD 9.6), in the "good" range. Rescheduling (9/12) and syncing (10/12) were weaker, for identifiable reasons. Nine requirements were met, two partially (R4, R8) and one not evaluated (R12). The answer to RQ3 is therefore that Dose is usable in simulation and judged useful by its intended users, with three severe problems to resolve before a field pilot. Whether it is useful in the stronger sense of changing follow-up practice remains open.

## 7.2 Contributions

{{hevner2004|n}} ask that design science research contribute something beyond the artifact itself, and {{zimmerman2007|n}} argue that research through design should produce knowledge that others can extend. I claim two contributions of this kind, stated as provisional design principles rather than proven laws.

The first is **list-first follow-up**. Tools for health workers often organize the interface around records (patients, forms, registers) because that is how the underlying data are stored {{derenzi2011}}. Dose instead organizes its home screen around obligations: who is overdue, who is due today. The evaluation suggests this reframing is what CHWs valued most, and it addresses directly the weakness of the paper register identified in Chapter 1, which holds due dates without surfacing them. The principle is that a follow-up tool's first screen should answer "whom must I see?" rather than "what data do I hold?".

The second is **quiet sync**, revised by the evaluation. Chapter 4 proposed that synchronization should be a background fact shown by a small, non-blocking indicator, consistent with local-first thinking in which the network is an opportunity rather than a precondition {{kleppmann2019}}. Chapter 6 confirmed the non-blocking part but showed that a count on a cloud icon was read as an alert. The refined principle is that quiet sync status should describe state in the user's terms ("waiting to send", "sent") and should be paired with an explicit confirmation of delivery, because in settings where data costs fall on the worker, confidence that a record has arrived is part of what sync is for.

A third, smaller contribution is methodological: the requirements traceability table in Chapter 6 shows how a master's-scale project can make the link between requirements and evaluation explicit, which the Design Science Research Methodology recommends but which is easy to leave implicit {{peffers2007}}.

## 7.3 Reflecting on the process

{{schon1983|n}} describes design as a reflective conversation with the situation, in which each move the designer makes is answered by the situation in ways that reframe the problem. That description fits the project better than the linear sequence of a methodology diagram. The paper prototypes answered back about the visit form, which had nine fields until CHWs skipped most of them. The sync dialog answered back in iteration 2, when participants cancelled it to protect their data bundles. The date picker answered back only in the evaluation, and it did so because it was the one component I had adopted without putting it through the same conversation. The lesson I take is that the design cycle must cover every component that a user touches in a core task, including the defaults a platform supplies.

Against the criteria {{zimmerman2007|n}} propose, the process is documented in enough detail to be reproduced, the artifact combines known techniques in a way that is new for this setting, and its relevance is grounded in observed work. Extensibility is the weakest criterion: the principles of Section 7.2 are derived from one district and one artifact.

## 7.4 Limitations

The evaluation was simulated, short and small. It took place in halls rather than households, lasted about 50 minutes per participant, and involved twelve CHWs, so its findings are best read as identifying problems and plausible strengths, not as estimates of performance in practice. No clinical outcome was measured. Medication non-adherence has many causes beyond missed follow-up {{osterberg2005}}, and evidence that mobile tools improve health outcomes is mixed {{free2013}}; nothing in this thesis shows that Dose would reduce missed follow-ups, still less improve adherence. Battery use (R12) was not measured. The requirements came from one district, and the constraints of connectivity, power and cost vary between settings {{brewer2005}}. Finally, I designed, built and evaluated the artifact myself, which brings the risk that my familiarity shaped the tasks; using a separate group of CHWs for evaluation reduced, but did not remove, that risk.

## 7.5 Next steps

The immediate next step is a fourth iteration addressing the three severe problems: a reschedule control built around the common follow-up intervals rather than a calendar, a sync indicator that uses words and confirms delivery, and layouts tested with Kiswahili strings. The conflict view designed in Chapter 4 should be built, and the weekly summary should add the per-CHW breakdown both supervisors requested. After that, a field pilot of several months with a small number of CHWs would allow battery use, sync reliability and data quality to be measured under real conditions. Only then would it be reasonable to design an outcome study comparing follow-up rates against the paper register, informed by WHO guidance on digital interventions that support health workers {{who2019}}. Integration with district health information systems, excluded from this project, would also need to be addressed before any wider use. Further design work could test whether more of the interface can rely on icons and less on text {{medhi2007}}.

## 7.6 Personal reflection

I began the project expecting the hard problems to be technical, chiefly sync and encryption on a constrained phone. Those were demanding, but the decisions that most shaped Dose came from watching CHWs work: the realization that the register is good at storing and bad at reminding, and that a dialog asking to send data reads as a request to spend someone's money. I also learned to treat a negative evaluation result as a finding rather than a failure. The sync badge problem was uncomfortable to report, since quiet sync was the idea I was most attached to, but it is the result from which the most transferable knowledge came.

## 7.7 Conclusion

This thesis set out to design, build and evaluate a tool that helps community health workers see who is due or overdue for medication follow-up and record visits without a network connection. Through contextual inquiry, three design iterations and a working offline-first prototype, it produced twelve requirements, an artifact that met nine of them in full, and a usability evaluation in which CHWs rated the prototype as good and completed its core tasks with high success. Its most durable contribution is likely to be the two design principles, list-first follow-up and quiet sync with explicit delivery confirmation, which other designers working in intermittently connected settings can test and extend.

# Abstract

This thesis is a history of the Kestrel Bay Teachers' Credit Union (KBTCU), a small cooperative savings and loan society organized by eleven teachers in a Great Lakes mill-and-port city in October 1928 and followed here to the eve of American entry into the Second World War. It asks who joined and borrowed and for what purposes, how the union survived the years 1930 to 1933 when the city's largest local bank failed, what changed when it took a federal charter in 1935, and what the case reveals about "character" as a basis for lending.

Drawing on the union's board and credit-committee minutes, a loan register of 2,516 entries, treasurer's reports, the members' newsletter *The Thrift Bell*, the city newspaper and an oral-history interview, the thesis argues that the union came through the banking collapse not because it was better capitalized than the banks around it but because it lent on character: on members' detailed knowledge of one another, enforced through the credit committee and the shared life of the school building. That moral economy of credit carried the union through the salary-warrant crisis of 1932–33, the suspension of Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust in February 1933 and the national bank holiday, with loan losses in 1932–1934 held to $812. The federal charter of 1935 formalized and partly displaced it, substituting written rules and examiners' standards for personal judgment as membership more than doubled. By 1941 lending had turned decisively toward automobiles and household appliances, the kind of borrowing the movement's founders had hoped to keep members away from.

The case refines national histories of the credit union movement and cultural histories of consumer credit by showing, at the level of daily lending decisions, how one institution translated the movement's ideals into practice under stress, and how those ideals were reshaped by success.

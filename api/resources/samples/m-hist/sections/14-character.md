# Chapter 5: Character, Credit and the Consumer Turn, 1937–1941

In the autumn of 1937 the credit committee of the Kestrel Bay Teachers' Credit Union approved, for the first time, a loan whose stated purpose was the purchase of a new automobile outright rather than the repair of an old one or the settlement of a dealer's contract already in arrears. The borrower was a science teacher at Lakeside High School; the sum was $310, about five times the average loan of the depression years; and the security was a chattel mortgage on the car together with the signatures of two co-makers. Frances Delacroix, who had chaired the committee since 1931, recorded the decision in a single line, without the paragraph of explanation that had accompanied almost every unusual loan in the crisis years.[^d1] The brevity is itself the evidence. By the late 1930s a kind of borrowing that the union's founders had regarded as precisely the temptation a credit union existed to forestall had become routine enough to need no defense.

This chapter addresses the third and fourth of the research questions set out in Chapter 1: what changed after the federal charter, and what the Kestrel Bay case says about "character" as the basis for credit. Chapter 4 showed how the charter of 18 June 1935 brought the union under federal supervision, standardized its forms and, with the widening of the common bond in 1936, opened membership to the board of education's janitors, clerks and city-hall staff. Here I follow the consequences into the loan register and the credit committee's minutes. I argue that the union's lending turned decisively toward consumer durables in these years, and that this turn was made possible by, and in turn accelerated, a shift in what "character" meant at the committee table: from an assessment grounded in members' personal knowledge of one another, exercised in the school building, to an assessment increasingly mediated by paper, collateral and the regularity of a salary. The moral economy of credit described in Chapters 2 and 3 did not disappear. It was rather put to new uses, and the language of thrift that had once defined the union against the instalment seller came, by 1941, to describe the disciplined repayment of instalment debt itself.

## 5.1 The shape of lending after the charter

### 5.1.1 What members borrowed for

The loan register records a stated purpose for each of its 2,516 entries, entered by the treasurer from the member's application. The categories are the treasurer's own, and their boundaries were not always stable; Appendix A discusses the problems of coding them and the change of hand in the register in 1939. Grouped into the three periods used throughout this thesis, they nonetheless show a change in the composition of borrowing that is too large to be an artifact of classification. Table 5.1 sets out the shares.

**Table 5.1** Loan purposes by period (share of loans made, %)

| Purpose | 1929–1932 | 1933–1936 | 1937–1941 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medical and dental | 27 | 24 | 15 |
| Paying off other debts (incl. small-loan companies) | 22 | 26 | 12 |
| Family support / funerals | 14 | 16 | 8 |
| Home repairs and fuel | 13 | 12 | 11 |
| Education and summer courses | 11 | 8 | 7 |
| Automobile purchase or repair | 6 | 7 | 29 |
| Household appliances and furniture | 4 | 4 | 15 |
| Other | 3 | 3 | 3 |

*Note.* Calculated from the loan register, KBTCU Records, boxes 4–5 (n = 514, 736 and 1,266 loans in the three periods). Columns may not sum to 100 because of rounding.

Two features of Table 5.1 deserve emphasis. The first is the stability of the first two periods. Between 1929 and 1936 the union lent overwhelmingly for "provident" purposes: medical bills, debts owed elsewhere, the support of relatives and burials, fuel and house repairs. These four categories accounted for more than three-quarters of all loans in both periods; the depression altered their proportions but not their character. The second feature is the abruptness of the change after 1936. Loans for automobiles rose from 7 to 29 percent of all loans made, and loans for appliances and furniture from 4 to 15 percent. Together, durable goods went from roughly one loan in ten to more than four in ten. Every provident category lost ground, though "home repairs and fuel" held almost steady, a reminder that the Kestrel Bay winter did not change with the credit market.

The change was not simply a matter of new borrowers wanting new things. The widened common bond brought in more men, who borrowed for automobiles more often than women did, yet the turn is visible among the teachers too. Of the automobile loans made in 1937–1941, I estimate from the register's occupational entries that well over half went to teachers, the majority of them women.[^d2] Nor was it confined to recent recruits: automobile and appliance loans went to members who had joined in every year from 1928, including several founders listed in Table 2.1.

The mean loan fell from $64 in 1929–1932 to $58 in 1933–1936, as members borrowed smaller sums to bridge the gaps left by salary cuts and the warrant period discussed in Chapter 3, and then more than doubled to $121 in 1937–1941. The aggregate figures in Appendix B tell the same story from another angle. Loans outstanding at year-end rose from $24,110 in 1937 to $43,310 in 1941, an increase of nearly 80 percent, while membership grew from 371 to 532, an increase of about 43 percent. Each member, on average, owed the union considerably more at the end of the period than at its beginning. Shares grew in step, from $27,300 to $47,900, and the ratio of loans outstanding to shares stayed within a narrow band of roughly 0.86 to 0.90 throughout. The union lent more, but not beyond its means.

Larger loans meant longer terms. The founders had preferred loans repaid within ten months, roughly a school year, so that the unpaid summer would not interrupt repayment. From 1937 the minutes record a growing number of loans over fifteen and eighteen months, and in 1939 the board formally extended the maximum term for loans secured by a chattel mortgage to twenty-four months.[^d3] The summer problem was handled by a device that tells us a good deal about how the union now understood its members: borrowers were encouraged to deposit a fixed sum in shares each month during the school year, against which the summer payments could be drawn. Thrift, in this arrangement, had become a technique for servicing debt.

## 5.2 Automobiles, refrigerators and the instalment rival

The founders of the Kestrel Bay union would not have been surprised that their members wanted cars. What would have troubled them was that members should borrow to buy them. The Credit Union National Extension Bureau's literature, which Agnes Thornquist read closely and quoted in the early numbers of *The Thrift Bell*, presented the credit union as a defense against two predators: the loan shark who lent at ruinous rates to the desperate, and the instalment seller who lent at concealed rates to the merely tempted.[^d4] The movement's own leaders framed their work as a campaign for economic democracy that would give wage earners an honest alternative to both.{{bergengren1952}} In the union's first years, as Chapter 2 showed, a loan to pay off a dealer's contract in arrears was acceptable because it rescued a member from a worse creditor; a loan to buy a radio was not.

The economic historiography makes clear why this position was difficult to hold for long. The decade before the union's founding had seen a genuine revolution in household purchases of durable goods, driven, on Olney's account, less by changes in household tastes than by manufacturers' advertising and by the spread of instalment credit through sales finance companies.{{olney1991}} Calder's cultural history places the same development within a longer story: Americans had always borrowed, but between 1890 and 1940 consumer credit was institutionalized and made respectable, with automobile instalment credit as the decisive innovation of the 1920s.{{calder1999}} Contemporary investigators of the Russell Sage Foundation, writing in the late 1930s, treated consumer credit as a significant factor in economic fluctuation and compiled year-end estimates of its outstanding volume by type, a project that presupposed instalment debt was now a permanent feature of household finance rather than an aberration.{{nugent1939}} By 1937, in other words, the instalment contract had long been part of ordinary life in the city. What changed after 1936 was that members increasingly brought such contracts to the union.

The register makes this visible in an unexpected way. Many of the loans entered as "automobile" in 1937 and 1938 are annotated with the name of a local dealer or finance company, and the minutes show that the committee frequently approved them on the understanding that the member would use the proceeds to pay off a dealer's contract in full and take title to the car.[^d5] The union was, in effect, refinancing instalment debt at its own rate of 1 percent per month on the unpaid balance, which the *Thrift Bell* regularly contrasted with the higher effective charges of the finance companies. A notice in the newsletter's issue of March 1938 put the case plainly:

> If you are buying a car on the dealer's plan, take your contract and a pencil to Miss Thornquist on any Tuesday or Friday. She will show you what you are paying, and what you would pay us. Most members who have done the sum have not gone back to the dealer.[^d6]

The appeal was to arithmetic, and through arithmetic to self-respect: the thrifty member was now the one who financed a car intelligently, not the one who went without. In this the union reproduced, on a small scale, the argument Calder makes about the discipline of instalment repayment. Regular payments imposed their own order on household budgets, and the union's officers seem to have come to see that order as continuous with, rather than opposed to, the habits of saving they had tried to instill.{{calder1999}}

Appliances followed the same path. Entries for "refrigerator," "washer," "range" and "furniture" cluster in the spring and early summer, when the city's merchants ran their seasonal promotions. Ruth Ellison, who was thirteen in 1937, remembered her family's first electric refrigerator as a credit union purchase, and remembered too that her mother had made a point of telling the neighbors so:

> She didn't want anybody thinking we'd bought it on time from the store. It was the credit union, and the credit union was different. That was how she put it. It was the same payments every month, but it was different.[^d7]

The memory needs the caution any recollection of childhood requires, and Ellison was recalling her mother's account rather than the transaction. It nonetheless captures a distinction the documents only imply: borrowing from the union carried a moral standing that borrowing from the store did not, even when the object and the monthly payment were the same. The reputation the union had built in the crisis years by refusing to lend for indulgence now lent respectability to the very borrowing it had once refused.

## 5.3 Character on paper

### 5.3.1 The committee after the charter

What made the consumer turn possible inside the union was a change in how the credit committee did its work. In the years examined in Chapters 2 and 3, the committee's judgments rested on what its members knew. Delacroix and her colleagues taught in the same buildings as the applicants, sat in the same staff rooms, attended the same funerals. The minutes from 1931 to 1933 are full of references to that knowledge: a member was "steady," had "nursed her mother through the winter," had "always paid the milkman." Applications were short, often a single sheet with a purpose and a sum; the committee's deliberation filled the gap between what the form recorded and what the members knew.[^d8]

The federal charter did not abolish this practice, but it changed its texture. The examiner's visits described in Chapter 4 required written applications, statements of salary and other obligations, and documentation of security. The forms adopted in 1936 asked what the committee had once answered from memory: length of employment, dependents, other debts, the names and salaries of co-makers. By 1938 the typical loan file in the surviving sample contains three or four documents where a 1931 file contained one.[^d9]

At first the forms supplemented personal knowledge; Delacroix's minutes in 1937 still record character in her own idiom alongside the figures. Her retirement as chair in 1938 marks a sharper break. Her successors' minutes are shorter and formulaic, citing salary and co-makers as the grounds for approval. The phrase "approved on the usual security" first appears in October 1938 and is standard within a year.[^d10] Thornquist's retirement as treasurer in 1939, marked by a change of hand in the loan register, completes the passage from the founding generation to administrators who had joined the union rather than built it.

### 5.3.2 Collateral, co-makers and the salary

The change of idiom reflected a change of substance. Larger loans for cars and appliances could be secured by the goods themselves, and from 1937 the committee took chattel mortgages routinely on automobile loans above $100. Co-makers, always used for larger loans, were now required on almost all of them. Above all, the committee came to rely on the regularity of a public-sector salary. In the crisis years the salary had been the problem: cut, delayed and in 1932–33 partly paid in warrants, it was precisely what the committee could not take for granted. By the late 1930s the board of education's payroll had become the most dependable thing about a member, and the committee's minutes treat it as such. A teacher, a janitor or a clerk with several years on the payroll was a good risk because the payroll was a good risk.

The delinquency figures suggest that this approach worked. The share of loans more than two months delinquent fell from its peak of 9.4 percent in 1933 to 2.9 percent in 1937, rose to 3.6 percent in 1938, a year of renewed recession nationally, and fell again to 2.0 percent by 1941.{{kennedy1999}} Of the $1,284 written off in the union's first fourteen years, $812 was lost in 1932–1934; the five years of the consumer turn, with far larger sums outstanding, account for only a small part of the remainder. The union lent more, for longer and for goods its founders distrusted, and lost less.

It would be easy to read this as modernization, an informal and personal system of judgment giving way to a rational, impersonal one. The evidence supports a more qualified reading. In 1931–1933 personal knowledge had been the only basis on which the union could lend at all, since the salary that later sustained lending had lost its meaning. What the late 1930s show is not the triumph of the form over the person but a division of labor between them. Collateral and salary did the work of judging ordinary loans, while character, in the older sense, was reserved for the exceptions: the hardship loan, the request for an extension, the member who had fallen behind. The committee's minutes from 1939 to 1941 still contain long discussions, in the older style, of members in trouble. They contain almost none of members buying cars.

## 5.4 The small-loan company and the moral map of credit

One of the clearest changes in Table 5.1 is the fall in loans to pay off other debts, from 26 percent in 1933–1936 to 12 percent in 1937–1941. Behind that category lay, more often than any other creditor, the licensed small-loan companies of Kestrel Bay. Contemporary investigators for the Russell Sage Foundation distinguished such licensed lending to wage earners from the unregulated "loan shark" trade it was meant to suppress, and debated the maximum rate the law should permit.{{robinson1935}} The Uniform Small Loan Law, as Carruthers, Guinnane and Lee show, sought to bring honest capital to poor borrowers by licensing lenders to charge above the usury ceiling, so that a legal market would drive out an illegal one.{{carruthers2012}}

The Kestrel Bay union stood in an ambiguous relation to this reform. Its officers shared the reformers' hostility to the loan shark, and the *Thrift Bell* printed accounts of teachers ensnared by lenders operating outside the law.[^d11] But they regarded the licensed company with almost equal suspicion: its rates, though legal, were too high, and its collection methods, which included notifying employers, threatened a teacher's standing. Thornquist's annual report for 1934 described the union's debt-consolidation loans as a rescue of members "from the companies, licensed and unlicensed alike."[^d12] The distinction the reformers drew between legitimate and illegitimate lenders mattered less to her than the distinction between lenders who knew the borrower and lenders who did not.

The fall in debt-consolidation loans after 1936 may reflect the union's success in keeping members from contracting new small-loan debts, or simply the restoration of salaries; the register cannot separate the two, and both were probably at work. What is clearer is that the union's moral map of credit was being redrawn. In the early 1930s the decisive line ran between the union and every commercial lender. By 1941 it ran between borrowing for sensible purposes on sensible terms, wherever it was done, and borrowing that was reckless. The *Thrift Bell* now taught members to compare a dealer's plan, a bank's personal loan and the union's terms, as though the union were one lender among several.[^d13]

This shift corresponds closely to the larger transformation Hyman describes, in which personal debt moved from the margins of American economic life to its center, not because Americans became less virtuous but because federal policy, financial innovation and retail competition made consumer lending profitable and respectable.{{hyman2011}} An institution founded to protect its members from consumer debt had become, within a decade, one of the means by which they took it on.

## 5.5 The widened bond and who counted as known

The consumer turn coincided with a change in who the members were. The widening of the common bond in 1936 to "employees of the Kestrel Bay board of education" admitted janitors, clerks and city-hall staff, and the share of men rose from 29 percent in 1930 to 37 percent in 1941. The newcomers were, by definition, less well known to the teachers who ran the credit committee. A janitor at Harbor Street School might be known to the teachers in that building, but not to a committee that met at Lakeside High and was drawn from its staff.

The committee responded in two ways. It relied more heavily on the documents described above, which made a stranger legible in the same terms as a colleague, and it extended the older system of personal vouching by requiring that new members' first loans be co-signed by a member of at least two years' standing, preferably one who worked in the same building.[^d14] The building, which had been the medium of the union's knowledge since its founding, thus remained a unit of credit judgment even as the union outgrew any single building. Knowledge of character was being delegated, through co-makers, from the committee to the membership at large.

Cohen's study of Chicago's industrial workers offers a suggestive comparison here. When the depression overwhelmed the ethnic benefit societies, ethnic banks and neighborhood stores on which those workers had relied, Cohen argues, they turned to the federal state and to industrial unions for the security those local institutions could no longer provide.{{cohen1990}} The Kestrel Bay members did not abandon their local institution; they joined it in growing numbers. But the institution itself changed in ways that parallel Cohen's story, accepting federal supervision, adopting national forms and recruiting from a circle defined by an employer rather than by acquaintance. The trajectory complicates any simple contrast between local institutions that failed in the depression and national ones that replaced them. In Kestrel Bay a local institution survived by absorbing features of the national order.

Gender adds a further dimension. The union had been founded and run largely by women: eight of the eleven founders, the treasurer for its first eleven years and the chair of its credit committee through the crisis. Their vision of thrift was in part a vision of household management. The automobile loans of the late 1930s, and the men who took a disproportionate share of them, drew the union toward a different model of the borrower: a wage earner buying goods on the strength of a regular income. I have found no record of open dispute about this change, and the register does not show how women members experienced it. It is nonetheless worth noting that the union's turn toward consumer durables coincided with the departure of the two women, Thornquist and Delacroix, who had done most to define what it meant by character.[^d15]

## 5.6 Thrift redefined

The language of the *Thrift Bell* in its last years of publication before the war records the final stage of this transformation. In its early numbers the newsletter had printed homilies on saving, household budget forms and accounts of members rescued from the small-loan companies. By 1939 it carried merchants' advertisements, the union's terms for automobile and appliance loans and articles on calculating the cost of an instalment contract. The word "thrift" appeared more often than ever, but it now named a set of competences rather than a set of renunciations: knowing the rate, comparing the terms, keeping up the payments, maintaining a share balance against the summer months.[^d16]

The movement's national leadership had always held that credit and thrift were compatible, and that the credit union's purpose was to make credit available for "provident or productive purposes" on fair terms, a phrase embodied in the federal statute under which the union was chartered.{{fcua1934}} What the Kestrel Bay case shows is how elastic that phrase could become in practice. A loan to pay a doctor in 1931 and a loan to buy a refrigerator in 1940 could both be described as provident, the second on the grounds that a refrigerator saved food and money over time. The union's officers made exactly this argument in the newsletter, and their members appear to have accepted it.[^d17] By the end of the period the union was explaining the purchase of durable goods in the same moral vocabulary it had once used to discourage it.

It would be a mistake to treat this as a betrayal of the founders' ideals. They had wanted members free of predatory creditors, deliberate about money and able to help one another through emergencies, and the union of 1941 did all of these things: it lent at a regulated rate, kept delinquency low, paid a steady 4 percent dividend and still extended hardship loans on the basis of what it knew about members in trouble. What had changed was the place of consumer goods in members' lives and, with it, the union's place in the credit economy. By 1941 it was a consumer lender, distinguished from banks and finance companies chiefly by its rate, its ownership and the residual sense, which Ruth Ellison's mother expressed, that borrowing from it was "different."

## 5.7 Conclusion

This chapter has traced the union through its last five years before the war. The loan register shows a sharp turn toward automobiles and household durables after 1936, with a doubling of the mean loan and longer terms. The credit committee's minutes show how that turn was made possible: character judged through personal knowledge in the school building gave way, for ordinary loans, to character inferred from salary, collateral and co-makers. The departures of Delacroix and Thornquist in 1938 and 1939 closed the founding generation, and the newsletter shows thrift redefined from the avoidance of consumer debt to its competent management.

These findings answer the third research question: the charter's effects were gradual, working through forms, examination and the widened common bond over several years. They bear on the fourth by showing that "character" in credit was never a fixed quality but a practice, sustained by particular institutions and particular people, which could be partly formalized and partly displaced without being abandoned. Chapter 6 draws these threads together with the findings of the earlier chapters and considers what the Kestrel Bay case contributes to the histories of the credit union movement and of American consumer credit.

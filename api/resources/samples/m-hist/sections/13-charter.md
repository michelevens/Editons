# Chapter 4: The Federal Charter, 1934–1937

On 18 June 1935 the Kestrel Bay Teachers' Credit Union ceased to be a creature of state law alone and received a charter under the Federal Credit Union Act. The document itself, a printed form with the union's name and common bond typed into the blanks, survives in the union's correspondence files, folded in three and slightly foxed.[^d1] It is an unremarkable object, and the union's own records treat its arrival with little ceremony: the board minutes note its receipt in a single sentence. Yet the charter marks the point at which the practices described in Chapter 3, the informal, face-to-face judgments of a small group of colleagues, began to be written into standard forms, reviewed by outsiders and measured against national rules.

This chapter addresses the third research question: what changed with the federal charter? It argues that the charter formalized the union's moral economy of credit rather than replacing it, but that formalization had consequences its officers did not fully foresee. The federal by-laws and forms turned the credit committee's knowledge of borrowers into documents, especially the signatures of co-makers, and in doing so made character something that could be recorded and checked. The widening of the common bond in 1936, which the federal framework made easier, brought in members who were not teachers and whom the committee did not know in the old way. And the union's rapid growth after 1935 changed the scale on which it operated, so that by 1937 it was lending more, and lending larger sums, to a membership whose ties to one another were looser than those of the founders. These changes prepared the ground for the turn toward consumer durables examined in Chapter 5.

The chapter begins with the union's recovery in 1934 (Section 4.1), then sets the federal act in the context of the national movement and the New Deal (Section 4.2). Sections 4.3 to 4.5 follow the decision to convert, the experience of federal forms and examination, and the widening of the common bond. Section 4.6 closes the account of the failed bank, and Section 4.7 considers where the union stood in 1937.

## 4.1 Recovery Without Rescue, 1934

The union recovered in 1934 without outside help. By the end of the year it had 214 members, more than at any earlier point, shares of $12,980 and loans outstanding of $10,340; it made 172 loans, and delinquency fell from 9.4 percent of loans at the end of 1933 to 5.8 percent. The annual meeting of January 1935 declared a dividend of 3 percent for 1934, the first since 1932.[^d2] The causes of recovery are partly visible in the records. The board of education's warrants were redeemed in the course of the year, which allowed the union to turn its paper back into cash and relieved members whose pay had been held up. Salaries were partly restored. And new members joined, among them younger teachers appointed after 1933 who had no memory of the union's early years but who had heard, in the building, that it had not closed when the bank did.[^d3]

That reputation was an asset in its own right. *The Thrift Bell* made the most of it, running in its September 1934 issue a short history of the union that described the spring of 1933 as a test the members had passed together.[^d4] The account is self-congratulatory, and it passes over the frozen deposit, the withdrawal limit's exceptions and the losses of 1932–1934 in a sentence. But it shows the union's officers beginning to tell the story of the crisis as a story about the union's character, an argument this thesis takes seriously while treating the officers' own version of it with caution.

The year 1934 is also the most poorly documented in the archive. The credit committee's minutes for 1934 are missing; the volume covering 1933 ends in December of that year and the next begins in January 1935, with no indication of what happened to the intervening book.[^d5] For this year the committee's decisions can be reconstructed only from the loan register, which records each loan's date, amount, term, purpose and co-makers, and from the board minutes, which note the committee's monthly totals. The register shows that the committee resumed lending at close to the rate of 1932, but it cannot show the reasoning that lay behind individual decisions, which is precisely what the minutes for other years supply. The gap is a reminder that the archive is itself the product of decisions about what to keep, and that the transition to federal forms, which is the subject of this chapter, was also a change in the kind of record the union produced.[^d6]

## 4.2 A Federal Law for the Small Borrower

The Federal Credit Union Act, signed by President Roosevelt on 26 June 1934, authorized the federal government to charter credit unions anywhere in the country.{{fcua1934}} The act defined a credit union as a cooperative association organized to promote thrift among its members and to create a source of credit for provident or productive purposes, and it limited membership to groups sharing a common bond of occupation or association, or to residents of a well-defined neighborhood, community or rural district.{{fcua1934}} These definitions were not new. They codified the principles the movement had been promoting for more than a decade, and the act was the culmination of a campaign that J. Carroll Moody and Gilbert Fite trace through the work of Edward Filene and Roy Bergengren and the long effort to pass enabling laws state by state.{{moody1971}}

Bergengren's own account of that campaign, written as the managing director of the Credit Union National Extension Bureau (CUNEB), must be read as the testimony of a participant rather than as a neutral history. It presents the federal act as a victory for what he called economic democracy, a means by which working people could manage their own savings and credit without depending on banks or lenders who exploited them.{{bergengren1952}} The language of the KBTCU's records in these years echoes Bergengren closely, which is not surprising: the union received the bureau's publications, and Thornquist corresponded with its staff.[^d7] When *The Thrift Bell* described the federal act as "a charter of independence for the teacher who saves," it was translating the movement's rhetoric into the idiom of the school.[^d8]

The act belonged to the wider program of financial reform that followed the crisis of 1933, but it differed from that program in an important respect. The Banking Act of 1933 had stabilized commercial banking by insuring deposits and separating commercial from investment banking.{{bankingact1933}} It extended nothing to credit unions. The Federal Credit Union Act, by contrast, gave credit unions a federal identity and a federal supervisor but no guarantee of members' shares. David Kennedy reads the New Deal's reforms as aimed above all at creating economic security for ordinary Americans, and the credit union act fits that reading in its intentions.{{kennedy1999}} In its mechanism, however, it was a different kind of measure. It did not offer security from Washington; it offered a framework within which members could provide security for one another, much as the KBTCU had done in 1933. The act's supporters regarded this as its virtue. For the union's officers, still carrying a frozen deposit in a failed bank, it was a point of genuine uncertainty.

The economics of the institution the act created help explain why. As John Croteau's later analysis emphasized, a credit union gathered members' savings and lent them back to members in small sums, so that its soundness depended on the quality of its loans and the steadiness of its members' saving rather than on any external backing.{{croteau1963}} A federal charter could improve the bookkeeping and the supervision of such an institution. It could not change the fact that its depositors and its borrowers were the same people.

The contrast with deposit insurance is instructive. Charles Calomiris and Eugene White have shown that federal insurance for bank deposits was adopted in 1933 less because experts thought it sound than because small unit banks and their allies in Congress pressed for a guarantee that would protect them.{{calomiris1994}} Credit unions had no comparable lobby in 1933, and the movement's leaders, judging by Bergengren's account, did not seek one; they sought chartering powers instead.{{bergengren1952}} The result was a federal framework that treated credit unions as self-governing cooperatives to be supervised rather than as depositories to be guaranteed. For a union like the KBTCU this meant that the moral economy of 1933 remained its only real protection, now overlaid with federal rules about how that protection should be documented.

## 4.3 The Decision to Convert, 1934–1935

The board first discussed a federal charter in October 1934, four months after the act was signed. The minutes record that Thornquist presented a summary of the act prepared from CUNEB circulars and that the directors were divided.[^d9] The arguments in favor were practical. A federal charter would bring the union under a supervisory system designed specifically for credit unions, with standard forms, regular examination and a national body of rules; it would give the union a standing that the state statute did not; and, in Thornquist's view, it would make it easier to widen the common bond if the board ever chose to. The arguments against were also practical. Conversion meant new by-laws, new forms and more paperwork for a treasurer who already gave two afternoons a week to the union. It meant inviting federal examiners into a school building. And it gained nothing, as one director pointed out, in the way of protection for members' savings.[^d10]

Superintendent Elmer Haskins, whose consent was needed for the union to continue occupying school premises, was consulted and gave a characteristically cautious reply. He did not object to a federal charter, he wrote, provided that the union's business continued to be conducted outside school hours and that the board of education incurred no liability of any kind.[^d11] His letter is a useful reminder that the union's relationship with the school system remained one of tolerance rather than sponsorship. The building gave the union its common bond, its office and the setting in which members knew one another; the board of education gave it nothing else.

The board voted to apply for a federal charter at its meeting of 12 February 1935, by a majority the minutes do not record.[^d12] The application required the union to adopt the standard federal by-laws, to reconstitute its board and credit committee under the new rules, to create a supervisory committee, and to submit a statement of its assets and liabilities. That statement raised the awkward question of the frozen deposit, which the union had carried on its books since February 1933 as a claim on the liquidators of Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust. Correspondence with the federal authorities in the spring of 1935 shows Thornquist explaining the claim and the dividends so far received, and being told that it could be carried as an asset at the estimated value of future recoveries.[^d13] The charter was granted on 18 June 1935. The union's common bond was defined, as before, as teachers employed by the Kestrel Bay board of education.

## 4.4 Forms, Examiners and the Written Character

The most immediate effect of the charter was on paper. The standard federal forms replaced the union's homemade loan applications, and the change is visible at once in the files. The applications of 1929–1934 are single sheets, often handwritten, recording the amount requested, the purpose in a few words, and the names of co-makers; the credit committee's decision is noted in the margin. The federal forms, used from the summer of 1935, are printed, longer and more systematic. They ask for the applicant's salary, existing debts, dependents and the security offered, and they require co-makers to sign a separate statement of their own obligations.[^d14]

The co-maker requirement had always been part of the union's practice, but under the federal by-laws it became more formal and more frequently invoked, because the by-laws required security for loans above a modest threshold, and many of the union's loans, which averaged $58 in the period 1933–1936, exceeded it.[^d15] In practice, security for a teacher's loan almost always meant the signatures of one or two colleagues. The written co-maker statement thus converted the informal knowledge on which the committee had always relied into a legal obligation: a colleague who signed was not merely vouching for a borrower's character but undertaking to pay if the borrower did not. Character, in other words, was being written down. The committee's minutes from 1935 onward record fewer of the discursive explanations that characterize those of 1932–1933 and more references to the form: "application in order, co-makers satisfactory, approved."[^d16]

This was formalization, not displacement, and the distinction matters. The credit committee still met, still knew most of the borrowers personally, and still exercised discretion. Frances Delacroix remained its chair until 1938, and her interventions continued to draw on knowledge that no form could capture. But the forms changed what counted as a reason. Before 1935 a loan could be justified by the committee's view of the borrower; after 1935 it had also to be justified by the information on the application, and a federal examiner would review the file. The first federal examination, conducted in the spring of 1936, produced a report that was broadly favorable but criticized the union's bookkeeping for informality and recommended that the credit committee record its reasons for exceptions more fully.[^d17] The recommendation is revealing. The examiner wanted the committee's judgments to be legible to outsiders, and legibility required that they be expressed in terms an outsider could check.

How borrowers experienced the new forms is harder to recover. The union's records preserve no complaints about them, but the absence of complaint is weak evidence in an archive kept by the officers who introduced the forms. The new application asked members to state their debts and their dependents on paper that would be read by the committee and, potentially, by an examiner, information that before 1935 had passed between colleagues in conversation if it passed at all. Ruth Ellison remembered her mother filling out a loan application at the kitchen table and remarking that "the government wants to know how many children I have," a recollection that, whatever its precision, suggests the forms were felt as an intrusion as well as a safeguard.[^d18] The formalization of character, in other words, also made members' private circumstances more visible to the institution.

The supervisory committee, which the federal by-laws required to audit the union's accounts, added another layer of documentation. Its members were teachers, and its reports in 1936 and 1937 are brief and approving.[^d19] But its existence meant that the treasurer's work was now formally reviewed by someone other than the board, which had not been the case before. Agnes Thornquist, who had kept the union's books single-handed since 1928, accepted the change with what the minutes suggest was mixed feeling. Her treasurer's report for 1935 thanks the supervisory committee for its "careful attention" and notes, in the next sentence, that the union's accounts had been "correct to the cent" for seven years without it.[^d20]

## 4.5 Widening the Common Bond, 1936

The federal framework also made it easier to change who could join. In 1936 the board voted to widen the common bond from teachers to "employees of the Kestrel Bay board of education," a change that brought in janitors, clerks in the board's offices at city hall and other non-teaching staff.[^d21] The proposal had been discussed since 1934, and Thornquist had cited it as one advantage of a federal charter. Its supporters argued that the union's services were needed at least as much by lower-paid employees as by teachers, and that the bond of a common employer was as real as the bond of a common profession. Its opponents, so far as the minutes record their views, worried that the credit committee would not know the new members as it knew teachers.[^d22]

Both arguments proved correct. Membership grew from 262 at the end of 1935 to 318 at the end of 1936 and 371 at the end of 1937, and the new members borrowed actively. But the composition of the membership changed in ways that loosened the ties on which character lending had depended. Women had made up 71 percent of members in 1930; the share of men, who had been 29 percent of members then, rose steadily after the bond was widened, reaching 37 percent by 1941. Many of the new male members worked in buildings and offices the teachers rarely entered. The credit committee could still ask about them, and the co-maker requirement meant that a new member usually needed a colleague willing to sign for him, but the knowledge on which its judgments rested was now more often second-hand.

The widening of the bond thus illustrates the double effect of the charter. It extended the union's services to people who needed them and whom the founders had not originally imagined as members, which was in keeping with the movement's ideals as Bergengren described them.{{bergengren1952}} It also weakened the social basis on which the union had survived the crisis. A union of teachers in a handful of schools could rely on the corridor and the staff room; a union of all the board of education's employees had to rely increasingly on forms.

## 4.6 The Last of the Bank

The account with Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust was finally closed in October 1936, when the liquidators paid the last of the dividends on the bank's frozen deposits. By then the union had recovered 62 percent of the $3,140 it had held at the bank in February 1933.[^d23] The board carried the unrecovered balance against the union's reserves, and Thornquist recorded the charge separately from the loan losses in her annual report, a choice that kept the union's lending record unblemished by the bank's failure. The *Evening Courier*'s report on the bank's final dividend noted that depositors had received "less than two-thirds of their money after more than three years," and contrasted their experience with that of depositors in banks that had reopened after the holiday and now enjoyed federal insurance.[^d24]

For the union, the closing of the account had symbolic weight. The board minutes of 13 October 1936 record a resolution of thanks to the members "who stood by their credit union when the bank did not stand by them."[^d25] The phrase restated the union's account of itself: an institution that had survived not through capital or guarantees but through the loyalty of its members. It was also, by 1936, a story about the past. Most of the union's members had joined since 1933, and many had never been teachers.

## 4.7 The Union in 1937

By the end of 1937 the union was larger and more prosperous than at any earlier point. It had 371 members, shares of $27,300 and loans outstanding of $24,110; it made 241 loans in the year, and delinquency stood at 2.9 percent. The dividend, 3 percent for 1934 to 1936, was raised to 4 percent for 1937.[^d26] The union's growth after the charter is visible in Figure 3.1, where loans outstanding rise more steeply after 1935 than in any earlier period.

The pattern of lending in the charter years remained close to that of the crisis. In 1933–1936, according to the loan register, medical and dental bills accounted for 24 percent of loans, paying off other debts (including small-loan companies) for 26 percent, family support and funerals for 16 percent, home repairs and fuel for 12 percent and education for 8 percent; automobiles accounted for 7 percent and household appliances and furniture for 4 percent. The mean loan was $58, slightly smaller than in 1929–1932. These were the provident purposes the federal act described and the founders had intended: loans to meet emergencies and to escape more expensive debt. The record suggests that formalization did not, by itself, change what members borrowed for.

What it did change was the institution's capacity to lend for other purposes. A union with standard forms, written co-maker obligations, regular examinations and a membership drawn from the whole of the board of education's workforce could lend larger sums to people its committee knew less well, because the forms and the co-makers supplied some of the assurance that personal knowledge had once provided. The charter, in this sense, made the consumer turn of the late 1930s possible without causing it. Chapter 5 examines that turn, and asks what became of character as a basis for credit when members began borrowing for automobiles and appliances.

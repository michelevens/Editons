# Chapter 6: Conclusion

This thesis began with a small puzzle. A credit union run from a room off a high-school library, open four hours a week and holding a few thousand dollars, came through the banking collapse of 1930–1933 in a city whose largest local bank did not. The Kestrel Bay Teachers' Credit Union was not better capitalized than Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust, and in February 1933 it lost access to $3,140 of its own funds when the bank suspended payments. Its survival therefore demands an explanation that does not rest on the size of its reserves. This concluding chapter draws together the answers the preceding chapters have given to the four research questions, considers what the case contributes to the histories of the credit union movement and of American consumer credit, and sets out the limits of the evidence and the questions it leaves open.

## 6.1 Answering the research questions

The first question asked who joined and borrowed, and for what. Chapter 2 showed that the union began as an association of colleagues: eleven founders, eight of them women, drawn from three schools and organized around Lakeside High School. Its membership grew from 46 at the end of 1928 to 201 at the end of 1931, fell to 176 in the worst year of the depression, and then rose steadily to 532 by 1941. Women were the large majority throughout, 71 percent in 1930 and still 63 percent in 1941, although the widening of the common bond in 1936 brought in janitors, clerks and city-hall staff and with them a larger proportion of men. For its first eight years the union lent overwhelmingly for provident purposes: medical and dental bills, the clearing of debts owed to other lenders, family support and funerals, fuel and the repair of houses. Chapter 5 showed how sharply that pattern changed after 1936, when loans for automobiles and household durables rose from about a tenth of all loans to more than four in ten, and the mean loan almost doubled, from $58 in 1933–1936 to $121 in 1937–1941.

The second question asked how the union survived 1930–1933. Chapter 3 argued that the answer lies in the union's capacity to lend, collect and restrict withdrawals on the basis of what its members knew about one another. When the board of education paid teachers partly in salary warrants in the 1932–33 school year, the union accepted those warrants from members at face value for shares and repayments, and bought them for cash at a modest discount. In doing so it converted an illiquid and mistrusted paper into a medium its members could use, at a cost the union could bear because it knew which warrant-holders would remain on the payroll. When Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust failed, and when the national bank holiday followed, the board limited withdrawals to $25 per member per month while preserving discretion, exercised by the credit committee, for illness and funerals. Delinquency rose to 9.4 percent of loans in 1933 and the union wrote off $812 in 1932–1934, most of its losses for the whole period, but it paid no dividend in 1933 rather than draw down its capital, and it did not close. The banking-crisis literature emphasizes the destruction of the information and relationships on which credit depended once banks failed.{{bernanke1983}} The Kestrel Bay union survived because its information and relationships were lodged not in a bank's ledgers but in the staff rooms of the city's schools.

The third question asked what changed with the federal charter. Chapter 4 showed that the charter of 18 June 1935 brought examination, standardized accounting and the forms and guidance of the national movement, and Chapter 5 traced its slower consequences. The charter did not transform the union overnight. Its effects worked through the paperwork it required, through the widened common bond that followed in 1936, and through the departure of the founding officers, Delacroix in 1938 and Thornquist in 1939. By 1941 the credit committee judged most loans on salary, collateral and co-makers, and reserved its older, personal mode of judgment for members in difficulty. The union grew faster under the charter than before it, and its delinquency fell to 2.0 percent by 1941, but its lending had moved toward the kind of borrowing its founders had hoped to discourage.

The fourth question asked what the case says about "character" as the basis for credit. The answer this thesis offers is that character was not a quality borrowers possessed but a practice institutions performed. In Kestrel Bay it was performed by a credit committee whose members taught alongside the applicants, in a building where reputations were made and could be lost, and it was enforced by the fact that a defaulter would face her creditors every working day. That practice proved more robust than the banks' formal assessments of creditworthiness in 1931–1933, precisely because it did not depend on the instruments, a regular salary above all, that the crisis had destroyed. It proved less necessary, and was partly displaced, once those instruments were restored and federal supervision required that judgments be written down. Character did not vanish from the union's lending; it changed function, from the foundation of all lending to the court of appeal for exceptional cases.

## 6.2 Contributions

The case refines the national histories of the credit union movement in two ways. The standard accounts trace the movement through its leaders, its legislative campaigns and the growth of its institutions, culminating in the Federal Credit Union Act of 1934.{{moody1971}} The participant's account written by the movement's chief organizer presents that campaign as a crusade for economic democracy.{{bergengren1952}} Neither is designed to show what the movement's ideals looked like at a credit committee table on a Friday afternoon in March 1933. The Kestrel Bay records show that the ideals were translated, not simply applied: the union's officers improvised the warrant scheme and the withdrawal limit from local materials, and their idea of provident lending stretched, by 1940, to cover a refrigerator. The movement's national history, in this light, is also a history of many such translations, whose variety the aggregate figures conceal.

The case also contributes to the cultural history of consumer credit. Calder's argument that consumer borrowing was institutionalized and legitimized between 1890 and 1940, and that regular repayment imposed its own discipline, finds strong local confirmation in the union's late-1930s turn, and in the newsletter's redefinition of thrift as competent debt management.{{calder1999}} Olney's emphasis on the supply-side forces behind the durables revolution helps explain why Kestrel Bay's teachers came to the union with dealers' contracts in hand.{{olney1991}} Hyman's account of debt's passage from the margins to the mainstream of American life is visible here in miniature.{{hyman2011}} What the case adds is an institution these accounts usually treat as marginal: a cooperative lender, founded in opposition to the instalment economy, which became one of its local channels. The legitimation of consumer credit, the Kestrel Bay evidence suggests, was achieved not only by sales finance companies and banks but also by institutions whose moral reputation allowed borrowing to be presented as thrift.

Finally, the thesis offers a qualification to accounts of the depression that contrast the failure of local and ethnic institutions with the rise of federal and national ones.{{cohen1990}} The Kestrel Bay union was a local institution that survived. It did so, however, partly by becoming less local, accepting federal supervision and national forms. The line between the local and the national in the 1930s ran through institutions as well as between them.

## 6.3 Limitations

The conclusions of this thesis rest on a single institution and a single archive, and they should be read with the limitations of both in mind. A microhistory justifies its narrowness by what a small case can illuminate about a larger culture, but it cannot establish how typical that case was.{{lepore2001}} I cannot say, on this evidence, whether other teachers' credit unions accepted warrants, limited withdrawals or turned toward durables in the same years. The records of the union are rich for its lending but thin for its members' lives outside it; the credit committee's minutes for 1934 are missing, the loan register's occupational entries are incomplete after the change of hand in 1939, and the voices of borrowers survive chiefly where the officers chose to record them.[^d1] The archive was made by the union's officers, and its silences, about members refused loans, about disputes the minutes smoothed over, reflect their position.{{trouillot1995}} The single oral history interview used here is valuable but limited: Ruth Ellison was a child in the period and recalled, in part, her mother's account of it. Finally, the quantitative series in Appendix B rest on year-end figures from the treasurer's reports and my own coding of the register, and the purpose categories in particular depend on the treasurer's entries, which were not designed for the questions I have put to them.

## 6.4 Further research

Three lines of inquiry follow. The first is comparative. Teachers' credit unions were numerous in the interwar years, and a study of several, in cities whose banks failed and cities whose banks did not, would test whether the moral economy described here was a general feature of occupational credit unions or a product of Kestrel Bay's particular circumstances. The second concerns the city's other financial institutions, above all the licensed small-loan companies whose debts the union repaid in the early 1930s, whose records, if they survive, would show the union from the outside. The third would carry the story into the war years and beyond, when wartime controls on consumer credit and the postwar boom in durables again changed what members borrowed for. Local historians are well placed to pursue such work, and the connection between a single community's records and the larger questions of national history is one of local history's characteristic strengths.{{kammen2014}}

## 6.5 Closing

The founders of the Kestrel Bay Teachers' Credit Union believed that teachers who knew one another could lend to one another more safely and more justly than any bank. Between 1928 and 1933 they were proved right, in circumstances harder than any of them had imagined. Between 1935 and 1941 the institution they built became larger, more secure and more like the lenders it had been founded to resist. Neither outcome was a failure. Together they show how the ideal of credit grounded in character was sustained by a particular community at a particular moment, and how it gave way, not to its opposite, but to a quieter and more ordinary kind of trust: the expectation that a salary would be paid, a payment made and a contract honored. That expectation, which the crisis years had shown could not be taken for granted, was by 1941 the foundation of the union's lending, and of the consumer economy it had joined.

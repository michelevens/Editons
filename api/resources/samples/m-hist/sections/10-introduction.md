# Chapter 1: Introduction

On the afternoon of Tuesday, 7 March 1933, with every bank in the country closed by presidential proclamation and the doors of Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust already shut for two weeks, Agnes Thornquist unlocked a small room off the library of Lakeside High School and opened the cash box of the Kestrel Bay Teachers' Credit Union. The board had met the night before. Its minutes record a single resolution passed without dissent: until the end of June, no member could withdraw more than $25 from his or her shares in any month, unless the credit committee agreed that illness or a funeral required more.[^d1] Thornquist, a mathematics teacher who had kept the union's books since its first meeting in 1928, noted in her ledger that nineteen members came to the window that afternoon. Eleven made deposits, mostly in the city's interest-bearing salary warrants rather than in cash. Four repaid installments on loans. Four asked to withdraw and were given what the new rule allowed.[^d2]

This scene is small, but it contains the problem this thesis sets out to explain. The bank down the street, a commercial institution with professional managers, a board of local businessmen and many times the union's resources, had failed. The credit union, run after school by volunteers from a room the size of a storeroom, did not. It paid no dividend in 1933, its delinquent loans rose, and its membership fell. Yet it kept its window open on Tuesdays and Fridays throughout the crisis, and by 1941 it had more than eleven times as many members as at the end of its first year. How it did so, and what that survival cost and changed, is the subject of what follows.

## 1.1 Argument and research questions

This thesis argues that the Kestrel Bay Teachers' Credit Union (KBTCU) survived the banking collapse of the early 1930s not because it was better capitalized than the banks around it but because it lent on "character." By character, its officers meant something concrete: members' knowledge of one another, accumulated in staff rooms and corridors over years of shared work, and enforced through a credit committee whose members saw borrowers every day and through the small, dense society of the school building. That moral economy of credit held through 1933. It was formalized and partly displaced by the federal charter the union received on 18 June 1935, which brought written rules, federal examiners and a widened membership. By 1941 it had turned toward the financing of automobiles and household appliances, precisely the kind of borrowing the movement's founders had hoped to keep members away from.

Four research questions organize the inquiry. First, who joined the union and who borrowed from it, and for what purposes? Second, how did the union survive the years 1930 to 1933, when the city's board of education paid teachers partly in warrants and the city's largest local bank suspended payments? Third, what changed when the union exchanged its state charter for a federal one? Fourth, what does this case say about character as a basis for credit, a notion that the credit union movement invoked constantly but that historians have rarely examined at the level of individual lending decisions?

These questions are local, but the answers bear on three larger historiographies: of the American credit union movement, of the banking crisis of the Great Depression, and of the rise of consumer credit. The sections that follow place the study within each, then describe the sources and methods on which it rests and outline the chapters.

## 1.2 The credit union movement

The standard scholarly history of American credit unions remains J. Carroll Moody and Gilbert Fite's study of the movement from its European origins to 1970.{{moody1971}} Moody and Fite trace the cooperative credit idea from the rural banks of Raiffeisen and the urban people's banks of Schulze-Delitzsch, through Alphonse Desjardins's caisses populaires in Quebec, to the campaign in the United States financed by Edward Filene and directed by Roy Bergengren. Their account is institutional and national. It follows the passage of state enabling statutes, the work of the Credit Union National Extension Bureau (CUNEB), the passage of the Federal Credit Union Act in 1934 and the growth of a national association. Individual credit unions appear chiefly as evidence of the movement's spread.

Bergengren's own account of the campaign, published in 1952, is indispensable but must be read as a participant's testimony rather than as history.{{bergengren1952}} As managing director of the extension bureau, Bergengren had every reason to present credit unions as he wished them to be: instruments of what he called economic democracy, through which working people would learn thrift, escape usurious lenders and govern their own savings. His book is therefore a primary source for the movement's ideals, and this thesis treats it as such. Much of what the founders of the Kestrel Bay union believed about thrift, character and the dangers of installment buying came to them, directly or indirectly, from the literature that Bergengren's bureau distributed.[^d3]

The economic literature has approached credit unions from a different angle, asking how they gathered members' savings and made small loans and how they fitted within the wider system of consumer finance.{{croteau1963}} That perspective is useful for framing the union's balance sheet, but it says little about how a particular credit committee decided whether a particular teacher should be lent sixty dollars for a dental bill.

What both the institutional and the economic literatures leave largely unexamined, then, is the daily practice of lending. The movement claimed that credit unions could lend safely to people whom banks would not serve because members knew one another. This was the meaning of the "common bond" that state laws, and later the federal statute, required: a shared occupation, association or neighborhood that made members known quantities to each other.{{fcua1934}} Whether and how that knowledge was actually mobilized when a member asked for money, what happened when it failed, and how it changed as a credit union grew, are questions that can be answered only from the records of individual societies. This thesis answers them for one.

## 1.3 The banking crisis

The second historiography concerns the banking crisis itself. Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz made the waves of bank failures between 1930 and 1933 central to their account of the Great Contraction, arguing that the Federal Reserve's failure to counter them allowed the money stock to collapse.{{friedman1963}} Subsequent work has qualified this account in several directions. Elmus Wicker's reconstruction of the five banking panics stresses their regional rather than national character.{{wicker1996}} Eugene White, using balance-sheet data for individual banks, found that the failures of 1930 were concentrated among small banks already weakened by regional distress and did not differ in kind from the failures of the 1920s.{{white1984}} Gary Richardson's analysis of Federal Reserve records on the causes of suspensions concluded that both illiquidity and insolvency mattered, and that heavy withdrawals were a primary cause in a large share of cases.{{richardson2007}} Richardson and William Troost showed, by comparing banks on either side of a Federal Reserve district border, that central-bank lending could mitigate panics.{{richardson2009}}

Ben Bernanke's argument about the non-monetary effects of the crisis is particularly relevant here.{{bernanke1983}} Bernanke contended that bank failures destroyed the information and relationships through which banks had channeled credit to households, farmers and small firms, so that credit became dearer and scarcer for precisely the borrowers least able to find it elsewhere. The Kestrel Bay case can be read as a small-scale illustration of the other side of that argument. If the failure of Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust destroyed one set of credit relationships, the credit union preserved another, grounded not in bank examiners' ratios but in the dense knowledge that teachers had of one another. This thesis does not claim that such institutions could have offset the national contraction; the union was far too small for that. It claims rather that the union's survival illuminates, at close range, what kind of information sustained lending when the banks could not.

The political history of the crisis provides the national frame within which the local events of 1933 unfolded. Susan Estabrook Kennedy's study of the banking crisis of 1933 follows the path to the collapse of early that year, the interregnum between Hoover and Roosevelt, the national bank holiday and the emergency and reform legislation that followed.{{kennedy1973}} Among that legislation was the Banking Act of 1933, which created federal deposit insurance and reorganized commercial banking.{{bankingact1933}} Charles Calomiris and Eugene White have explained deposit insurance as the product of interest-group politics in which small unit banks and their congressional allies prevailed.{{calomiris1994}} David Kennedy's synthesis of the period reads the New Deal's reforms as aimed above all at economic security for ordinary Americans.{{kennedy1999}} The Federal Credit Union Act of 1934 belonged to that effort, but, as Chapter 4 shows, it reached Kestrel Bay as a mixed blessing: it conferred legitimacy and federal supervision, yet it also began to replace the personal judgment on which the union had relied.

Lizabeth Cohen's study of Chicago's industrial workers offers a further point of comparison.{{cohen1990}} Cohen showed how the Depression overwhelmed the ethnic benefit societies, neighborhood banks and local stores on which workers had relied, and how workers turned instead to the federal state and to industrial unions. The Kestrel Bay teachers were not industrial workers, and their union was not an ethnic institution. But their credit union was a local, voluntary, mutual institution of exactly the kind Cohen describes as failing, and it did not fail. One aim of this thesis is to ask why, and whether its survival was owed to features that the institutions Cohen studied lacked: a salaried membership whose employer, the board of education, continued to pay, however irregularly; and a workplace that doubled as a site of mutual surveillance.

## 1.4 Consumer credit and the meaning of thrift

The third historiography concerns consumer credit. Lendol Calder has dismantled the image of a thrifty, debt-free American past, showing that Americans borrowed long before the so-called credit revolution and that what changed between 1890 and 1940 was the institutionalization and legitimation of consumer credit, with installment selling of automobiles as the watershed of the 1920s.{{calder1999}} Calder also argued that regular repayment imposed a discipline of its own, so that installment credit did not simply erode thrift but redefined it. Martha Olney's quantitative study located a genuine revolution in household purchases of durable goods in the 1920s and attributed it largely to manufacturers' advertising and the spread of installment credit through sales finance companies.{{olney1991}} Louis Hyman has traced personal debt's movement from the margins to the mainstream of American life in the twentieth century, explaining it through structural changes in capitalism and federal policy rather than through cultural decline.{{hyman2011}}

Contemporary investigators of the 1930s approached the same phenomena as a social problem. The Russell Sage Foundation's studies of small-loan lending examined loan sharks, the regulation of licensed lenders and the characteristics of borrowers.{{robinson1935}} Rolf Nugent, who directed the foundation's consumer credit studies, treated consumer credit as a factor in economic fluctuations.{{nugent1939}} The campaign for the Uniform Small Loan Law, which licensed lenders to charge wage earners rates above the usury ceilings in exchange for regulation, has been analyzed by Bruce Carruthers, Timothy Guinnane and Yoonseok Lee.{{carruthers2012}} Credit unions occupied an ambiguous position in this debate. Their promoters presented them as an alternative to both the loan shark and the licensed lender, offering cheaper credit for "provident" purposes while teaching members to save.

The Kestrel Bay records allow these arguments to be tested in a single institution over thirteen years. They show a credit union that, in its first years, lent mainly for medical bills, the repayment of other debts, family emergencies and summer study, and that by the end of the 1930s was lending more for automobiles than for any other single purpose. That shift, documented in Chapter 5, is the case's most direct contribution to the history of consumer credit. It suggests that the line between the movement's ideal of provident borrowing and the consumer credit it deplored was drawn less firmly in practice than in its literature, and that it moved as members' circumstances, and the institution's own scale, changed.

## 1.5 Sources and method

### 1.5.1 Microhistory and local history

This is a study of one small institution in one city. Its method owes something to microhistory, and it is worth being precise about what that means. Jill Lepore distinguished microhistory from biography by its purpose: biography values a life for its own sake, while microhistory studies a single, often obscure, life or episode for what it reveals about the wider culture.{{lepore2001}} The KBTCU is interesting in itself, and Agnes Thornquist, its treasurer for eleven years, is the closest thing this study has to a protagonist. But the union is examined here chiefly for what it shows about how the credit union movement's ideals worked, or failed to work, when they met the pressures of the Depression and the temptations of the consumer economy that followed it. Lepore also warned that historians grow attached to their subjects. I have tried to keep that warning in view, especially in Chapters 3 and 5, where the temptation to cast the union's officers as heroes or as backsliders is strongest.

The study is also local history, with that genre's particular responsibilities. Carol Kammen has written of the local historian's obligations to a community that will read what is written about it, and of the need to treat conflict and uncomfortable episodes honestly rather than to celebrate.{{kammen2014}} Some families in Kestrel Bay will recognize names in these pages. The credit-committee minutes record debts, illnesses, funerals and delinquencies that were once matters of acute private embarrassment. I have used members' names where they held office or where their actions were matters of public record, and have otherwise referred to borrowers by initials and loan number. David Kyvig and Myron Marty's guide to "nearby history" has been useful in a practical sense, particularly its discussion of institutional records as sources for the history of communities.{{kyvig2010}}

### 1.5.2 The archive and its silences

The core of the evidence is the union's own records, deposited at the Kestrel Bay Historical Society (KBHS) in 1972 when the union merged with a larger regional credit union.[^d4] The collection comprises board minutes for the whole period, credit-committee minutes, a loan register of 2,516 entries from 1929 to 1941, the treasurer's annual reports, a run of the members' newsletter *The Thrift Bell* from 1931 to 1941 and a box of correspondence. Appendix A describes the collection in detail.

These records are unusually full for an institution of this size, but they are not a neutral window onto the past. Antoinette Burton and her contributors have argued that archives are artifacts with their own histories, shaped by the conditions under which they were made, kept and opened to researchers.{{burton2005}} The KBTCU Records were made by the union's officers, above all by Thornquist, and they record what those officers thought worth recording. Carolyn Steedman's reflection on the archive as a repository of fragments is apt: what survives is the dust of an institution's daily life, and the history is made from what the historian does with it.{{steedman2001}} The minutes record decisions, rarely the arguments that preceded them. The loan register records amounts, purposes and repayments, but its "purpose" column reflects what borrowers chose to declare and what the clerk chose to write.

Michel-Rolph Trouillot identified four moments at which silences enter historical production: the making of sources, of archives, of narratives and of history in the final instance.{{trouillot1995}} All four are at work here. The members least likely to appear in the union's records are those who were refused loans, since the credit committee often recorded refusals by number only, and those who never joined. The records are silent on the teachers who borrowed from small-loan companies because they did not wish their colleagues to know their circumstances, a silence that Chapter 2 suggests was itself a product of lending on character. The minutes of the credit committee for 1934 are missing altogether. And this thesis, by organizing the material around the survival of the institution, makes its own choices about what to foreground. I have tried to mark those choices where they matter.

### 1.5.3 Newspaper and oral history

Two other bodies of evidence supplement the union's records. The *Kestrel Bay Evening Courier* reported the failure of Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust, the board of education's resort to salary warrants and, occasionally, the doings of the credit union itself.[^d5] Its coverage reflects the outlook of the city's business community, which was, for most of the period, skeptical of a "teachers' bank."

The second is an oral-history interview I recorded in 2023 with Ruth Ellison, born in 1924, whose family belonged to the union and who accompanied a parent to the office as a child.[^d6] Paul Thompson and Joanna Bornat have described both the value of oral history for recovering ordinary people's experience and the care required in using memory as evidence.{{thompson2017}} Ellison's testimony is a child's memory recalled across nine decades. It cannot establish what the credit committee decided or why. It can, however, convey what the union felt like to a family that depended on it: the smell of the library, the hush at the window, the sense that a loan was a matter between neighbors rather than a transaction with strangers. I use it for that purpose, and I flag where it diverges from the documentary record.

### 1.5.4 Counting

Finally, the thesis uses simple quantitative methods. I transcribed the loan register in full, coding each entry by year, amount, declared purpose, the borrower's sex and school, and repayment outcome. From these data, together with the treasurer's annual reports, I compiled the year-end series of membership, shares, loans outstanding, loans made and delinquency reported in Appendix B. The figures are descriptive. They are used to establish patterns, such as the shift in loan purposes after 1937 and the movement of delinquency through the crisis, which the documentary sources then help to interpret. Where the coding of a purpose required judgment, as when a loan was entered as "debts and doctor," I followed rules set out in Appendix A.

## 1.6 Kestrel Bay

A brief word on the setting. Kestrel Bay is a lake port and paper-mill city of about seventy thousand people in a Great Lakes state. In the late 1920s its economy rested on three paper mills, a grain and coal harbor, and the commerce that served them. Its public schools employed several hundred teachers, overwhelmingly women in the elementary grades and a more mixed staff in its two high schools. Teachers' salaries were modest, paid in ten monthly installments during the school year, and subject to the fortunes of a municipal budget that depended heavily on mill property taxes.[^d7] Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust, organized in the 1890s, was the city's largest locally owned bank and held the deposits of many teachers as well as of the union itself.

## 1.7 Chapter outline

The thesis proceeds chronologically, with each chapter organized around one of the research questions.

Chapter 2, "Founding a Teachers' Bank, 1928–1930," reconstructs the union's organization by eleven teachers in October 1928, the ideas that animated its founders and the practices of its first two years. It shows how the credit committee developed a working definition of character from the materials of school life, and it examines who joined and borrowed and for what.

Chapter 3, "Holding On, 1931–1933," is the heart of the thesis. It follows the union through the board of education's payment of salaries in warrants, the board's decision in December 1932 to accept warrants from members and to buy them for cash at a discount, the suspension of Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust on 21 February 1933 with $3,140 of the union's funds inside it, and the limit on withdrawals imposed during the bank holiday. It argues that the union survived because its officers could make discriminating judgments about individual members when blanket rules would have broken it.

Chapter 4, "The Federal Charter, 1934–1937," examines the union's decision to seek a federal charter, its reception on 18 June 1935 and the widening of the common bond in 1936 to all employees of the board of education. It shows how federal supervision formalized the credit committee's practices and, in doing so, began to change them.

Chapter 5, "Character, Credit and the Consumer Turn, 1937–1941," analyzes the transformation of the union's lending in its last prewar years, when automobile and appliance loans rose to a large share of new lending and the mean loan roughly doubled. It asks what character meant when the borrower was a janitor at a school the credit committee had never visited and the purpose was a used car.

Chapter 6 concludes by returning to the four research questions and considering what the Kestrel Bay case suggests about character as a basis for credit. Two appendices describe the sources and present the membership and lending series on which the quantitative arguments rest.

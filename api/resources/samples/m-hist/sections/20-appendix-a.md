# Appendix A: A Note on the Sources

The principal source for this thesis is the Kestrel Bay Teachers' Credit Union Records, MS 214, held by the Kestrel Bay Historical Society. The collection is arranged in eight boxes. This note describes its series, the gaps within them and the ways in which I have used them, so that readers can judge the evidence on which the argument rests.

**Board minutes (boxes 1–2).** The minutes of the board of directors run without a break from the organizing meeting of 9 October 1928 to the end of the period. Until 1935 they were kept in a bound ledger in the hands of successive secretaries; after the federal charter they were typed on loose sheets and filed by year. They record decisions rather than debates, and on contested questions, such as the acceptance of salary warrants in December 1932, the minutes give the motion and the vote but seldom the arguments. Where the reasoning behind a decision matters, I have read the minutes alongside the correspondence and the newsletter.

**Credit committee minutes (box 3).** These are the richest source for the union's lending practice and the most uneven. From 1929 to 1933, and especially under Frances Delacroix's chairmanship from 1931, they contain extended discussion of individual applications. The minutes for the whole of 1934 are missing; the folder for that year contains only a note, in an unidentified hand, that they were "lent to the examiner." I have not been able to trace them, and my account of the committee's practice in that year relies on the loan register and the board minutes. From 1938 the committee's minutes become brief and formulaic, a change discussed in Chapter 5.

**Loan register (boxes 4–5).** The register records 2,516 loans made between 1929 and 1941, giving for each the date, the borrower's name, school or department, the sum, the stated purpose, the security and the dates of repayment. It was kept by the treasurer, Agnes Thornquist, until her retirement in 1939, when the handwriting changes and the entries become less consistent: occupational information is often omitted, and purposes are sometimes recorded in a single word. I coded the purposes of all 2,516 loans into the eight categories used in Table 5.1 and Table B.2. Where an entry named two purposes I assigned it to the first named; where none was given (fewer than one entry in fifty) I placed it under "Other." A small number of loan files, containing applications and security documents, survive interleaved with the register for 1936–1941.

**Treasurer's annual reports (box 6).** These typed reports, presented to each annual meeting, are the source of the year-end figures for members, shares and loans outstanding in Table B.1. They also contain Thornquist's commentary on the year, which I have used, with appropriate caution, as evidence of how the union's principal officer understood its purposes.

**The Thrift Bell (box 7).** The members' newsletter appeared from the autumn of 1931 to 1941, usually monthly during the school year. The run is nearly complete, though several issues from 1933 and 1934 are missing. As an official publication it presents the union as its officers wished members to see it, and I have treated it as evidence of the union's self-presentation rather than of members' views.

**Correspondence (box 8).** The correspondence is fragmentary. It includes letters from the national movement's organizers, the state supervisory office and, after 1935, the federal authorities, together with a small number of letters from members. It is strongest for 1934–1936, when the union was preparing its federal application.

**Other sources.** The *Kestrel Bay Evening Courier* provided the public narrative of the bank failure, the warrant episode and the bank holiday. I also recorded one oral history interview, with Ruth Ellison (born 1924), whose mother was a member, deposited with the Kestrel Bay Oral History Project as KBOH-23-07. Her recollections, filtered through childhood and through her mother's retelling, are used chiefly for the meanings members attached to borrowing, not for dates or sums.{{thompson2017}}

Every archive is the product of choices about what to keep, and this one was kept by the union's officers.{{burton2005}} It records the loans that were made far more fully than the applications that were refused, and the voices of borrowers only where officers chose to write them down. Those silences are part of the evidence, and I have tried to mark them where they bear on the argument.{{trouillot1995}} What survives is, in Steedman's sense, fragments, from which a history has to be made rather than simply recovered.{{steedman2001}}

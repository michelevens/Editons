# Chapter 3: Holding On, 1931–1933

On the afternoon of Tuesday, 21 February 1933, Agnes Thornquist opened the credit union's office off the Lakeside High School library at half past three, as she had done twice a week for more than four years. By then the whole city knew that Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust, the largest local bank and the institution that held the credit union's cash, had not opened its doors. Thornquist's entry in the treasurer's daybook for that afternoon is two lines long. It records eleven members who came to the office, the sum each wished to withdraw, and the sum each was in fact paid, which in every case was smaller.[^d1] Those two lines condense this chapter's problem. The Kestrel Bay Teachers' Credit Union (KBTCU) entered the worst years of the Depression small, young and undercapitalized, with its reserves partly locked inside a failing bank. It came out of them poorer and without a dividend, but intact.

This chapter answers the second of the thesis's research questions: how did the union survive the years 1930 to 1933, when the city's largest bank did not? It argues that survival depended less on the union's balance sheet, which was weaker than its officers liked to admit, than on three practices that drew on the members' knowledge of one another. The first was the board's decision in December 1932 to accept the city's salary warrants as if they were money, which kept members' accounts moving when the board of education stopped paying in cash. The second was a rationing of withdrawals during the spring of 1933 that members accepted because the people imposing it were colleagues they saw every day. The third was the credit committee's forbearance toward delinquent borrowers, exercised case by case on the basis of what its members knew about each borrower's circumstances and conduct. Together these practices amounted to what Chapter 1 called a moral economy of credit: a set of shared expectations about who deserved help and on what terms, enforced not by law or collateral but by the school building itself.

Section 3.1 places Kestrel Bay within the literature on the banking crisis and Section 3.2 sets out the union's figures. Sections 3.3 to 3.5 follow the warrant episode, the bank failure and the national bank holiday, and Section 3.6 turns to delinquency, where the meaning of "character" was tested most directly.

## 3.1 The Banking Crisis and a Small City

The historiography of the banking crisis of 1930–1933 offers several frames for a local case, and they do not point in the same direction. The most influential remains Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz's account of the "Great Contraction," in which successive waves of bank failures turned a recession into a depression: the money stock collapsed and the Federal Reserve failed to stop it.{{friedman1963}} Here the failing bank matters chiefly as a unit in an aggregate.

Later work has made the local scale more, not less, important. Elmus Wicker's reconstruction of the five banking panics of the period found them to be more regional than national, concentrated in particular Federal Reserve districts and following the failure of particular banking groups rather than sweeping uniformly across the country.{{wicker1996}} Eugene White's study of the failures of 1930, using the balance sheets of individual banks, argued that many of the banks that failed were small institutions already weakened by regulation and by regional distress, and that their failures resembled those of the 1920s more than the standard narrative of panic allowed.{{white1984}} Gary Richardson's analysis of the Federal Reserve's own records of why banks suspended found that both explanations had force: most banks that closed for financial reasons were insolvent, but heavy withdrawals were also a primary cause in a large share of suspensions.{{richardson2007}} And Richardson and William Troost, comparing banks on either side of a Federal Reserve district boundary, showed that where the central bank lent freely to troubled banks more of them survived and local commerce contracted less, which implies that the fate of a given town's bank depended partly on decisions made far away.{{richardson2009}}

A different line of argument concerns not money but credit. Ben Bernanke argued that bank failures and debtor insolvencies damaged the services banks provided in assessing and lending to borrowers, so that credit became dearer and scarcer for those, such as households and small firms, who could not turn to securities markets.{{bernanke1983}} This "nonmonetary" channel is the one most relevant to a credit union, which existed precisely to lend to people banks did not, on the whole, serve.

The national literature on the climax of the crisis completes the frame. Susan Estabrook Kennedy's study of 1933 traced how the system reached the collapse of that winter, through the interregnum between Herbert Hoover's defeat and Franklin Roosevelt's inauguration, to the national bank holiday and the emergency legislation that followed.{{kennedy1973}} Charles Calomiris and Eugene White, explaining the adoption of federal deposit insurance in the Banking Act of 1933, emphasized the politics of small unit banks and their congressional allies, which matters here because the reform that restored confidence in banks did nothing directly for credit unions.{{calomiris1994}}

None of these works describes how an institution as small as the KBTCU experienced the crisis, but together they suggest what to look for. If Wicker and White are right, the failure of Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust should be read as the story of one bank rather than an instance of national panic. If Richardson is right that illiquidity and insolvency were intertwined, the union's own problem in 1933, a shortage of cash created by another institution's insolvency, should be visible in its records. And if Bernanke is right, the union's lending should show signs of absorbing demand that had nowhere else to go. The evidence bears out the second and third expectations more clearly than the first, because the union's archive says much about its own cash and borrowers and little about the bank's books.

The union's year-end figures for 1930 to 1934, drawn from the treasurer's annual reports and checked against the loan register, are set out in Table 3.1. They describe an institution that grew rapidly until 1931, contracted for two years, and began to recover in 1934.

Three features of Table 3.1 deserve comment. The first is how mild the contraction was. Between the end of 1931 and the end of 1933 membership fell from 201 to 176 and shares from $12,470 to $10,630, roughly one-seventh of members' savings. Loans outstanding fell further, from $10,880 to $8,770, as the committee lent more cautiously and repayments continued to come in. These were real losses, but not the collapse that overtook the city's bank. The second is that lending did not stop. The union made 171 loans in 1932, more than in any previous year, at the very moment its members' savings began to shrink. Demand for small loans rose as salaries fell, which is consistent with Bernanke's suggestion that the crisis left households without their usual sources of credit, although the union's records cannot show where members would otherwise have borrowed.[^d2] The third is delinquency, which rose from 2.5 percent of loans in 1930 to 9.4 percent in 1933 before falling back. That rise, and the committee's response to it, is the subject of Section 3.6.

## 3.3 Paid in Paper: The Salary Warrants, 1932–1933

### 3.3.1 The board's decision of December 1932

The crisis reached the union's members first through their pay. In the autumn of 1932, faced with falling tax collections, the Kestrel Bay board of education began paying part of teachers' salaries in interest-bearing warrants: promises to pay, redeemable when the city's revenues allowed, rather than cash. The *Evening Courier* reported the decision with sympathy for the board and some anxiety for local merchants, who would now be asked to accept paper in place of money.[^d4] Practices of this kind were not peculiar to Kestrel Bay, and the national literature treats the scrip and warrants of the period as symptoms of a payments system under strain.[^d5] A teacher could hold a warrant until redemption, which few could afford, or sell it at a discount.

The KBTCU's board took up the question at its meeting of 13 December 1932. The minutes record a long discussion, unusual in a body whose meetings were normally brisk, and two decisions. The union would accept warrants at face value from members as share deposits and as payments on loans. It would also buy warrants from members for cash at a discount of 3 percent, up to $40 per member per month.[^d6] A member holding a $40 warrant could thus exchange it at the credit union's office for $38.80 in cash, or apply the full $40 to her share account or her loan. The minutes do not record a vote count, but they do record the treasurer's reservation. Thornquist pointed out that every warrant the union accepted replaced cash with paper, and that the union's own obligations to members wishing to withdraw shares would have to be met in money.[^d7]

The decision was not obviously prudent. A bank would have had every reason to discount warrants heavily, if it accepted them at all. The board chose instead to stand between its members and the market in warrants, absorbing part of the risk of the city's default in exchange for a modest discount and the warrants' interest. Its reasoning combined self-interest with obligation. If members could not use their warrants to repay loans, delinquency would rise; if they sold warrants to brokers at steep discounts, they would have less to save. But the minutes also record a director's observation that the union "was organized by teachers for teachers, and if it will not take a teacher's pay it has no business calling itself a teachers' bank."[^d8] The phrase "teachers' bank," which recurs in the union's records, expressed an identification of the institution with the occupational community that the warrant episode put to a practical test.

### 3.3.2 Warrants and the credit committee

The purchase limit of $40 per member per month was itself an instrument of character. It was set low enough that the union could not be drained by a few large holders, and it applied equally to every member, which mattered in a building where everyone knew roughly what everyone else earned. Requests to exceed it went to the credit committee, chaired since 1931 by Frances Delacroix. The committee's minutes for the first half of 1933 record a series of such requests, almost all from members facing medical bills, rent arrears or the support of relatives. The committee granted some and refused others, and its reasons are revealing. Exceptions were made for members whose need was known and whose record of repayment was good; they were refused, in one case, to a member who had "not been regular in her payments since September" and, in another, without recorded reason to a member whose request the committee "did not feel able to support."[^d9]

The warrant episode also shows how much of the union's business depended on Thornquist's own labor. Warrants had to be checked, endorsed, recorded by serial number and held until redemption, and the treasurer kept a separate warrant ledger in her own hand for the whole of 1933.[^d10] *The Thrift Bell*, the members' newsletter, explained the arrangement in its January 1933 issue in terms that emphasized mutual obligation rather than financial calculation: members who did not need cash were urged to deposit their warrants rather than sell them, so that the union's limited cash could go to those who did.[^d11] The appeal assumed that members would sort themselves by need without compulsion, and the warrant ledger suggests that many did: a substantial share of warrants came in as deposits and loan payments rather than as sales for cash.

Ruth Ellison, whose mother was a member of the union, recalled the warrants ninety years later as part of the texture of her childhood:

> My mother called them "Haskins money," which wasn't fair to Mr. Haskins, he didn't make the city broke. She'd come home on a Friday and put the warrant on the kitchen table and say, well, that's the coal, and then she'd take it down to Miss Thornquist on Tuesday. (Ruth Ellison, b. 1924, member's daughter)[^d12]

Ellison was eight years old that winter, and her recollection is shaped by family retellings as well as memory, a problem of evidence discussed in Chapter 1. It nonetheless captures what the minutes do not: that the acceptance of warrants was experienced at home as a matter of keeping a household running, and was associated with a person rather than an institution. Superintendent Elmer Haskins, whose name the family attached to the warrants, appears in the union's records as a cautious ally. In January 1933 he confirmed in writing that the board of education would recognize the union's endorsement on warrants presented for redemption, a small courtesy that made the arrangement workable.[^d13]

## 3.4 The Failure of Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust

Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust suspended payments on 21 February 1933. The *Evening Courier* of the following day reported the closure on its front page, attributing it to "heavy withdrawals in recent weeks" and printing a statement from the bank's directors that depositors would be paid in full once assets could be realized.[^d14] Whether the bank was insolvent before the withdrawals began or was made insolvent by them cannot be said: that is the distinction Richardson shows to be difficult even with supervisory records, and in Kestrel Bay the records that might settle it have not survived.[^d15] What the union's papers do show is how the failure looked from the position of a small depositor with close ties to the bank's staff.

The KBTCU had kept its cash at Savings & Trust since 1929. When the bank closed, the union had $3,140 on deposit there, which was frozen with all other deposits. The sum was large in relation to the union's resources: it represented its working cash, the reserve fund the state statute required it to maintain, and the undivided earnings of the previous year, swollen in the early weeks of 1933 by loan repayments that had come in while the credit committee was approving fewer new loans. It was money the union could not lend and could not pay out to members, at precisely the moment when members most needed it.

Correspondence in box 8 suggests that the treasurer was not taken entirely by surprise. In late January 1933 Thornquist wrote to Harold Lindqvist, the bank's cashier, asking whether the union might withdraw part of its balance to meet expected share withdrawals. Lindqvist's reply, dated 2 February, is courteous and evasive. It assured her that the bank was "in a sound position" and asked, as a personal favor, that the union not reduce its balance "at a time when every account counts."[^d16] Thornquist withdrew $300 on 7 February and left the rest. The decision is easy to criticize with hindsight, but it is intelligible as a judgment of character of the kind the union applied to its own borrowers: Lindqvist had handled the union's account since 1929 and belonged to the community whose confidence the bank depended upon. An institution that lent on personal knowledge also, it appears, deposited on it, and here personal knowledge failed.

The failure reached the union in two ways. Directly, it removed the union's cash. Indirectly, it alarmed members. The daybook for 21 and 24 February records withdrawal requests far above the usual level, and the treasurer met them only partially, from the cash in the office safe and the proceeds of warrants redeemed that week.[^d17] There was no run on the union in the sense that the bank had experienced one, but there was the beginning of one, and the board met in emergency session on 27 February to discuss whether to suspend withdrawals altogether. It decided not to, on the grounds that a suspension would "tell the members we are no better than the bank."[^d18] Instead it asked members, through a special issue of *The Thrift Bell*, to limit their withdrawals voluntarily to what they needed for the month, and it authorized the treasurer to borrow against the union's warrant holdings if necessary.[^d19]

The union's officers drew the comparison with the bank themselves. Both faced depositors who wanted their money and assets that could not quickly be turned into cash. The bank's depositors had no obligation to one another and every reason to withdraw first; the union's members knew that a withdrawal by one reduced the cash available to the others, and would see the treasurer in the corridors the next morning. The difference was not one of capital, since so much of the union's liquid reserve was frozen. It lay in the relationship between those who held claims on the institution and those who managed it.

## 3.5 The Bank Holiday and the Withdrawal Limit

Within two weeks the local crisis had become a national one. On 6 March 1933 the new administration proclaimed a national bank holiday, closing every bank in the country while the condition of each was examined, and the emergency legislation that followed allowed sound banks to reopen in stages.[^d20] In Kestrel Bay, where the largest bank had already closed, the holiday changed little in practice.{{kennedy1973}} It did not apply to credit unions, and the KBTCU could legally have continued to pay withdrawals in full. Its board chose not to. At a meeting on the evening of 6 March it resolved that, from the following day until further notice, share withdrawals would be limited to $25 per member per month, with exceptions for illness or funerals to be granted by vote of the credit committee.[^d21] The limit took effect on Tuesday, 7 March, and remained in force until 30 June 1933. Its announcement in *The Thrift Bell* justified it as a measure of fairness rather than of necessity: the union, Thornquist wrote, would "treat every member alike, and no member will be paid at the expense of another."[^d22]

The withdrawal limit is the clearest instance in the union's records of a rule that worked because members consented to it. The board had no means of enforcing it other than the treasurer's refusal to pay, and a member determined to recover her savings could, in principle, have sought legal redress. None appears to have done so. The credit committee's minutes record exception requests at a steady rate through the spring, most of them for medical bills, and two for funerals; the committee granted the funeral requests without discussion and most of the medical ones after hearing from the member or, in several cases, from a colleague who vouched for her.[^d23] One request was refused because the committee judged that the member's need could be met by a loan, which suggests that the committee distinguished between withdrawals that would reduce the union's cash and loans that would at least be repaid. Ellison recalled her mother's account of the period as one of restraint rather than fear:

> She said nobody took more than they had to. You'd have been ashamed to, with everybody knowing. (Ruth Ellison, b. 1924, member's daughter)[^d24]

The recollection is too neat to take at face value, and the daybook shows that some members did press for more. But withdrawals under the limit were well below what the board had feared, and share balances steadied by May.[^d25] Members' knowledge of one another made a rule that was not legally binding into one that was largely observed.

The Banking Act of 1933, signed in June, created federal deposit insurance and tightened federal regulation of banks.{{bankingact1933}} Its passage coincided with the end of the withdrawal limit, but the board lifted the limit on its own reading of the union's cash: the act insured bank deposits, not credit-union shares. Calomiris and White's account of deposit insurance as a victory for small unit banks helps explain why the reform offered nothing to cooperatives like the KBTCU, which had no voice in the politics that produced it.{{calomiris1994}} The union came out of the crisis without any federal guarantee, a fact that would weigh on its officers when the question of a federal charter arose (Chapter 4).

## 3.6 Delinquency, Forbearance and Character

The crisis tested the credit committee's methods most severely in the treatment of members who could not pay. Delinquency, measured as the share of loans more than two months in arrears, rose from 4.3 percent at the end of 1931 to 7.6 percent in 1932 and 9.4 percent in 1933 (Table 3.1). The by-laws allowed the committee to demand full repayment, pursue co-makers or go to law; the minutes show that it almost never did. It extended terms, accepted reduced payments, took warrants, and sometimes simply waited.[^d26]

Forbearance was not indiscriminate. The committee's minutes record its reasons, and they consistently distinguish between borrowers who could not pay and those who, in the committee's view, would not. A teacher whose husband had lost his job at the paper mill was granted a six-month suspension of principal payments; a member whose arrears the committee attributed to "other obligations she has chosen to put first" was asked to bring her account up to date within the month.[^d27] The committee drew on information no bank could have collected: who was ill, whose family was out of work, who had been seen buying furniture while behind on her payments. Delacroix appears in the minutes as firm and well informed, repeatedly bringing to the table facts about a borrower's household that could only have come from the staff room or the corridor.

This is the practical meaning of lending on "character" in the union's records, and it was not simply benevolent. It gave the committee a power over members' private lives that a commercial lender lacked and that members could not easily resist, since the colleagues who granted forbearance could withdraw it. The minutes record decisions and brief reasons but not the conversations behind them, nor the views of members who were refused; a member who felt unfairly judged had little opportunity to say so in any document the union kept.[^d28] The moral economy of credit protected members who conformed to its expectations of thrift, regularity and candor, and pressed hardest on those who did not.

The losses eventually written off suggest that the committee's judgments were, on the whole, sound. The $812 charged off between 1932 and 1934 was a substantial sum for an institution of this size but small in relation to the loans outstanding, and delinquency fell to 5.8 percent by the end of 1934 as warrants were redeemed. Forbearance mostly bought time for borrowers who were eventually able to pay. Whether a stricter policy would have lowered losses cannot be known; it would certainly have alienated members at a time when the union depended on their goodwill.

## 3.7 Holding On

The union survived the years 1931–1933 not because it was stronger than Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust but because it was a different kind of institution. Its capital was small, its cash was frozen in a failed bank, and its members' pay had been partly converted into municipal paper. What it had that the bank lacked was a set of relationships that allowed it to ask for restraint and to receive it. The acceptance of warrants kept members' accounts functioning when their pay did not; the withdrawal limit spread the burden of illiquidity evenly; and the credit committee's forbearance kept most delinquent borrowers within the union. Each practice depended on members' knowledge of one another and on the school building as the place where that knowledge was produced and enforced.

The national literature locates this story without fully explaining it. The union's experience confirms the importance of the particular bank that Wicker and White emphasize, shows Richardson's liquidity problem from the side of a depositor, and gives concrete form to Bernanke's argument by showing an institution that maintained credit relationships when the city's bank could not. It also shows the limits of that achievement. The union's deposit stayed frozen for years, and only 62 percent of it would ever be recovered; it paid no dividend for 1933 and ended the year with fewer members than it had had two years earlier.

By the end of 1934 the union was growing again, but it was growing in a changed legal and political environment. The Federal Credit Union Act of 1934 offered credit unions a federal charter and a federal identity, and the union's officers had to decide whether the practices that had carried them through the crisis would survive the forms and examinations that came with it. Chapter 4 follows that decision.

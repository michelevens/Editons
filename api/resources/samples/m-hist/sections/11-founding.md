# Chapter 2: Founding a Teachers' Bank, 1928–1930

The Kestrel Bay Teachers' Credit Union was organized on the evening of Tuesday, 9 October 1928, in the library of Lakeside High School, by eleven teachers who between them subscribed $55 in shares. This chapter reconstructs how they came to do so and what they built in the two years that followed, before the Depression reached the city's schools. It addresses the first of the thesis's research questions, who joined and borrowed and for what, and lays the groundwork for the fourth, by showing how the union's credit committee assembled a working definition of "character" out of the ordinary materials of school life. The argument is that the practices which would carry the union through the crisis of 1931–1933 were not improvised under pressure. They were established in these first two years, in a period of modest prosperity, by people who had absorbed the credit union movement's ideals and adapted them to the circumstances of a single school system.

## 2.1 Teachers' money in Kestrel Bay

To understand why a group of teachers would found a bank, it is necessary first to understand the peculiar shape of a teacher's finances in a city like Kestrel Bay. The board of education paid its teachers in ten monthly installments, from September to June.[^d1] A teacher who did not save through the spring therefore faced a summer of ten or eleven weeks with no income at all, and the arithmetic of a salary that barely covered board, clothing and the obligations of family made saving difficult. Many teachers, moreover, were required or encouraged to take summer courses to renew or upgrade their certificates. The board's salary schedule rewarded additional credits, but the courses had to be paid for, in tuition, travel and lodging, at exactly the moment when pay stopped.

Medical and dental bills formed a second pressure, since a teacher lost pay after a short allowance of sick days. Women teachers, who made up the large majority of the city's staff, were in most cases unmarried, since the board did not then employ married women except as substitutes, and a good number of them contributed to the support of parents or younger siblings.[^d2] Men teachers, fewer in number, were more often married and supporting households on a single salary.

Where did teachers in such straits turn? Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust and the city's other banks made personal loans only rarely, and then to borrowers who could offer collateral or a well-to-do endorser. The alternatives were less respectable. The *Evening Courier* carried regular advertisements for two licensed small-loan companies on Front Street, and the city also had its share of the unlicensed "salary buyers" who advanced cash against a borrower's next paycheck at rates that contemporary investigators found extortionate.{{robinson1935}} The campaign for small-loan regulation, which sought to license such lenders and cap their charges, was in this period spreading from state to state, and by the end of the 1920s it had made the licensed small-loan company a familiar feature of urban life.{{carruthers2012}} For teachers, however, borrowing from either kind of lender carried a further risk. Salary buyers often secured their advances by an assignment of wages, and an assignment filed with the board of education's paymaster meant that the teacher's employer, and quite possibly her principal, would learn of her debts.[^d3]

This point matters for the argument of the thesis. The founders would describe their purpose in the movement's language of thrift and rescue from the loan shark. But for their first members one of a teachers' credit union's chief attractions was discretion: a loan from colleagues was known to the credit committee and the treasurer, not to the paymaster or the principal. Lending on character in Kestrel Bay was thus from the outset a matter of both knowledge and confidentiality.

## 2.2 The idea arrives

The surviving records do not allow the origin of the union to be dated precisely, but the correspondence files suggest that the idea came to Kestrel Bay through Walter Pruitt, who taught manual training at Lakeside High School. In the spring of 1928 Pruitt wrote to the Credit Union National Extension Bureau in Boston, asking for information on how a group of teachers might organize under the state's credit union law. The bureau replied with a package of pamphlets, a model set of bylaws and a form letter offering the assistance of a field organizer.[^d4] Pruitt's letter has not survived, but the bureau's reply refers to his mention of a teachers' credit union in another city that he had read about in a professional journal.

The literature Pruitt received was the product of a deliberate national campaign. Under Roy Bergengren's direction, and with Edward Filene's money, the bureau had spent the 1920s lobbying state legislatures for enabling statutes and organizing credit unions among groups of employees.{{moody1971}} Its pamphlets presented the credit union as a school of thrift and a weapon against usury, in which wage earners would pool their savings and lend them to one another for provident purposes under their own democratic management.{{bergengren1952}} They drew a sharp line between provident borrowing, for illness, education or the consolidation of expensive debts, and borrowing to buy what a member could not afford. The pamphlets in the KBTCU's files are marked in pencil in places, apparently by Pruitt, and the marked passages concern the dangers of installment buying and the role of the credit committee in judging the "character" of applicants.[^d5]

Pruitt took the material to Agnes Thornquist, who taught mathematics in the classroom next to his shop. Thornquist, then in her late thirties, had taught at Lakeside since before the war and was known in the building for keeping the accounts of the teachers' social fund. In a short memoir she contributed to the union's twenty-fifth-anniversary booklet in 1953, she recalled that her first reaction had been doubt about whether teachers could be trusted to run a bank, and her second, after reading the bureau's sample balance sheets, that the bookkeeping was well within the competence of anyone who taught algebra.[^d6] The memoir is retrospective and celebratory, but its emphasis on the ordinariness of the work rings true: the union was organized by teachers who believed that keeping accounts was a skill they already possessed.

Over the summer and early autumn of 1928 Pruitt and Thornquist recruited nine colleagues. The pattern of recruitment is visible in Table 2.1. Seven of the eleven founders taught at Lakeside High, where Pruitt and Thornquist worked; the remaining four came from two elementary schools, Garfield and Harbor Street, and appear to have been recruited through personal connections. Ida Marchetti of Garfield Elementary had been Thornquist's student at Lakeside, and Thomas Reardon, principal of Harbor Street School, had taught with Pruitt in the early 1920s.[^d7]

Two features of the founding group deserve comment. First, it was weighted toward the high school and toward men in a way the membership would not be: all three men held office, two on the credit committee. Second, it included two people whose positions gave them particular knowledge of other teachers. Grace Whitcomb, the librarian, saw almost every Lakeside teacher in a week, and it was through her that the union came to occupy a room off the library. Thomas Reardon, as a principal, knew his teachers' circumstances intimately, which both strengthened and, as section 2.4 shows, complicated his work on the credit committee.

## 2.3 Organization and the room off the library

The organization meeting of 9 October 1928 adopted bylaws closely modeled on those the bureau had supplied. Shares were set at $5 each, payable in installments of as little as 25 cents a week, and each member paid an entrance fee of 25 cents. Membership was open to teachers employed by the Kestrel Bay board of education, a common bond that would remain unchanged until 1936. Loans were to be made only to members, for provident or productive purposes, at an interest rate of 1 percent per month on unpaid balances, the ceiling permitted by the state's credit union statute. Loans above $50 required a co-maker, also a member; loans above the borrower's shareholding required the approval of the full credit committee rather than of a single member of it.[^d8] The supervisory committee was to audit the books each quarter.

The founders applied for a state charter that month and received it before the end of the year.[^d9] Where the union would receive its members was settled by Superintendent Elmer Haskins, whose role in the union's history was that of a cautious ally. Haskins had no objection to the teachers' enterprise, and he permitted the union to use a small room off the Lakeside High School library, formerly a storeroom for periodicals, on condition that its business be conducted outside school hours. He declined, however, the founders' request that the board of education deduct share payments and loan installments from teachers' salaries. His letter of refusal explained that the board could not become a collecting agent for a private society, and that it would not wish to be seen to favor one financial institution over others in the city.[^d10]

The refusal shaped the union's practice unexpectedly. Without payroll deduction, every share payment and installment had to be brought to the office in person. Those hours were fixed in November 1928 as Tuesdays and Fridays from 3:30 to 5:30 in the afternoon, after classes had ended.[^d11] The union thus became a place as well as an institution: a room teachers visited twice a week, where they saw the treasurer and one another, and where their regularity, or its absence, was visible. Ruth Ellison, who accompanied her family to the window as a child in the early 1930s, remembered the room chiefly as quiet, with a line of teachers waiting along the library wall and Thornquist at a small table with the ledger and the cash box.

> You went in and you didn't talk. It was like church. Miss Thornquist would look up and say your name, she knew everybody's name, and you'd hand over the envelope. (Ruth Ellison, b. 1924, recalling the early 1930s)[^d12]

A child's sense of solemnity may owe more to the library than to the union, but the memory captures what the minutes confirm: payment at the window was a public act, performed before colleagues, and the treasurer knew who had come and who had not.

Between office days the union's cash went into an account at Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust, where most of the founders already banked; the consequences of that natural choice are the subject of Chapter 3. The bank's cashier, Harold Lindqvist, seems to have regarded the new depositor with polite amusement. His letter acknowledging the opening of the account wished the "teachers' bank" every success and offered, should its officers ever require it, the bank's advice on matters of credit.[^d13]
## 2.4 Learning to lend: the credit committee and character

The credit committee met on the evenings of office days, in the same room, to consider applications. Its first members were Leona Babcock, Raymond Kowalczyk and Thomas Reardon. The bylaws required it to judge each applicant's character, the providence of the purpose and the capacity to repay from salary. What the committee's minutes reveal is how the members translated those tests into decisions.

The application form, printed in November 1928, asked for the amount requested, the purpose, the proposed schedule of repayment, the applicant's salary and existing debts, and the name of a co-maker where one was required.[^d14] It did not ask for references. The committee did not need them. In the union's first two years, every applicant was a teacher in the city's schools, and in almost every case at least one member of the committee knew the applicant personally or could find out about her by the next office day. The minutes record this informal inquiry obliquely. A typical entry from the spring of 1929 notes that an application was "held over for information" and approved at the following meeting; another records that a committee member "spoke for" an applicant whom she knew from church.[^d15]

What did the committee want to know? Reading the minutes and the loan register together, three kinds of knowledge appear to have counted as evidence of character. The first was reliability at work: whether a teacher was regular in attendance, kept her post, and had the confidence of her principal. The second was conduct in money matters, especially whether she was known to owe money to merchants or to small-loan companies and whether she paid her share installments regularly at the union's window. The third, and the most delicate, was what the minutes call "circumstances": the state of a teacher's family, the health of her parents, the obligations she bore. Circumstances could argue either way: a mother's operation made a strong case; a brother rumored to drink made a weak one, because the money might not stay where it was lent.

The presence of a principal on the committee complicated this system. Thomas Reardon knew more about the teachers in his own building than anyone else could, and his knowledge was useful. But it was also the kind of knowledge the union's discretion was meant to shield from employers. In June 1929 an application from a Harbor Street teacher was withdrawn after the applicant learned that her principal would consider it; the minutes record only that the applicant "preferred not to proceed."[^d16] At the annual meeting in January 1930 the members amended the bylaws to provide that no member of the credit committee should vote on an application from a teacher whom he or she supervised.[^d17] Reardon left the committee at the end of 1930 and no principal replaced him. The episode shows members grasping that lending on character required a particular kind of knowledge: that of peers, not superiors.

The committee's understanding of provident purposes was, at first, strict. In its first year it approved loans for medical and dental bills, for the repayment of debts to small-loan companies and merchants, for funerals and family emergencies, for fuel and repairs to houses, and for summer study. It refused, or discouraged, loans for consumer goods. In April 1929 it declined an application for $90 to buy a radio set, noting that the applicant "should save for this," and in the same month it approved a smaller loan to the same member to pay a dentist.[^d18] A loan to buy a used automobile was approved in the autumn of 1929 only after the applicant, a Garfield teacher, explained that she needed it to visit an invalid father in a nearby town, and the minutes take care to record that reason.[^d19] The same automobile, in other words, could be provident or improvident depending on what the committee knew of the borrower's life.

At 1 percent a month on unpaid balances, a $60 loan repaid in ten equal monthly installments cost the borrower $3.30 in interest, far less than a salary buyer would demand. But price was not the union's only attraction. It offered discretion, and a schedule tied to the school calendar: summer loans for courses and medical bills began repayment in October, after the first paycheck of the new year. The committee thus built the rhythm of the teacher's year into its loans, an adaptation that would matter when the warrants of 1932 disrupted that rhythm.

## 2.5 Growth, 1929–1930

The union grew quickly in its first two full years. From 46 members at the end of 1928, it reached 112 by the end of 1929 and 168 by the end of 1930. Members' shares grew from $610 to $4,850 and then to $9,120. The credit committee approved 61 loans in 1929 and 118 in 1930, and by the end of 1930 loans outstanding stood at $8,040.[^d20] Delinquency was low: loans more than two months in arrears amounted to 1.6 percent of loans at the end of 1929 and 2.5 percent at the end of 1930. The board declared a dividend on shares of 6 percent for each of these years, a return higher than the savings banks were paying and one the treasurer's report for 1930 announced with evident pride.[^d21]

New members came disproportionately from the elementary schools, where Marchetti, Dunmore and Ferris recruited in their buildings. Among the 1929 recruits was Frances Delacroix, who would chair the credit committee from 1931 to 1938. By the end of 1930 women made up 71 percent of the membership, a proportion that more closely reflected the composition of the teaching force than the founding group had.

The purposes for which members borrowed in these years can be reconstructed from the loan register. The distribution for 1929–1932 is given with the later periods in Table 5.1 in Chapter 5, and only its main lines need be noted here. Medical and dental bills were the leading purpose, accounting for 27 percent of loans made in 1929–1932; the repayment of other debts, including those owed to small-loan companies, accounted for 22 percent; family support and funerals for 14 percent; home repairs and fuel for 13 percent; and education and summer courses for 11 percent. Automobiles accounted for only 6 percent and household appliances and furniture for 4 percent. The mean loan in the period was $64. These figures confirm the impression the minutes give. In its first years the union lent overwhelmingly for the purposes its founders and the national movement regarded as provident, and it lent in small sums.

The debt-repayment loans deserve notice. The register often names the creditor as one of the Front Street small-loan companies, so the union was doing what the movement promised, drawing members away from dearer lenders. The same loans reveal how many teachers had borrowed from those lenders before the union existed. The earlier silence on this point, noted in Chapter 1, was a product of discretion: teachers who had hidden their debts from colleagues now disclosed them to a committee they trusted not to tell.

Growth required adjustments. By the autumn of 1930 the office sessions had become crowded, and the board appointed Edith Sorensen assistant treasurer to receive payments while Thornquist kept the ledger.[^d22] In January 1931 the supervisory committee recommended that the treasurer be bonded and that loan applications be filed in a cabinet rather than in Thornquist's desk drawer.[^d23] These were the first, modest signs of the formalization that the federal charter would later accelerate.

Whether the KBTCU's growth or its low delinquency was typical of credit unions elsewhere cannot be established from the sources used here. What can be said is that in its first two years the union met every test its founders had set for it.

## 2.6 Conclusion

By the end of 1930 the union had 168 members, more than $9,000 in shares and some $8,000 out on loan, and it was still run by volunteers after school in a room off a library. Its practices had been shaped by the national movement's ideals, by the peculiar shape of teachers' finances, and by the dense social world of the school system, which gave the credit committee the knowledge it needed to lend on character and gave members reason to trust it with their secrets.

That combination had not yet been tested. The union's first two years were years of rising membership and steady salaries, when a member who fell behind could usually be expected to catch up at the next paycheck. Chapter 3 follows the union into the years when that expectation failed: when the board of education paid teachers in warrants, when Kestrel Bay Savings & Trust closed its doors with the union's funds inside, and when the credit committee's knowledge of its members became not a convenience but the condition of the institution's survival.
